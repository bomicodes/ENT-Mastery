
## NOE Fracture ORIF  (slug: noe-orif, region: Facial Plastics / Trauma)

A-noe-orif.summary: A naso-orbito-ethmoid fracture drives the central midface back and apart, so the medial canthal tendon drifts laterally with its bone fragment and the nasal dorsum loses projection. The repair turns on the central fragment that carries the medial canthal tendon: fix it, or reattach the tendon posterior and superior to the posterior lacrimal crest, while protecting the lacrimal system, skull base and orbit.
A-noe-orif.layer1: Coronal approach — scalp skin and subcutaneous tissue; galea; subgaleal loose areolar plane; pericranium; temporoparietal fascia with the frontal branch laterally. Incise behind the hairline from ear to ear. Elevate subgaleally down to about 2-3 cm above the supraorbital rims, then incise pericranium and continue subperiosteally. Laterally, incise the superficial layer of the deep temporal fascia where it splits, and stay deep to it down to the arch to protect the frontal branch.
A-noe-orif.layer2: Supraorbital rim and nasal root — supraorbital neurovascular bundle (notch or foramen); supratrochlear bundle; nasofrontal suture; nasal bones. Free the supraorbital nerve from a notch with periosteal elevation, or from a foramen by removing a small wedge of bone with an osteotome. Continue subperiosteally down the nasal bones.
A-noe-orif.layer3: Medial orbit — frontal process of the maxilla; anterior lacrimal crest; lacrimal sac in the lacrimal fossa; posterior lacrimal crest; lamina papyracea; frontoethmoidal suture with anterior and posterior ethmoidal foramina. Elevate periorbita off the medial wall behind the lacrimal sac. The anterior ethmoidal artery is at the frontoethmoidal suture, about 24 mm behind the anterior lacrimal crest, then the posterior ethmoidal about 12 mm further back, and the optic canal about 6 mm beyond that (the 24-12-6 rule; distances vary). The suture line marks the level of the anterior skull base.
A-noe-orif.layer4: Lower lid and sublabial access — transconjunctival approach to the infraorbital rim and floor; sublabial approach to the piriform aperture; medial buttress (nasomaxillary). These expose the inferior parts of the central fragment and the medial and inferior orbital walls. Existing lacerations can be used when they are well placed.
A-noe-orif.layer5: Central fragment and medial canthal tendon — central bone fragment; medial canthal tendon (anterior limb on the frontal process of the maxilla, posterior limb to the posterior lacrimal crest). Do not strip the tendon from its fragment. Classify the injury (Markowitz-Manson): I = single large fragment with tendon attached; II = comminuted but tendon attached to a fixable fragment; III = tendon avulsed or attached to a tiny fragment.
A-noe-orif.layer6: Reduction and fixation — frontal bar / nasofrontal region; infraorbital rim; piriform aperture and medial buttress; transnasal wire or canthopexy device. Reduce to stable bone above (frontal) and below (maxilla). For types II-III, transnasal wiring pulls the central fragments or tendon posteriorly and superiorly, aimed at a point posterior and superior to the posterior lacrimal crest. A canthus pulled only medially, not back, still looks wide.
A-noe-orif.layer7: Orbital walls, nose and frontal sinus — medial wall and floor defects; nasal dorsum (cantilever bone graft if needed); frontal sinus outflow tract. Reconstruct the medial wall and floor after the canthal relationships are set. Restore nasal projection, often with a cantilever graft. Assess frontal sinus outflow injury, which NOE fractures commonly cause.
A-noe-orif.layer8: Closure and soft-tissue redraping — pericranium; temporal fascia; medial canthal soft tissue bolsters; scalp. Resuspend the temporal fat pad and soft tissues. Light external bolsters over the medial canthal region help the skin adhere to the new bony contour.
A-noe-orif.landmark1: Anterior and posterior lacrimal crests — Follow subperiosteally from the nasal sidewall onto the frontal process of the maxilla; the anterior crest is the raised edge in front of the lacrimal sac fossa, the posterior crest is the ridge behind it (Define the canthal tendon insertion and the target for canthopexy (posterior and superior to the posterior crest))
A-noe-orif.landmark2: Frontoethmoidal suture — Along the medial orbital wall at the junction of lamina papyracea (below) and frontal orbital plate (above); the ethmoidal foramina lie along it (Marks the level of the anterior skull base and the ethmoidal arteries)
A-noe-orif.landmark3: Anterior ethmoidal artery — At the frontoethmoidal suture about 24 mm posterior to the anterior lacrimal crest (variable range) (Bleeding source and a limit for medial wall dissection)
A-noe-orif.landmark4: Supraorbital neurovascular bundle — On the superior rim in about the midpupillary line; in a coronal flap it tethers the flap until released from its notch or foramen (Releasing it allows the flap to come down to the nasal root without nerve injury)
A-noe-orif.landmark5: Medial canthal tendon fragment — Grasp the medial canthus through the skin and pull laterally (bowstring test), and feel for movement of the underlying bone (Tells you whether the tendon is attached to a fixable fragment)
A-noe-orif.landmark6: Lacrimal sac and canaliculi — Sac sits in the fossa between the crests; canaliculi enter it from the lid puncta medially and can be probed (Protect the sac; repair canalicular lacerations over a stent)
A-noe-orif.danger1: Medial canthal tendon — where: Attaches to the central fragment around the lacrimal fossa; protect: Keep it attached to bone; when transnasal canthopexy is needed, aim posterior and superior to the posterior lacrimal crest; injury: Telecanthus, rounded canthus, lid malposition
A-noe-orif.danger2: Lacrimal sac and nasolacrimal duct — where: Lacrimal fossa and nasolacrimal canal in the medial orbit and lateral nasal wall, emptying into the inferior meatus; protect: Elevate behind the sac; probe and stent injured canaliculi; avoid screws in the duct path; injury: Epiphora, dacryocystitis
A-noe-orif.danger3: Anterior skull base and dura — where: Cribriform plate and fovea ethmoidalis above the frontoethmoidal suture; posterior table of the frontal sinus; protect: Assess CT for skull base fracture; avoid instrument passage above the suture line; repair dural tears; injury: CSF leak, meningitis, anosmia
A-noe-orif.danger4: Optic nerve — where: Optic canal at the orbital apex, about 6 mm or so behind the posterior ethmoidal foramen; protect: Stop medial wall dissection before the posterior ethmoidal artery; check vision; injury: Blindness
A-noe-orif.danger5: Frontal branch of the facial nerve — where: In or on the deep surface of the temporoparietal fascia over the zygomatic arch and temple; protect: In a coronal approach, stay deep to the superficial layer of the deep temporal fascia and subperiosteal on the arch; injury: Brow ptosis, loss of forehead movement
A-noe-orif.danger6: Frontal sinus outflow tract — where: Posteromedial floor of the frontal sinus draining into the frontal recess; protect: Evaluate for outflow tract injury on CT and at surgery; plan obliteration, cranialization or endoscopic follow-up when injured; injury: Mucocele, frontal sinusitis
A-noe-orif.variant1: The ethmoidal artery distances from the lacrimal crest vary; some patients have a middle (accessory) ethmoidal artery
A-noe-orif.variant2: Supraorbital nerve may exit through a foramen rather than a notch and needs an osteotomy to release
A-noe-orif.variant3: Frontal sinus size varies from absent to very large, changing the risk to the outflow tract
A-noe-orif.variant4: Skull base slope (Keros depth) varies and changes where dura lies relative to the medial orbit
A-noe-orif.supply1: Arterial supply — Anterior and posterior ethmoidal, supraorbital, supratrochlear and dorsal nasal arteries (ophthalmic); angular artery (facial); infraorbital (maxillary)
A-noe-orif.supply2: Sensory — Supraorbital and supratrochlear (V1) for the forehead; infratrochlear and anterior ethmoidal for the nose; infraorbital (V2) below
A-noe-orif.supply3: Motor at risk — Frontal branch of CN VII (coronal approach)
A-noe-orif.pimp1: Q: What is the Markowitz-Manson classification? A: Type I: single large central fragment with the medial canthal tendon attached; type II: comminuted, tendon attached to a fixable fragment; type III: tendon avulsed or attached to a tiny fragment
A-noe-orif.pimp2: Q: In which direction should a transnasal canthopexy pull? A: Posteriorly and superiorly, to a point posterior and superior to the posterior lacrimal crest
A-noe-orif.pimp3: Q: What is the 24-12-6 rule? A: Approximate distances from the anterior lacrimal crest to the anterior ethmoidal foramen (24 mm), then to the posterior ethmoidal foramen (12 mm), then to the optic canal (6 mm)
A-noe-orif.pimp4: Q: What does the frontoethmoidal suture mark? A: The level of the anterior skull base along the medial orbital wall, with the ethmoidal foramina along it
A-noe-orif.pimp5: Q: Where does the nasolacrimal duct empty? A: Inferior meatus, under the inferior turbinate (valve of Hasner)
A-noe-orif.pimp6: Q: In a coronal approach, which layer protects the frontal branch? A: Dissection deep to the superficial layer of the deep temporal fascia (and subperiosteal on the arch); the nerve runs in the temporoparietal fascia above
A-noe-orif.pimp7: Q: What is the bowstring test? A: Lateral traction on the lower lid while palpating the medial canthus; laxity or bone movement suggests an unstable canthal tendon fragment
A-noe-orif.pimp8: Q: What are the two limbs of the medial canthal tendon and their insertions? A: Anterior limb to the frontal process of the maxilla at the anterior lacrimal crest; posterior limb to the posterior lacrimal crest
OR-noe-orif.step1: Define the NOE fracture pattern and medial canthal tendon attachment on thin-cut CT before exposure.
OR-noe-orif.step2: Expose the central midface through the required coronal/transconjunctival/intraoral or existing-wound approaches while protecting lacrimal structures.
OR-noe-orif.step3: Identify the central fragment carrying the medial canthal tendon and determine whether the tendon remains attached to bone.
OR-noe-orif.step4: Reduce the nasal/medial orbital buttresses in three dimensions using stable frontal/nasal/maxillary reference points.
OR-noe-orif.step5: Fix the central fragment rigidly; if the medial canthal tendon is avulsed or attached to a non-fixable fragment, perform transnasal canthopexy to a stable position.
OR-noe-orif.step6: Reconstruct associated orbital/nasal/frontal defects after central projection and intercanthal distance are restored.
OR-noe-orif.step7: Confirm symmetric canthal position, nasal projection, globe position, and lacrimal considerations.
OR-noe-orif.step8: Obtain hemostasis and close approaches with attention to soft-tissue redraping.

## Orbital Floor Repair  (slug: orbital-floor, region: Facial Plastics / Trauma)

A-orbital-floor.summary: Orbital floor repair restores the floor between the orbit and maxillary sinus to free entrapped tissue and correct enophthalmos. The relationships that decide safety are the infraorbital nerve in its groove and canal on the floor, the inferior rectus and inferior oblique close above the floor, and the posterior ledge and optic canal, which set how far back the implant can go.
A-orbital-floor.layer1: Lower eyelid approach — conjunctiva; lower lid retractors (capsulopalpebral fascia); orbital septum; orbicularis oculi; lateral canthal tendon (inferior crus, if cantholysis). Transconjunctival: preseptal route between septum and orbicularis, or retroseptal route through the fornix behind the septum (orbital fat seen). Subciliary: skin-muscle flap to the septum. Add lateral canthotomy and inferior cantholysis for wide exposure.
A-orbital-floor.layer2: Orbital rim and periosteum — arcus marginalis; periosteum of anterior maxilla; infraorbital rim. Incise periosteum a few mm below the rim on the anterior face of the maxilla, avoiding the septum attachment, and elevate over the rim.
A-orbital-floor.layer3: Subperiosteal floor dissection — periorbita; orbital floor (maxilla, zygoma laterally, palatine posteriorly); infraorbital groove and canal; inferior orbital fissure (lateral). Elevate periorbita off the floor medially and laterally around the defect; the infraorbital nerve runs anteroposteriorly in the groove/canal.
A-orbital-floor.layer4: Defect and herniated contents — herniated orbital fat; inferior rectus and its sheath; maxillary sinus mucosa. Reduce herniated tissue hand over hand from the sinus; enlarge a trapdoor gently if needed.
A-orbital-floor.layer5: Posterior ledge — posterior intact floor; medial floor-wall junction (inferomedial strut); orbital process of palatine bone. Identify stable bone around the entire defect, especially the posterior ledge where the floor rises; the floor slopes upward going back and medially.
A-orbital-floor.layer6: Implant placement — implant; posterior ledge; medial and lateral bony margins; inferior orbital fissure. Seat the implant on stable bone with no tissue trapped beneath; keep it well anterior to the optic canal.
A-orbital-floor.layer7: Confirmation and closure — globe position; forced duction; periosteum; conjunctiva/lid. Repeat forced ductions; resuspend the canthus if cantholysis was done; close periosteum when feasible.
A-orbital-floor.landmark1: Infraorbital nerve — Foramen on the anterior maxilla roughly 6-10 mm below the rim, near the line of the pupil; on the floor it runs in a groove that becomes a canal anteriorly (Avoid stretch and cautery; the defect usually lies medial to it)
A-orbital-floor.landmark2: Inferior orbital fissure — Posterolateral floor, a slit running posterolaterally between floor and lateral wall (Contents (zygomatic nerve, venous connections) can be cauterized and divided; a lateral landmark for orientation)
A-orbital-floor.landmark3: Posterior ledge — Follow the floor back until stable bone rises upward; behind the central and medial floor it is commonly about 25-30 mm from the rim (range up to about 37-39 mm) and much closer laterally (Implant must rest on it for support)
A-orbital-floor.landmark4: Inferior oblique origin — Anteromedial floor, just lateral to the lacrimal fossa, behind the rim (Avoid detaching it during medial exposure)
A-orbital-floor.landmark5: Forced duction — Grasp conjunctiva and inferior rectus insertion near the inferior limbus (insertion about 6.5 mm from the limbus) and rotate the globe up (Confirms release of entrapment)
A-orbital-floor.danger1: Optic nerve — where: Optic canal at the orbital apex, posterior and superomedial to the end of the floor; about 40-45 mm from the infraorbital rim (range about 37-49 mm); protect: Do not dissect beyond the posterior ledge; avoid long implants pressed backward; injury: Blindness
A-orbital-floor.danger2: Globe — where: Above the dissection; protect: Corneal shield; malleable retractor with gentle intermittent pressure; injury: Corneal abrasion, globe injury
A-orbital-floor.danger3: Inferior rectus and inferior oblique — where: Inferior rectus lies directly above the floor; IO arises anteromedially; protect: Free entrapped muscle gently; avoid trapping it under the implant; injury: Persistent diplopia
A-orbital-floor.danger4: Infraorbital nerve — where: Groove and canal on the floor; protect: Avoid compression by implant edges and retraction; injury: Cheek, upper lip, lateral nose and upper teeth numbness
A-orbital-floor.danger5: Retrobulbar space — where: Behind the globe; protect: Hemostasis; check vision and pupils immediately after; injury: Retrobulbar hematoma with vision loss; treat with lateral canthotomy and cantholysis
A-orbital-floor.danger6: Lower lid structures — where: Approach layers; protect: Choose plane carefully; canthal resuspension; injury: Ectropion (subciliary) or entropion and lid malposition
A-orbital-floor.variant1: Pediatric trapdoor fracture with muscle entrapment and a white eye (minimal external signs); oculocardiac reflex with bradycardia
A-orbital-floor.variant2: Combined medial wall fracture with loss of the inferomedial strut
A-orbital-floor.variant3: Infraorbital nerve dehiscent in the floor or running in an open groove
A-orbital-floor.variant4: Enlarged maxillary sinus or low orbital floor variations
A-orbital-floor.supply1: Arterial — Infraorbital artery (branch of the third part of the maxillary artery) with the nerve; ophthalmic artery branches in the orbit
A-orbital-floor.supply2: Venous — Inferior ophthalmic vein connecting to the pterygoid plexus through the inferior orbital fissure
A-orbital-floor.supply3: Sensory — Infraorbital nerve (V2): lower lid, cheek, lateral nose, upper lip, and anterior/middle superior alveolar branches to upper teeth
A-orbital-floor.supply4: Motor — Oculomotor (inferior division) to inferior rectus and inferior oblique
A-orbital-floor.pimp1: Q: Which bones form the orbital floor? A: Maxilla (most), zygoma (lateral) and the orbital process of the palatine (posterior)
A-orbital-floor.pimp2: Q: Where is the orbital floor thinnest? A: Medial to the infraorbital groove
A-orbital-floor.pimp3: Q: What is the oculocardiac reflex? A: Bradycardia, nausea or syncope from traction on extraocular muscles; afferent V1 (trigeminal), efferent vagus
A-orbital-floor.pimp4: Q: What is the spiral of Tillaux? A: Rectus insertions from the limbus: medial about 5.5 mm, inferior 6.5, lateral 6.9, superior 7.7
A-orbital-floor.pimp5: Q: What is the 24-12-6 rule? A: From the anterior lacrimal crest along the medial wall: about 24 mm to the anterior ethmoidal foramen, 12 mm more to the posterior ethmoidal foramen, 6 mm more to the optic canal
A-orbital-floor.pimp6: Q: What nerve innervates the inferior oblique? A: Inferior division of the oculomotor nerve
A-orbital-floor.pimp7: Q: What is the emergency treatment for retrobulbar hematoma? A: Lateral canthotomy with inferior cantholysis
A-orbital-floor.pimp8: Q: What passes through the inferior orbital fissure? A: Infraorbital and zygomatic nerves (V2), infraorbital artery, branches of the inferior ophthalmic vein to the pterygoid plexus
OR-orbital-floor.step1: Perform and document preoperative visual acuity, pupils, color vision, EOM, globe position, and CT-defined defect/entrapment before incision.
OR-orbital-floor.step2: Use the planned transconjunctival/subciliary approach and dissect to the orbital rim in the correct plane while protecting the globe and infraorbital nerve.
OR-orbital-floor.step3: Elevate periorbita from the orbital floor and identify intact posterior/lateral bony ledges around the defect.
OR-orbital-floor.step4: Reduce herniated orbital tissue completely from the maxillary sinus, freeing entrapped muscle/periorbita without aggressive traction.
OR-orbital-floor.step5: Measure the defect and shape the implant to rest on stable bony margins without impinging the optic canal or extraocular muscles.
OR-orbital-floor.step6: Place the implant under direct visualization and verify that no soft tissue is trapped beneath or around it.
OR-orbital-floor.step7: Repeat forced ductions and inspect globe position; revise the implant if restriction persists.
OR-orbital-floor.step8: Close the approach and immediately repeat vision, pupils, and EOM assessment; new proptosis/vision loss is an emergency.

## Otoplasty  (slug: otoplasty, region: Facial Plastics / Trauma)

A-otoplasty.summary: Prominent ears usually result from an underdeveloped antihelical fold, excess conchal depth or a protruding lobule, alone or together. The operation works on the cartilage through a posterior approach; safety depends on not injuring the tightly adherent anterior skin and on setting the concha back posteriorly so the ear canal is not narrowed.
A-otoplasty.layer1: Marking and posterior incision — planned antihelical fold (marked by folding the helix back); postauricular sulcus; posterior auricular skin (thicker, mobile). Place the incision on the posterior auricle a little lateral to the sulcus, or excise an ellipse of skin there, so the final scar sits in the sulcus. Do not excise much skin; skin excision alone does not correct the deformity.
A-otoplasty.layer2: Posterior subcutaneous plane and soft tissue — posterior auricular muscle and ligament; posterior auricular artery branches; postauricular soft tissue behind the concha. Elevate on the posterior perichondrium toward the helical rim to expose the scapha and back of the antihelix. Excise the postauricular muscle and soft tissue behind the concha when conchal setback is planned so the concha can sit close to the mastoid.
A-otoplasty.layer3: Anterior cartilage surface (if scoring) — anterior perichondrium; anterior skin (thin, adherent, almost no subcutaneous fat); antihelical cartilage. If the cartilage is stiff, weaken the anterior surface of the planned antihelix by scoring or rasping through a small tunnel. Anterior skin is attached tightly to the perichondrium, so keep tunnels in the correct plane and avoid buttonholes.
A-otoplasty.layer4: Antihelical fold creation (Mustardé sutures) — scapha; concha (inner limb); posterior auricular cartilage and anterior perichondrium. Place several horizontal mattress sutures of permanent material through the cartilage and anterior perichondrium but not the anterior skin, spanning the planned fold. Tighten gradually so the antihelix is a smooth curve, not a sharp ridge, and the superior crus is defined.
A-otoplasty.layer5: Conchal setback — posterior conchal cartilage; mastoid periosteum; external auditory canal meatus. Furnas concha-mastoid sutures pass from the posterior concha to the mastoid periosteum. Place the mastoid bite posterior enough that the concha rotates back and medially; anterior placement pushes the concha into the meatus. Conservative conchal cartilage excision is an alternative for deep conchae.
A-otoplasty.layer6: Lobule and upper pole — cauda helicis (cartilage tail into the lobule); lobular fibrofatty tissue; superior helix. Correct a protruding lobule by skin excision, fishtail excision, or repositioning the cauda helicis. Check that the upper pole, middle third and lobule set back in proportion before tying the final sutures.
A-otoplasty.layer7: Closure and dressing — postauricular skin; conforming dressing in the helical folds. Cover sutures with soft tissue so they do not extrude. Obtain hemostasis carefully. Use a light, padded dressing that supports the new shape without pressure necrosis.
A-otoplasty.landmark1: Planned antihelical fold — Fold the helix gently back toward the scalp with a finger; the line where the ear folds naturally marks the new antihelix, transferred to the posterior surface with needles and ink (Guides suture position so the fold is smooth and correctly placed)
A-otoplasty.landmark2: Concha depth and conchal wall — Press the concha toward the mastoid; if the helix sits too far out after the fold is made, the concha is deep (conchal excess) (Decides whether conchal setback or cartilage reduction is needed)
A-otoplasty.landmark3: Mastoid periosteum anchor site — Behind the concha, after excising postauricular soft tissue, take a firm bite of periosteum over the mastoid posterior to the conchal attachment (Correct posterior anchor rotates the concha back without narrowing the canal)
A-otoplasty.landmark4: External auditory meatus — Watch the meatus from the front while tightening the concha-mastoid sutures (Shows any narrowing before the sutures are tied)
A-otoplasty.landmark5: Helix-to-mastoid distance — Measure from the outer helical rim to the scalp at the upper pole, middle third and lobule on both sides (Checks symmetry and avoids telephone or reverse telephone deformity)
A-otoplasty.danger1: Anterior auricular skin — where: Thin, tightly adherent to perichondrium on the lateral surface; protect: Do not pass sutures through the anterior skin; score gently in a careful plane; avoid tight dressings; injury: Skin necrosis, cartilage exposure, sharp ridging
A-otoplasty.danger2: Auricular cartilage and its blood supply — where: Cartilage depends on the perichondrium; hematoma separates them; protect: Careful hemostasis, light dressing, check early for unilateral pain; injury: Hematoma, then chondritis (often Pseudomonas) and cartilage loss with cauliflower deformity
A-otoplasty.danger3: External auditory canal — where: Immediately anterior and medial to the concha; protect: Place concha-mastoid sutures posteriorly; remove postauricular soft tissue that would push the concha forward; injury: Narrowing of the meatus
A-otoplasty.danger4: Great auricular nerve — where: Runs up across the sternocleidomastoid to the lobule and posterior auricle; protect: Keep dissection superficial and near the posterior auricle when working low near the lobule; injury: Numbness of the lobule and lower ear; neuroma
A-otoplasty.danger5: Permanent sutures — where: Just under the posterior skin; protect: Bury knots under soft tissue; avoid excess skin excision that tightens the closure; injury: Suture extrusion, granuloma, sinus tract
A-otoplasty.variant1: Antihelix effacement alone, conchal excess alone, or both, often with a prominent lobule
A-otoplasty.variant2: Stahl ear (third crus) and constricted (cup or lop) ears need different cartilage work
A-otoplasty.variant3: Asymmetry is common; the two ears may need different maneuvers
A-otoplasty.variant4: Stiff adult cartilage often needs scoring, whereas a child's cartilage may fold with sutures alone
A-otoplasty.supply1: Arterial supply — Posterior auricular artery (ECA) posteriorly; superficial temporal artery anterior auricular branches; perforators pass through the cartilage
A-otoplasty.supply2: Sensory — Great auricular (C2-C3) to the lower and posterior ear; lesser occipital to the upper posterior ear; auriculotemporal (V3) to the tragus and anterosuperior ear; Arnold nerve (CN X) to the concha and posterior canal
A-otoplasty.supply3: Embryology — Six hillocks of His: hillocks 1-3 from the first arch (tragus, helical crus, part of the helix) and 4-6 from the second arch (antihelix, antitragus and most of the remaining auricle); texts differ on the exact derivative of each hillock
A-otoplasty.pimp1: Q: What are the two most common anatomic causes of prominent ears? A: Underdeveloped antihelical fold and excessive conchal depth (often with lobule protrusion)
A-otoplasty.pimp2: Q: What do Mustardé sutures correct? A: An effaced antihelical fold; they are scapha-to-concha mattress sutures that create the fold
A-otoplasty.pimp3: Q: What do Furnas sutures correct? A: Conchal protrusion; they are concha-to-mastoid sutures that set the concha back
A-otoplasty.pimp4: Q: What happens if concha-mastoid sutures are placed too anteriorly on the mastoid? A: The concha is pushed forward into the canal and narrows the external auditory meatus
A-otoplasty.pimp5: Q: What is a telephone ear deformity? A: Overcorrection of the middle third with relative undercorrection of the upper pole and lobule
A-otoplasty.pimp6: Q: What is the main arterial supply of the auricle? A: Posterior auricular artery, with contribution from the superficial temporal artery
A-otoplasty.pimp7: Q: What is the earliest sign of postoperative hematoma? A: Sudden unilateral pain
A-otoplasty.pimp8: Q: Which branchial arch forms most of the auricle? A: The second arch (hillocks 4-6, including the antihelix and antitragus)
A-otoplasty.pimp9: Q: Why can neonatal ear molding work? A: Maternal estrogen keeps the newborn's cartilage soft in the first weeks after birth
OR-otoplasty.step1: Mark the desired antihelical fold, conchal position, and helical-mastoid relationship bilaterally before infiltration, comparing symmetry from frontal and posterior views.
OR-otoplasty.step2: Use a posterior auricular incision and elevate skin/perichondrial flaps only as needed to expose the deforming cartilage while preserving vascularity.
OR-otoplasty.step3: Create or weaken the antihelical fold with cartilage-sparing sutures/scoring according to cartilage stiffness, avoiding full-thickness anterior skin injury.
OR-otoplasty.step4: Place Mustardé-type mattress sutures to establish the antihelix with smooth curvature rather than a sharp ridge.
OR-otoplasty.step5: Correct conchal excess/protrusion with concha-mastoid sutures or conservative cartilage reduction while protecting the external auditory canal.
OR-otoplasty.step6: Address lobule projection and superior-pole asymmetry before tying final sutures permanently.
OR-otoplasty.step7: Compare both ears for projection, contour, and absence of telephone/reverse-telephone deformity, then obtain meticulous hemostasis.
OR-otoplasty.step8: Close without exposed permanent sutures and apply a nonischemic protective dressing while monitoring for hematoma and cartilage infection.

## Functional Septorhinoplasty / Nasal Valve Repair  (slug: septorhino, region: Facial Plastics / Trauma)

A-septorhino.summary: Nasal obstruction is treated by straightening the septum and widening or stiffening the internal and external nasal valves. Safety depends on leaving a strong dorsal and caudal L-strut attached at the keystone and anterior nasal spine, and on keeping at least one mucoperichondrial flap intact opposite every tear.
A-septorhino.layer1: Skin and soft-tissue envelope (open approach) — transcolumellar incision at the narrowest columella; marginal incisions along the caudal edge of the lateral crura; columellar arteries (divided); nasal SMAS; sub-SMAS / supraperichondrial plane over the lower lateral cartilages. The marginal incision follows the caudal border of the lateral crus, not the alar rim. Elevate deep to the nasal SMAS, directly on perichondrium, because the main vessels run superficial to that plane. Skin is thinnest at the rhinion and thicker at the nasion, supratip and tip.
A-septorhino.layer2: Dorsum and middle vault — upper lateral cartilages (ULCs); dorsal septum; rhinion and keystone (nasal bones overlapping the ULCs and septum); periosteum of the nasal bones (subperiosteal plane). Move from supraperichondrial over the ULCs to subperiosteal over the nasal bones. Separate the ULCs from the dorsal septum sharply, preserving the underlying mucosa, when spreader grafts or flaps are planned.
A-septorhino.layer3: Septal access and flap elevation — caudal septum; mucoperichondrium (subperichondrial plane, bluish-gray cartilage); quadrangular cartilage; bony-cartilaginous junction; chondrovomerine and maxillary crest junction. Enter through a hemitransfixion or Killian incision, or from above between the medial crura in an open approach. Elevate under the perichondrium. Inferiorly the perichondrium and periosteum do not continue smoothly at the maxillary crest, so flaps tear there; an inferior tunnel from the nasal floor, joined to the superior tunnel, helps.
A-septorhino.layer4: Deep septum — perpendicular plate of the ethmoid (posterosuperior); vomer (posteroinferior); maxillary crest and premaxilla; incisive canal (anterior floor) with the nasopalatine nerve and vessels. Resect deviated bone with through-cutting instruments. Do not twist or rock the perpendicular plate, because it is continuous with the cribriform plate. Keep away from the incisive canal when removing the anterior crest.
A-septorhino.layer5: Cartilage harvest and L-strut — dorsal strut; caudal strut; keystone attachment; anterior nasal spine attachment. Leave at least about 1-1.5 cm of cartilage dorsally and caudally, wider if the cartilage is weak. Keep the dorsal strut attached at the keystone. A caudal strut that has left the spine is sutured back to it.
A-septorhino.layer6: Internal valve reconstruction — internal nasal valve: septum, caudal ULC, head of inferior turbinate, nasal floor; spreader grafts or autospreader flaps between septum and ULC. The internal valve is the narrowest part of the airway; the angle between septum and caudal ULC is normally about 10-15 degrees. Spreader grafts or flaps widen the angle and support the middle vault after dorsal hump reduction.
A-septorhino.layer7: Osteotomies — piriform aperture; frontal process of the maxilla; Webster triangle (bone left at the inferior piriform aperture); nasofacial junction; nasal bone-frontal bone junction (root). Low-to-high lateral osteotomies begin at the piriform aperture above the inferior turbinate attachment and leave a small triangle of bone (Webster triangle) to protect the lateral valve. Keep the superior end below the medial canthus to protect the lacrimal sac. Elevate periosteum only along the osteotomy path to keep the bones supported.
A-septorhino.layer8: Tip and external valve — lateral crura (external valve support); medial crura and footplates; scroll region (ULC-LLC junction); alar rim (fibrofatty, no cartilage); nasal sill. Columellar strut or septal extension graft supports the tip; lateral crural strut grafts (deep to the crura) or alar batten grafts (in a pocket at the point of maximal collapse) stiffen the sidewall.
A-septorhino.landmark1: Subperichondrial septal plane — Score the perichondrium at the caudal septum until gray-blue cartilage shows, then elevate with a Cottle elevator; correct plane is relatively bloodless and the cartilage looks blue-gray (Flaps raised under the perichondrium hold blood supply and resist tearing)
A-septorhino.landmark2: Keystone area — Palpate the junction where nasal bones overlap the cephalic ULCs on the dorsum, at or just above the rhinion; the dorsal septum joins the perpendicular plate under it (Disruption leads to collapse of the middle vault and saddling)
A-septorhino.landmark3: Internal nasal valve — On anterior rhinoscopy, look at the angle between the caudal ULC edge and the septum about 1-1.5 cm in from the nostril; Cottle or modified Cottle maneuver (lifting the sidewall with a curette) tests it (Target for spreader grafts and flaps)
A-septorhino.landmark4: Anterior nasal spine — Palpate in the midline at the base of the columella through the hemitransfixion incision; the caudal septum rests on it (Anchor for the caudal strut and septal extension grafts)
A-septorhino.landmark5: Webster triangle — Start the lateral osteotomy at the piriform aperture just above the head of the inferior turbinate, leaving a small triangle of bone at the inferior aperture (Protects the lateral internal valve from narrowing)
A-septorhino.landmark6: External nasal branch of the anterior ethmoidal nerve — Emerges between the caudal nasal bone and the ULC on each side of the dorsum about 6-10 mm off the midline (Injury causes tip numbness; dissect deep to it on the dorsum)
A-septorhino.danger1: Mucoperichondrial flaps — where: On both sides of the septum; tears are most common at the chondrovomerine and maxillary crest junction and over spurs; protect: Stay subperichondrial; use inferior tunnels; repair tears and never leave tears facing each other on both sides; injury: Septal perforation with crusting, whistling and bleeding
A-septorhino.danger2: Dorsal and caudal L-strut — where: Dorsal edge from keystone to septal angle and caudal edge from septal angle to the spine; protect: Leave at least about 1-1.5 cm of strut; keep keystone and spine attachments; reinforce weak segments; injury: Saddle nose, loss of tip support, tip ptosis, columellar retraction
A-septorhino.danger3: Cribriform plate — where: Superior attachment of the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid; protect: Cut the perpendicular plate with sharp instruments; do not twist or rock bone; injury: CSF leak, anosmia
A-septorhino.danger4: Nasopalatine nerve and vessels — where: Incisive canal in the anterior maxillary crest behind the upper incisors; protect: Limit resection of the anterior crest; stay subperiosteal; injury: Numbness of the upper incisors and anterior palate; bleeding
A-septorhino.danger5: Nasal tip skin blood supply — where: Lateral nasal arteries (from the angular artery) run in the subdermal plexus just above the alar groove; columellar arteries are divided in an open approach; protect: Elevate deep to the nasal SMAS; avoid aggressive defatting; avoid alar base incisions that cross the supra-alar region; injury: Tip skin necrosis
A-septorhino.danger6: Lacrimal sac — where: In the lacrimal fossa posterior to the frontal process of the maxilla, near the medial canthus; protect: Keep the lateral osteotomy anterior and below the medial canthal level; injury: Epiphora, dacryocystitis
A-septorhino.variant1: Short nasal bones with long ULCs make the middle vault weak and prone to collapse after hump reduction
A-septorhino.variant2: Weak, thin or malpositioned (cephalically oriented) lateral crura cause external valve collapse
A-septorhino.variant3: Caudal septal deviation off the spine can obstruct a nostril even when the posterior septum is straight
A-septorhino.variant4: Concha bullosa or a large inferior turbinate narrows the internal valve area
A-septorhino.variant5: Prior septoplasty may have left a deficient L-strut or no harvestable cartilage
A-septorhino.supply1: Septal arterial supply — Kiesselbach plexus anteriorly: anterior ethmoidal (ophthalmic), sphenopalatine posterior septal branch (maxillary), greater palatine through the incisive canal, superior labial (facial)
A-septorhino.supply2: External nose arterial supply — Lateral nasal and angular arteries (facial), columellar branches (superior labial), dorsal nasal (ophthalmic)
A-septorhino.supply3: Sensory — External nasal branch of the anterior ethmoidal nerve (tip), infratrochlear (upper dorsum), infraorbital (sidewall, ala); nasopalatine and anterior ethmoidal internally
A-septorhino.supply4: Motor — Facial nerve buccal/zygomatic branches to nasalis and dilator naris (dilator naris helps hold the external valve open)
A-septorhino.pimp1: Q: What are the boundaries of the internal nasal valve? A: Septum, caudal edge of the upper lateral cartilage, head of the inferior turbinate and the nasal floor
A-septorhino.pimp2: Q: What is the normal internal valve angle? A: About 10-15 degrees
A-septorhino.pimp3: Q: What are the bony and cartilaginous components of the septum? A: Quadrangular cartilage, perpendicular plate of the ethmoid, vomer, maxillary and palatine crests (with the premaxilla anteriorly)
A-septorhino.pimp4: Q: What are the three major tip support mechanisms? A: Size, shape and strength of the lower lateral cartilages; attachment of the medial crural footplates to the caudal septum; attachment of the upper lateral cartilages to the lower laterals at the scroll
A-septorhino.pimp5: Q: What is the keystone area? A: The junction where the nasal bones overlap the upper lateral cartilages and the septum joins the perpendicular plate
A-septorhino.pimp6: Q: Which arteries form Kiesselbach plexus? A: Anterior ethmoidal, sphenopalatine (posterior septal), greater palatine and superior labial arteries
A-septorhino.pimp7: Q: Why must the perpendicular plate not be twisted? A: It is attached to the cribriform plate; twisting can fracture the skull base and cause a CSF leak
A-septorhino.pimp8: Q: Where does the external nasal branch of the anterior ethmoidal nerve emerge? A: Between the caudal edge of the nasal bone and the upper lateral cartilage
A-septorhino.pimp9: Q: What is the Webster triangle? A: A small triangle of bone left at the inferior piriform aperture during a low-to-high lateral osteotomy to preserve the inferior turbinate attachment and the internal valve
OR-septorhino.step1: Analyze and mark the external nasal deformity, septum, internal/external valves, dorsal lines, tip support, and airway goals before infiltration and incision.
OR-septorhino.step2: Expose the septum through open or endonasal approach and elevate mucoperichondrial flaps while preserving a structurally adequate dorsal/caudal L-strut.
OR-septorhino.step3: Harvest only the septal cartilage needed for reconstruction and correct obstructing bony/cartilaginous deviation without destabilizing the keystone area.
OR-septorhino.step4: Open and reconstruct the middle vault as needed, preserving or restoring internal nasal-valve angle with spreader graft/flap or equivalent support.
OR-septorhino.step5: Perform dorsal reduction/augmentation and controlled osteotomies as required, protecting mucosa and maintaining symmetric dorsal aesthetic lines.
OR-septorhino.step6: Rebuild tip/caudal support with the planned septal extension, columellar strut, lateral-crural, or alar-batten grafts to correct external valve collapse without over-rotation.
OR-septorhino.step7: Reassess septum, valve patency, tip symmetry, dorsal contour, and bony pyramid before closing mucosa/skin and replacing septum in the midline.
OR-septorhino.step8: Apply internal splints/external cast selectively and document airway, skin-perfusion, and postoperative edema/bleeding precautions.

## Facial Full-Thickness / Split-Thickness Skin Graft  (slug: skin-graft-face, region: Facial Plastics / Trauma)

A-skin-graft-face.summary: A skin graft brings in epidermis and dermis with no blood supply of its own, so it survives only if it touches a well-vascularized bed. Success depends on choosing the right donor for color and thickness, matching graft thickness to the risk of contraction, and preventing blood, fluid or shear from separating the graft from the bed in the first days.
A-skin-graft-face.layer1: Recipient bed — subcutaneous fat or muscle; perichondrium; periosteum; bare cartilage or bone (no take without perichondrium or periosteum). Grafts take on perichondrium, periosteum, muscle, fascia and fat. They do not take on bare cartilage, bone or tendon; on these, use a flap, delay grafting until granulation forms, or perforate or burr the surface.
A-skin-graft-face.layer2: Template and donor choice — preauricular skin; postauricular skin; supraclavicular skin; upper eyelid skin; conchal skin (with cartilage as composite); thigh or buttock for split-thickness. Nearby head and neck donors give the best color match. Upper eyelid is the thinnest skin; preauricular and postauricular skin suit the nose and ear; supraclavicular skin covers larger areas.
A-skin-graft-face.layer3: Full-thickness harvest — epidermis; full dermis; subcutaneous fat (removed). Excise the ellipse at the level of subcutaneous fat. Remove all fat from the underside with scissors until the white dermis shows, without making holes.
A-skin-graft-face.layer4: Split-thickness harvest — epidermis; partial dermis; dermatome setting (thin about 0.008-0.012 inch, medium about 0.012-0.018 inch, thick about 0.018-0.030 inch). Set the dermatome and keep even pressure on a lubricated, tense donor. Fine punctate bleeding means a thin graft; larger bleeding points mean a deeper cut.
A-skin-graft-face.layer5: Inset — graft edges; recipient wound edges; basting or quilting sutures. Fit the graft exactly, with the dermal side down. Basting sutures in the center keep it on contoured surfaces like the concha or nasal sidewall. Pie-crust small holes allow fluid to escape.
A-skin-graft-face.layer6: Bolster — tie-over or sutured bolster; nonadherent dressing. A bolster keeps the graft still and pressed to the bed for about 5-7 days. Do not compress nearby lids, nasal valve or lip.
A-skin-graft-face.layer7: Donor site — primary closure of FTSG donor; dressing of STSG donor. Close FTSG donor sites primarily along relaxed skin lines. STSG donors heal from adnexal structures left in the dermis.
A-skin-graft-face.landmark1: Vascularized bed — Look for pinpoint bleeding and pink tissue; perichondrium looks glistening and smooth, bare cartilage is white and dull (Grafts need a living bed to take)
A-skin-graft-face.landmark2: Preauricular donor — Skin in front of the tragus and helical root, where loose and hairless (above the sideburn in men is not hairless) (Good color and thickness match for the nose; scar hides in the preauricular crease)
A-skin-graft-face.landmark3: Postauricular donor — Skin of the posterior auricle and mastoid in the postauricular sulcus (Thin skin, hidden scar)
A-skin-graft-face.landmark4: Supraclavicular donor — Skin just above the clavicle in the lower neck, above the platysma (Larger hairless donor with acceptable match)
A-skin-graft-face.landmark5: Dermal undersurface of a FTSG — After defatting, the underside looks white and shiny with no yellow fat globules (Fat blocks plasmatic imbibition and inosculation)
A-skin-graft-face.danger1: Graft-bed interface — where: Under the graft in the first several days; protect: Hemostasis, pie-crusting, bolster, immobilization; injury: Hematoma or seroma lifts the graft and causes loss
A-skin-graft-face.danger2: Lower eyelid and lip — where: Free margins near the graft; protect: Use FTSG near free margins; oversize slightly; avoid vertical tension; injury: Ectropion or lip retraction from contraction
A-skin-graft-face.danger3: Preauricular donor vessels and nerve — where: Superficial temporal vessels and auriculotemporal nerve just in front of the tragus, deep to the skin and fat; protect: Harvest superficially at the level of subcutaneous fat; injury: Bleeding, numbness
A-skin-graft-face.danger4: Supraclavicular donor structures — where: External jugular vein and supraclavicular nerves deep to the platysma; protect: Harvest above the platysma; injury: Bleeding, numbness of the chest and shoulder
A-skin-graft-face.danger5: Contour — where: Deep defects; protect: Fill depth with a flap or delay grafting until granulation; injury: Depressed, shiny contour mismatch
A-skin-graft-face.variant1: Thick sebaceous nasal tip skin is hard to match; FTSG from preauricular or melolabial skin looks best
A-skin-graft-face.variant2: Prior radiation reduces bed vascularity and graft take
A-skin-graft-face.variant3: Hair-bearing donors in men (sideburn, beard) transfer hair
A-skin-graft-face.supply1: Graft survival phases — Plasmatic imbibition (first 24-48 h), inosculation (about 48-72 h), then revascularization with new vessel ingrowth over the first week
A-skin-graft-face.supply2: Contraction — Primary contraction (immediate recoil from elastin) is greater with FTSG; secondary contraction (during healing) is greater with STSG
A-skin-graft-face.supply3: Donor sensory — Preauricular: auriculotemporal (V3); postauricular: great auricular (C2-C3); supraclavicular: supraclavicular nerves (C3-C4)
A-skin-graft-face.pimp1: Q: What are the phases of skin graft take? A: Plasmatic imbibition, inosculation, then revascularization (neovascularization)
A-skin-graft-face.pimp2: Q: Which graft has more primary contraction and which more secondary contraction? A: FTSG has more primary contraction; STSG has more secondary contraction
A-skin-graft-face.pimp3: Q: Which recipient beds will not take a skin graft? A: Bare cartilage without perichondrium, bare bone without periosteum, bare tendon, and heavily infected or irradiated tissue with poor blood supply
A-skin-graft-face.pimp4: Q: What is the thinnest skin in the body for grafting? A: Upper eyelid skin
A-skin-graft-face.pimp5: Q: What is the most common cause of graft failure? A: Hematoma or seroma separating the graft from the bed
A-skin-graft-face.pimp6: Q: How long is a bolster usually left on a facial graft? A: About 5-7 days
A-skin-graft-face.pimp7: Q: Why does a STSG donor site heal on its own? A: Epithelium regrows from adnexal structures (hair follicles, sweat glands) left in the remaining dermis
OR-skin-graft-face.step1: Prepare the recipient defect to a clean, well-vascularized bed and achieve meticulous hemostasis before harvesting the graft.
OR-skin-graft-face.step2: Template the defect accurately and choose full-thickness versus split-thickness donor skin based on contour, contraction risk, color/texture, and recipient vascularity.
OR-skin-graft-face.step3: Harvest the graft at uniform thickness; for full-thickness graft, remove excess subcutaneous fat until only dermis remains without perforating it.
OR-skin-graft-face.step4: Orient and trim the graft to fit the defect exactly without folding, excessive tension, or unnecessary overlap.
OR-skin-graft-face.step5: Secure the graft perimeter and internal quilting/basting sutures so the undersurface remains in complete contact with the vascular bed.
OR-skin-graft-face.step6: Evacuate any blood/air trapped beneath the graft and obtain hemostasis without lifting the construct repeatedly.
OR-skin-graft-face.step7: Apply a bolster or pressure dressing that immobilizes the graft without causing ischemic compression of surrounding facial structures.
OR-skin-graft-face.step8: Close/dress the donor site and document the timing of bolster removal and monitoring for hematoma, infection, shear, and graft loss.

## ZMC ORIF  (slug: zmc-orif, region: Facial Plastics / Trauma)

A-zmc-orif.summary: The zygoma sets malar projection, facial width and the lateral and inferior orbit; a displaced zygomaticomaxillary complex fracture rotates it at its articulations with the frontal, sphenoid, temporal and maxillary bones. Safety and accuracy depend on reading rotation at the sphenozygomatic suture in the lateral orbital wall, and on respecting the globe, infraorbital nerve and frontal branch of the facial nerve as each fixation point is exposed.
A-zmc-orif.layer1: Gingivobuccal (sublabial) approach to the buttress — upper buccal sulcus mucosa (leave a cuff on the gingival side); buccinator / levator labii fibers; periosteum over the anterior maxilla; zygomaticomaxillary buttress; infraorbital foramen and nerve superiorly. Incise a few millimeters above the mucogingival junction so a mucosal cuff remains for closure, from the canine region back to about the first molar (stay anterior to the parotid papilla opposite the upper second molar). Elevate subperiosteally upward and laterally; identify the infraorbital nerve before stripping the upper anterior maxilla.
A-zmc-orif.layer2: Lower lid (transconjunctival) approach to the rim and floor — palpebral conjunctiva below the tarsus; capsulopalpebral fascia / lower lid retractors; orbital septum and orbital fat (retroseptal route); or orbicularis-septum plane (preseptal route); arcus marginalis and periosteum of the infraorbital rim. Incise the conjunctiva below the inferior tarsal border; a lateral canthotomy and inferior cantholysis widens exposure. Incise periosteum on the anterior face of the rim, not on its top edge, then elevate subperiosteally over the rim onto the orbital floor. The inferior oblique origin lies at the anteromedial floor just lateral to the lacrimal sac fossa; keep medial extension subperiosteal.
A-zmc-orif.layer3: Lateral brow / upper blepharoplasty approach to the ZF suture — skin and orbicularis; periosteum of the lateral orbital rim; zygomaticofrontal suture; lateral orbital tubercle (Whitnall) on the inner rim. Incise within the lateral brow or upper lid crease, go straight to bone and elevate subperiosteally both on the outer rim and inside the orbit. Inside the lateral orbit you can follow the lateral wall back to the sphenozygomatic suture.
A-zmc-orif.layer4: Temporal approach to the arch (Gillies) when the arch is depressed — temporal hair-bearing skin; temporoparietal (superficial temporal) fascia with the frontal branch and superficial temporal vessels; deep temporal fascia; temporalis muscle. Incise inside the temporal hairline and divide the temporoparietal fascia and the deep temporal fascia. The elevator passes deep to the deep temporal fascia, on the surface of temporalis, down under the arch; that plane keeps the frontal branch superficial to you.
A-zmc-orif.layer5: Reduction and assessment of rotation — sphenozygomatic suture (lateral orbital wall); zygomaticofrontal suture; zygomaticomaxillary buttress; infraorbital rim; zygomatic arch. Reduce with a Carroll-Girard screw or an elevator under the body. Alignment at one point can hide rotation; the long sphenozygomatic suture line is the most reliable check, with the arch and buttress confirming width and projection.
A-zmc-orif.layer6: Fixation — ZF suture plate (thick bone); zygomaticomaxillary buttress plate (screws above tooth apices); infraorbital rim plate (thin, palpable bone). Fix the most reliable point first, then add points as stability requires (one-, two- or three-point fixation). Buttress screws stay above the root apices; the canine root is the longest. Rim plates are low profile because lid skin is thin.
A-zmc-orif.layer7: Orbital floor exploration and reconstruction — infraorbital groove and canal (the floor medial to it is thinnest); inferior rectus and inferior oblique; orbital fat and periorbita; posterior ledge (orbital process of the palatine bone); inferior orbital fissure laterally. The floor rises from anterior to posterior and from lateral to medial. Elevate periorbita out of the defect, find stable bone on all sides, and rest the implant on the posterior ledge. The optic canal lies far posterior and superomedial; dissection does not need to go behind the posterior ledge.
A-zmc-orif.layer8: Final checks and closure — forced duction of the globe; lateral canthal tendon (resuspend to the lateral orbital tubercle if divided); lower lid layers; sulcus mucosa and mentalis/buccinator layer. Repeat forced ductions after implant placement. Resuspend the lateral canthus if cantholysis was done. Close the periosteum over the rim and buttress to reduce cheek ptosis.
A-zmc-orif.landmark1: Sphenozygomatic suture — Inside the lateral orbit after subperiosteal elevation through the brow or lower lid incision, follow the inner surface of the lateral orbital rim posteriorly onto the lateral orbital wall, where the zygoma meets the greater wing of the sphenoid (Long suture line; when it is aligned, rotation of the zygoma is usually corrected)
A-zmc-orif.landmark2: Infraorbital nerve and foramen — Roughly in the midpupillary line, about 6-10 mm below the infraorbital rim (individual range wider); from the sublabial approach, elevate upward over the canine fossa until the neurovascular bundle tents the periosteum (Must be protected during sublabial exposure and rim plating; often runs through the fracture line)
A-zmc-orif.landmark3: Zygomaticomaxillary buttress — Thick vertical bone ridge rising from above the first molar toward the zygoma body, felt through the sublabial incision lateral to the canine fossa (Strong bone for a plate that restores midface height and projection)
A-zmc-orif.landmark4: Zygomaticofrontal suture — Palpable step or diastasis on the lateral orbital rim, deep to the lateral brow tail; expose through a lateral brow or upper lid crease incision (Thick bone for fixation and a check on vertical position)
A-zmc-orif.landmark5: Posterior ledge of the orbital floor — Follow intact floor bone posteriorly along the medial side of the defect, beyond the end of the infraorbital groove, until a firm upward ledge is felt with the elevator (Posterior support for the orbital implant; without it the implant sags and enophthalmos persists)
A-zmc-orif.landmark6: Lateral orbital (Whitnall) tubercle — Small bony prominence a few millimeters inside the lateral orbital rim, roughly 1 cm below the ZF suture (Insertion of the lateral canthal tendon; site for canthopexy after cantholysis)
A-zmc-orif.danger1: Globe and optic nerve — where: Directly above the floor dissection; optic canal at the orbital apex, posterior and superomedial; protect: Use a malleable retractor to hold periorbita, release retraction periodically, check pupils, and avoid blind dissection past the posterior ledge; injury: Vision loss from retrobulbar hematoma, direct injury or compression; treat a tense proptotic orbit with lateral canthotomy and inferior cantholysis
A-zmc-orif.danger2: Infraorbital nerve (V2) — where: In the infraorbital groove and canal on the floor and exiting the foramen below the rim; protect: Identify it before plating; keep rim plates and screws off the foramen; do not place floor implants that compress the groove; injury: Numbness of the cheek, lateral nose, upper lip and upper incisor gingiva
A-zmc-orif.danger3: Frontal (temporal) branch of the facial nerve — where: Runs within or on the deep surface of the temporoparietal fascia, crossing the middle of the zygomatic arch roughly along a line from just below the tragus to above the lateral brow; protect: In a Gillies approach, place the elevator deep to the deep temporal fascia; in a coronal approach, stay deep to the superficial layer of the deep temporal fascia over the arch; injury: Ipsilateral brow ptosis and loss of forehead wrinkling
A-zmc-orif.danger4: Lower eyelid lamellae — where: Anterior (skin/orbicularis), middle (septum) and posterior (tarsus, capsulopalpebral fascia, conjunctiva) lamellae of the lower lid; protect: Prefer transconjunctival access; close periosteum; resuspend the lateral canthus; avoid septal scarring; injury: Ectropion, scleral show, lid retraction or entropion
A-zmc-orif.danger5: Maxillary tooth roots — where: Beneath the zygomaticomaxillary buttress and canine fossa; the canine root is the longest; protect: Keep buttress screws above the root apices and use monocortical screws; injury: Devitalized teeth, root injury, painful hardware
A-zmc-orif.danger6: Inferior rectus and inferior oblique — where: Inferior rectus on the floor, can herniate through the defect; inferior oblique arises at the anteromedial floor; protect: Free all herniated tissue from the defect; keep medial dissection subperiosteal; forced ductions before and after implant placement; injury: Diplopia, restricted upgaze, entrapment
A-zmc-orif.variant1: Infraorbital foramen height and position vary, and accessory infraorbital foramina occur
A-zmc-orif.variant2: Maxillary sinus pneumatization varies; a large sinus thins the floor and buttress
A-zmc-orif.variant3: Edentulous or atrophic maxilla leaves little buttress bone and changes screw placement
A-zmc-orif.variant4: Zygoma bipartitum (a divided zygoma) is rare but can mimic a fracture line on CT
A-zmc-orif.supply1: Arterial supply — Maxillary artery → infraorbital artery; transverse facial artery (superficial temporal); zygomatico-orbital and zygomaticofacial branches; angular artery medially
A-zmc-orif.supply2: Sensory — Infraorbital nerve (V2) for cheek and upper lip; zygomaticofacial and zygomaticotemporal nerves (V2) for the malar and temple skin
A-zmc-orif.supply3: Motor at risk — Frontal branch of CN VII over the arch; CN III/IV/VI and extraocular muscles within the orbit
A-zmc-orif.supply4: Displacing forces — Masseter pulls the body and arch inferiorly and medially
A-zmc-orif.pimp1: Q: Which four bones does the zygoma articulate with? A: Frontal, temporal, maxilla and the greater wing of the sphenoid
A-zmc-orif.pimp2: Q: Which articulation is the most reliable indicator of correct zygoma rotation? A: The sphenozygomatic suture in the lateral orbital wall
A-zmc-orif.pimp3: Q: Which muscle displaces a ZMC fracture inferiorly? A: Masseter
A-zmc-orif.pimp4: Q: Why do patients with a depressed arch have trismus? A: The arch impinges on the coronoid process and temporalis
A-zmc-orif.pimp5: Q: In a Gillies approach, in which plane does the elevator travel? A: Deep to the deep temporal fascia, on the temporalis muscle
A-zmc-orif.pimp6: Q: Where does the lateral canthal tendon insert? A: Lateral orbital (Whitnall) tubercle, just inside the lateral orbital rim
A-zmc-orif.pimp7: Q: Through which foramen does V2 leave the skull, and how does it reach the cheek? A: Foramen rotundum to the pterygopalatine fossa, then as the infraorbital nerve through the inferior orbital fissure, infraorbital groove, canal and foramen
A-zmc-orif.pimp8: Q: Which structures pass through the superior orbital fissure? A: CN III, IV, V1 branches (lacrimal, frontal, nasociliary), VI, and the superior ophthalmic vein
A-zmc-orif.pimp9: Q: What is the emergency treatment of retrobulbar hematoma with vision threat? A: Lateral canthotomy with inferior cantholysis
OR-zmc-orif.step1: Assess malar projection, ocular exam, trismus, and CT displacement at zygomaticofrontal, infraorbital rim, zygomaticomaxillary buttress, and arch interfaces.
OR-zmc-orif.step2: Expose the required fixation points through intraoral/lateral brow/transconjunctival approaches while protecting facial nerve and globe.
OR-zmc-orif.step3: Mobilize the zygoma and reduce it in three dimensions, using the sphenozygomatic region/arch/buttress alignment to judge rotation.
OR-zmc-orif.step4: Secure the most reliable reference point first, then sequentially fix additional buttresses while repeatedly checking malar symmetry.
OR-zmc-orif.step5: Inspect the orbital floor when the fracture pattern requires it and reconstruct any defect after the zygoma is reduced.
OR-zmc-orif.step6: Confirm infraorbital rim alignment, orbital volume, and absence of soft-tissue entrapment.
OR-zmc-orif.step7: Check forced ductions and ocular position before closure.
OR-zmc-orif.step8: Irrigate, obtain hemostasis, and close each approach in layers.

## Hyoid Suspension / Genioglossus Advancement Concepts  (slug: hyoid-genioglossus, region: Sleep Surgery)

A-hyoid-genioglossus.summary: Genioglossus advancement pulls the tongue base forward by advancing the bony window of the mandible that carries the genial tubercles, and hyoid suspension pulls the hyoid forward to open the hypopharynx. Safety depends on cutting the genial window below the tooth roots and medial to the mental foramina, and on staying on bone around the hyoid so the hypoglossal nerve, lingual artery and internal branch of the superior laryngeal nerve are not injured.
A-hyoid-genioglossus.layer1: Intraoral anterior mandible exposure — labial vestibule mucosa; mentalis muscle; periosteum of the anterior mandible; mental nerves at the lateral limits. Incise in the lower labial vestibule away from the gingiva, divide mentalis leaving a cuff to resuspend it, and elevate subperiosteally. Identify the mental foramina, usually below the second premolar or between the premolars.
A-hyoid-genioglossus.layer2: Genial window design — genial tubercles (lingual cortex, midline); incisor and canine root apices; inferior border of the mandible. Mark a rectangular window centered on the genial tubercles, which lie on the lingual cortex at about the level of the canine root apices or lower. Keep the upper cut several millimeters below the apices (the canine root is the longest) and leave an intact inferior border for strength.
A-hyoid-genioglossus.layer3: Osteotomy and advancement — outer (labial) cortex; medullary bone; lingual cortex with genioglossus attached; floor-of-mouth soft tissue behind the lingual cortex. Cut through both cortices, keeping the saw controlled at the lingual cortex to avoid floor-of-mouth vessels. Advance the segment with the genioglossus attached, rotate it so it cannot fall back, trim the outer cortex and marrow, and screw it to the mandible.
A-hyoid-genioglossus.layer4: Cervical approach to the hyoid — skin crease over the hyoid or thyrohyoid region; platysma; anterior jugular veins; infrahyoid strap muscles. Through a horizontal skin incision, divide platysma and expose the hyoid body in the midline between the anterior jugular veins.
A-hyoid-genioglossus.layer5: Hyoid release — hyoid body; lesser horns; medial greater horns; sternohyoid, omohyoid and thyrohyoid insertions; suprahyoid attachments (mylohyoid, geniohyoid, stylohyoid). Release only the attachments needed for the planned vector. Stay subperiosteal on the hyoid and medial on the greater horns rather than dissecting around their tips.
A-hyoid-genioglossus.layer6: Suspension (hyothyroidpexy) — thyroid cartilage alae / superior thyroid notch; permanent sutures around the hyoid; thyrohyoid membrane. Pass sutures around the hyoid body and through the upper thyroid cartilage on each side of the midline, and tie them to bring the hyoid forward and down over the thyroid cartilage. Older techniques suspended the hyoid to the mandible.
A-hyoid-genioglossus.layer7: Closure and airway — mentalis resuspension; vestibular mucosa; platysma and skin. Resuspend mentalis to prevent lower lip ptosis. Monitor the airway because floor-of-mouth hematoma or tongue swelling can obstruct it.
A-hyoid-genioglossus.landmark1: Genial tubercles — Midline lingual cortex of the anterior mandible; palpate bimanually through the floor of mouth and estimate their height relative to the canine roots on panoramic or CT images (The window must include the upper tubercles where genioglossus attaches)
A-hyoid-genioglossus.landmark2: Mental foramen — Below the second premolar or between the premolars, about midway between the alveolar crest and the inferior border in a dentate mandible (Lateral limit of the window; the nerve may loop forward a few millimeters in front of the foramen)
A-hyoid-genioglossus.landmark3: Canine root apex — Canine eminence on the labial surface; the root is long and extends low (Sets the height of the upper cut)
A-hyoid-genioglossus.landmark4: Hyoid body and greater horn — Palpate the hyoid as the first firm bar above the thyroid notch; follow its upper and lower edges laterally in a subperiosteal plane (Suspension target; staying on bone protects nerves and vessels)
A-hyoid-genioglossus.landmark5: Thyroid notch and upper thyroid cartilage — Palpate the notch in the midline below the hyoid; place sutures through the alae on either side (Anchor for hyothyroidpexy)
A-hyoid-genioglossus.danger1: Tooth roots — where: Above the genial window; the canine root extends lowest; protect: Measure root length on imaging; keep the upper cut several mm below the apices; injury: Devitalized teeth
A-hyoid-genioglossus.danger2: Mental nerve and inferior alveolar canal — where: Lateral to the window at the mental foramina, with an anterior loop in some patients; protect: Keep vertical cuts medial to the foramina with a safety margin; injury: Lower lip and chin numbness
A-hyoid-genioglossus.danger3: Floor-of-mouth vessels — where: Sublingual and submental artery branches behind the lingual cortex; protect: Controlled saw depth; check hemostasis; observe the airway; injury: Expanding floor-of-mouth hematoma and airway obstruction
A-hyoid-genioglossus.danger4: Hypoglossal nerve and lingual artery — where: Above the greater horn of the hyoid; the nerve on the hyoglossus, the artery deep to it; protect: Stay on the hyoid bone and medial on the greater horn; injury: Tongue weakness, bleeding
A-hyoid-genioglossus.danger5: Internal branch of the superior laryngeal nerve — where: Pierces the thyrohyoid membrane with the superior laryngeal artery below the lateral greater horn; protect: Avoid dissection lateral and inferior to the greater horns; injury: Supraglottic numbness and aspiration
A-hyoid-genioglossus.danger6: Mandibular fracture — where: Weakened symphysis after the window; protect: Leave adequate inferior border; avoid an overly large window; injury: Mandible fracture
A-hyoid-genioglossus.variant1: Genial tubercle height varies relative to the tooth roots; some patients have little room
A-hyoid-genioglossus.variant2: Anterior loop of the inferior alveolar nerve may extend forward of the mental foramen
A-hyoid-genioglossus.variant3: Edentulous mandibles are thin and fracture-prone
A-hyoid-genioglossus.variant4: Hyoid position (low hyoid) varies and affects the benefit of suspension
A-hyoid-genioglossus.supply1: Mandible — Inferior alveolar artery and nerve within the canal; mental branches at the foramen; lingual cortex blood supply from floor-of-mouth vessels
A-hyoid-genioglossus.supply2: Floor of mouth — Sublingual artery (lingual) and submental artery (facial)
A-hyoid-genioglossus.supply3: Genioglossus and geniohyoid — Genioglossus: CN XII; geniohyoid: C1 via XII; both arise from the genial tubercles (superior for genioglossus, inferior for geniohyoid)
A-hyoid-genioglossus.supply4: Hyoid region — Hypoglossal nerve and lingual artery above the greater horn; superior laryngeal artery and internal branch of SLN through the thyrohyoid membrane

A-hyoid-genioglossus.pimp2: Q: Where is the mental foramen usually found? A: Below the second premolar or between the first and second premolars
A-hyoid-genioglossus.pimp3: Q: What is the most feared early complication of genioglossus advancement? A: Floor-of-mouth hematoma with airway compromise
A-hyoid-genioglossus.pimp4: Q: What is the embryologic origin of the hyoid? A: Lesser horns and upper body from the second arch; greater horns and lower body from the third arch

A-hyoid-genioglossus.pimp6: Q: Which tooth root extends lowest in the anterior mandible? A: The canine
A-hyoid-genioglossus.pimp7: Q: In hyothyroidpexy, where is the hyoid moved? A: Forward and down, sutured over the upper thyroid cartilage
A-hyoid-genioglossus.pimp8: Q: What are the phases of the Riley-Powell protocol? A: Phase I: palatal surgery plus genioglossus advancement and/or hyoid suspension; phase II: maxillomandibular advancement

OR-hyoid-genioglossus.step6: Place symmetric suspension sutures or fixation from hyoid to thyroid cartilage/mandible according to technique and tension them to enlarge the hypopharyngeal airway.

## Hypoglossal Nerve Stimulator Implantation  (slug: hypoglossal-stimulator, region: Sleep Surgery)

A-hypoglossal-stimulator.summary: Hypoglossal nerve stimulation keeps the tongue base forward during inspiration by pacing the protrusor branches of CN XII. The key anatomic decision is placing the cuff distal to the retrusor (lateral) branches so only protrusor (medial) fibers are captured; the other risks are the marginal mandibular nerve, the lingual nerve and ranine veins, and, with older systems that use a separate respiratory sensing lead, the pleura. The current Inspire V system senses respiration with a sensor built into the generator, so there is no intercostal lead.
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.layer1: Submandibular incision — skin about two fingerbreadths below the mandible; platysma; investing layer of deep cervical fascia; marginal mandibular branch (deep to platysma, superficial to facial vessels). Make a horizontal incision in a skin crease below the mandible. Divide platysma and stay below the marginal mandibular branch by keeping the dissection low or by raising the fascia over the gland.
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.layer2: Submandibular triangle — submandibular gland; anterior belly and intermediate tendon of digastric; hyoid bone. Retract the gland superiorly and posteriorly. Find the digastric tendon above the hyoid and retract it inferiorly.
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.layer3: Hypoglossal nerve on hyoglossus — hyoglossus muscle; hypoglossal nerve; ranine veins (vena comitans of XII); mylohyoid posterior border. The nerve lies on the lateral surface of hyoglossus, deep to the digastric tendon and above the hyoid. Retract the mylohyoid anteriorly to follow it forward. Control the ranine veins, which run with it.
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.layer4: Branch mapping — lateral (retrusor) branches to styloglossus and hyoglossus; medial (protrusor) branch to genioglossus; branches to transverse and vertical intrinsic muscles; C1 fibers to geniohyoid. Follow the nerve forward until retrusor branches leave laterally. Stimulate to confirm: protrusor branches push the tongue forward; retrusor branches pull it back and up.
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.layer5: Cuff placement — protrusor branch bundle distal to retrusor branch takeoff. Place the cuff around the medial (protrusor) branches distal to the retrusor branches, without stretch, and anchor the lead to digastric tendon/fascia with a strain relief loop. The C1 fibers to geniohyoid, which pull the hyoid forward, are often included when they run with the medial branch, although inclusion has not been shown to change outcomes (Kumar et al., Laryngoscope 2020).
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.layer6: Chest generator pocket — infraclavicular skin; pectoralis major fascia. Create a pocket on the pectoralis fascia a few centimeters below the clavicle, usually on the right side to leave the left for cardiac devices.
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.layer7: Respiratory sensing lead (older systems only) — intercostal space; external intercostal muscle; internal intercostal muscle; intercostal neurovascular bundle; parietal pleura. Inspire V (FDA approved 2024) has an integrated respiratory sensor in the generator and needs no sensing lead or intercostal incision. With Inspire IV and earlier, place the lead between the external and internal intercostal muscles, sensor facing the pleura, in the space the device protocol specifies. Work at the upper border of the lower rib because the intercostal vessels and nerve run in the groove under the upper rib.
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.layer8: Tunneling and testing — subcutaneous tunnel over the clavicle and neck; generator. Tunnel leads superficial to the clavicle and platysma without sharp kinks. Test for straight or bilateral tongue protrusion without retraction, and a good respiratory signal from the integrated sensor or the sensing lead.
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.landmark1: Hypoglossal nerve — Retract the digastric tendon down and the submandibular gland up; the nerve lies on the hyoglossus just above the hyoid, deep to the tendon, with the ranine veins (Starting point for branch mapping)
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.landmark2: Pirogoff triangle — Bounded by the intermediate digastric tendon, the posterior border of mylohyoid and the hypoglossal nerve; the floor is hyoglossus (Lingual artery lies deep to hyoglossus in this triangle)
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.landmark3: Retrusor/protrusor branch point — Follow the nerve anteriorly under the mylohyoid edge; lateral branches turn up and back to styloglossus and hyoglossus, the medial branch continues forward to genioglossus; stimulate to confirm (Cuff must be distal to the retrusor branches)
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.landmark4: Lingual nerve and submandibular duct — Higher and deeper in the submandibular triangle; the lingual nerve is tethered to the gland by the submandibular ganglion and loops under the duct (Avoid superior dissection that injures them)
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.landmark5: Intercostal plane — Split the external intercostal fibers (running down and forward) to find the internal intercostal fibers (running perpendicular) (Correct plane for the sensing lead (older systems with a separate lead) without entering the pleura)
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.danger1: Hypoglossal nerve and protrusor branches — where: On the hyoglossus deep to the digastric tendon; protect: Gentle handling, minimal dissection, cuff without tension; injury: Tongue weakness, poor stimulation response
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.danger2: Marginal mandibular branch — where: Deep to platysma and superficial to the facial vessels along the mandible; protect: Low incision; dissect deep to the investing fascia over the gland; injury: Lower lip weakness
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.danger3: Ranine veins and lingual artery — where: Veins with XII on the hyoglossus; lingual artery deep to the hyoglossus; protect: Bipolar cautery and ligation; stay superficial to the hyoglossus; injury: Bleeding and floor-of-mouth hematoma with airway risk
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.danger4: Lingual nerve — where: Above the hypoglossal nerve, near the duct; protect: Limit superior dissection; injury: Tongue numbness and taste loss
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.danger5: Pleura and intercostal bundle (sensing-lead systems only) — where: Deep to the internal intercostal muscle; vessels under the upper rib; protect: Stay superficial to the internal intercostal; work near the upper border of the lower rib; injury: Pneumothorax, bleeding, intercostal neuralgia
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.variant1: The branch pattern of XII varies; the retrusor-protrusor transition must be found with stimulation, not by appearance alone
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.variant2: Ranine veins may be large and multiple, sometimes crossing the nerve
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.variant3: Thick neck or high hyoid changes the depth and position of the field
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.supply1: Nerve — CN XII exits the hypoglossal canal, runs between ICA and IJV, then forward below the posterior belly of digastric onto the hyoglossus
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.supply2: Tongue muscle supply — All intrinsic and extrinsic tongue muscles by XII except palatoglossus (vagus via pharyngeal plexus)
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.supply3: Arterial — Lingual artery deep to the hyoglossus; facial artery in the submandibular triangle
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.supply4: Venous — Ranine veins (venae comitantes of XII) on the hyoglossus
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.pimp1: Q: Which tongue muscle is the main protrusor? A: Genioglossus
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.pimp2: Q: Which muscles are retrusors and should be excluded from the cuff? A: Styloglossus and hyoglossus
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.pimp3: Q: Which tongue muscle is not supplied by CN XII? A: Palatoglossus (vagus via the pharyngeal plexus)
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.pimp4: Q: What are the boundaries of the Pirogoff triangle? A: Intermediate digastric tendon, posterior border of mylohyoid and the hypoglossal nerve; floor is hyoglossus with the lingual artery deep to it
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.pimp5: Q: Which vein runs with the hypoglossal nerve on the hyoglossus? A: The ranine vein (vena comitans of XII)
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.pimp6: Q: Which DISE finding is a contraindication to HNS? A: Complete concentric collapse at the velum
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.pimp7: Q: In systems with a separate respiratory sensing lead (Inspire IV and earlier), where is the lead placed relative to the intercostal muscles? A: Between the external and internal intercostal muscles (Inspire V has no sensing lead)
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.pimp8: Q: Why work near the upper border of the lower rib? A: The intercostal vein, artery and nerve run in the costal groove under the rib above
A-hypoglossal-stimulator.pimp9: Q: Which nerve fibers to geniohyoid travel with XII? A: C1 fibers
OR-hypoglossal-stimulator.step1: Expose the submandibular upper neck and identify the hypoglossal nerve distal to the digastric tendon while protecting marginal mandibular and lingual structures.
OR-hypoglossal-stimulator.step2: Use stimulation to distinguish protrusor branches from retrusor branches and select the inclusion branches that produce tongue protrusion without retraction.
OR-hypoglossal-stimulator.step3: Place the stimulation cuff around the selected hypoglossal branches without nerve compression or inclusion of undesired branches.
OR-hypoglossal-stimulator.step4: Create the chest generator pocket and expose the planned intercostal space for the respiratory-sensing lead.
OR-hypoglossal-stimulator.step5: Place the sensing lead in the correct intercostal plane and secure it while avoiding pleural entry.
OR-hypoglossal-stimulator.step6: Tunnel and connect stimulation and sensing leads to the generator with strain relief and no sharp kinks.
OR-hypoglossal-stimulator.step7: Test the system for respiratory sensing and symmetric tongue protrusion, revising cuff/lead position if activation is retrusive or asymmetric.
OR-hypoglossal-stimulator.step8: Obtain hemostasis, close neck/chest incisions without compressing hardware, and document delayed activation/titration plan.

## Lingual Tonsillectomy / Tongue-Base Reduction  (slug: lingual-tonsillectomy, region: Sleep Surgery)

A-lingual-tonsillectomy.summary: Lingual tonsillectomy and tongue-base reduction enlarge the retrolingual airway by removing lymphoid tissue or muscle from the posterior tongue. The lingual tonsil is a superficial layer on the tongue base without a true capsule; the lingual artery and hypoglossal nerve lie deep and lateral, so the midline and superficial planes are safest.
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.layer1: Exposure — teeth; oral tongue; circumvallate papillae; sulcus terminalis. Use a mouth gag or laryngoscope, or TORS/coblation with angled telescopes. Pull the tongue forward to show the base.
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.layer2: Lingual tonsil — lymphoid tissue on the tongue base; foramen cecum (midline apex of sulcus terminalis); vallecula (inferior). The lymphoid layer sits on the tongue muscle behind the circumvallate papillae and extends to the vallecula.
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.layer3: Deep plane — intrinsic and genioglossus muscle; dorsal lingual artery branches. Remove lymphoid tissue down to muscle; for tongue-base reduction, remove muscle in the midline.
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.layer4: Lateral limits — lateral tongue base; glossotonsillar sulcus; lingual artery and hypoglossal nerve deep and lateral. Stay medial and superficial; lateral and deep dissection approaches the neurovascular bundle.
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.layer5: Inferior limit — vallecula; epiglottis; glossoepiglottic fold. Avoid injuring the epiglottis and vallecula to prevent scarring and supraglottic stenosis.
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.landmark1: Circumvallate papillae — V-shaped row of large papillae in front of the sulcus terminalis (Anterior limit of the lingual tonsil)
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.landmark2: Foramen cecum — Midline pit at the apex of the sulcus terminalis (Origin of thyroid descent; lingual thyroid sits here)
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.landmark3: Vallecula — Pockets between the tongue base and epiglottis, separated by the median glossoepiglottic fold (Inferior limit)
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.landmark4: Lingual artery — Doppler or deep lateral pulsation; in TORS found deep and lateral at the tongue base (Major hemorrhage risk)
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.danger1: Lingual artery — where: Deep and lateral in the tongue base; protect: Stay medial and superficial; use Doppler when needed; injury: Major hemorrhage
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.danger2: Hypoglossal nerve — where: Lateral to hyoglossus, deep and lateral to the tongue base; protect: Avoid deep lateral dissection; injury: Tongue weakness
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.danger3: Glossopharyngeal nerve (lingual branches) — where: Lateral tongue base and posterior tongue; protect: Avoid deep lateral thermal injury; injury: Taste and sensory change
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.danger4: Epiglottis and vallecula — where: Inferior limit; protect: Stay above the vallecula; injury: Scarring, dysphagia, supraglottic stenosis
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.danger5: Airway — where: Tongue base edema; protect: Steroids; monitor; injury: Airway obstruction
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.variant1: Lingual thyroid at the foramen cecum (may be the only thyroid tissue)
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.variant2: Thyroglossal duct remnant
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.variant3: Large lingual tonsil after palatine tonsillectomy
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.supply1: Arterial supply — Lingual artery (dorsal lingual branches) and tonsillar branches
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.supply2: Sensory — Glossopharyngeal nerve (posterior third) and internal branch of SLN near the vallecula
A-lingual-tonsillectomy.supply3: Motor — Hypoglossal nerve

## Maxillomandibular Advancement  (slug: mma, region: Sleep Surgery)

A-mma.summary: MMA moves the maxilla (Le Fort I) and mandible (bilateral sagittal split osteotomy) forward together, expanding the whole velo-orohypopharyngeal airway by pulling the soft palate, tongue base and suprahyoid muscles with the bones. Safety depends on keeping the inferior alveolar nerve with the tooth-bearing segment in the split, protecting the descending palatine and internal maxillary arteries at the pterygomaxillary junction, and fixing the jaws in the planned occlusion with the condyles seated.
A-mma.layer1: Le Fort I exposure — maxillary vestibule mucosa above the mucogingival junction; periosteum of the anterior maxilla; piriform aperture and nasal mucosa; infraorbital nerves superiorly; zygomaticomaxillary buttresses; anterior nasal spine. Incise in the vestibule from first molar to first molar, leaving a mucosal cuff. Elevate subperiosteally to the piriform rims, keeping below the infraorbital foramina, and back to the pterygomaxillary junction through a tunnel. Elevate nasal floor and lateral nasal wall mucosa.
A-mma.layer2: Le Fort I osteotomy — lateral maxillary wall; medial maxillary (lateral nasal) wall; nasal septum at the vomer; pterygomaxillary junction; descending palatine artery in the posterior medial wall. Cut horizontally from the piriform rim backward, several millimeters above the root apices (canine roots are longest). Separate the septum from the maxilla and the lateral nasal walls. Separate the pterygoid plates from the tuberosity with a curved osteotome placed low and medially at the pterygomaxillary junction. Down-fracture and either preserve or ligate the descending palatine arteries.
A-mma.layer3: Sagittal split exposure — incision along the anterior ramus and external oblique ridge; temporalis attachment on the coronoid; medial ramus periosteum; lingula and mandibular foramen; lateral mandibular body. Incise lateral to the retromolar area along the external oblique ridge. Elevate the medial ramus just above the lingula to find the inferior alveolar nerve entering the mandibular foramen; protect it with a retractor. Elevate the lateral body down to the inferior border for the vertical cut, keeping the masseter attachment largely intact.
A-mma.layer4: Osteotomy cuts — horizontal medial cut above the lingula; sagittal cut along the anterior ramus and external oblique ridge; vertical buccal cut near the first or second molar to the inferior border; inferior alveolar canal in between. The medial cut goes through the lingual cortex only, above and behind the lingula. The sagittal and buccal cuts go through the buccal cortex only. The canal lies between the cortices, generally closer to the lingual cortex in the ramus and posterior body and moving buccally as it runs forward toward the mental foramen; its position varies between patients, so check it on the planning CT.
A-mma.layer5: Split — proximal (condyle-bearing) segment; distal (tooth-bearing) segment; inferior alveolar neurovascular bundle. Spread gradually with splitters and osteotomes directed along the inner buccal cortex. The nerve should stay in the distal segment; free it gently if it stays in the proximal segment.
A-mma.layer6: Advancement and fixation — intermediate and final occlusal splints; maxillomandibular fixation; plates at the piriform and zygomaticomaxillary buttresses; bicortical screws or plates on the mandible. Set the occlusion in the splint with temporary MMF. Fix the maxilla at the piriform and ZM buttresses. Seat the condyles in the glenoid fossae before fixing the mandible; avoid compressing the nerve between segments with lag screws.
A-mma.layer7: Closure — alar base cinch; V-Y vestibular closure; mandibular incisions. An alar cinch limits alar widening. Close the vestibule with V-Y to preserve upper lip length.
A-mma.landmark1: Lingula and mandibular foramen — On the medial ramus, about midway between the anterior and posterior borders and roughly at the level of the occlusal plane or a little above it; feel the bony spur (lingula) with the elevator (Medial cut lies above and behind it; the nerve enters just below and behind the lingula)
A-mma.landmark2: Pterygomaxillary junction — Follow the lateral maxillary wall back to the tuberosity, where the pterygoid plates attach; place the curved osteotome at the lower end, aimed medially and down (Separating here frees the maxilla; the maxillary artery lies higher up in the fissure)
A-mma.landmark3: Descending palatine artery — In the posterior medial wall of the maxillary sinus near the junction with the perpendicular plate of the palatine bone; seen after down-fracture (Preserve or ligate to control bleeding and palatal blood supply)
A-mma.landmark4: Infraorbital foramen — Midpupillary line about 6-10 mm below the infraorbital rim (individual range wider) (Superior limit of Le Fort I exposure)
A-mma.landmark5: External oblique ridge — Lateral ridge from the anterior ramus down to the body lateral to the molars (Guide for the sagittal cut and incision)
A-mma.landmark6: Root apices — Estimate from panoramic or CBCT root lengths; the canine eminence marks the longest maxillary root (Le Fort I cut must stay above them)
A-mma.danger1: Inferior alveolar nerve — where: Enters the mandibular foramen below the lingula and runs in the canal between the cortices of the ramus and body; protect: Protect medially with a retractor, keep cuts monocortical, split along the buccal cortex, free the nerve into the distal segment; injury: Lower lip and chin numbness (common, often temporary)
A-mma.danger2: Internal maxillary artery and pterygoid venous plexus — where: Above the pterygomaxillary junction in the pterygopalatine and infratemporal fossae; protect: Place the osteotome low at the junction, direct it medially and down, control force; injury: Severe hemorrhage
A-mma.danger3: Descending palatine artery — where: Greater palatine canal in the posterior medial maxilla; protect: Careful lateral nasal wall cuts; ligate or cauterize if injured; injury: Bleeding; rarely compromised maxillary perfusion (with other vascular injury)
A-mma.danger4: Lingual nerve — where: Medial to the ramus and the third molar region; protect: Subperiosteal medial dissection and retractor placement; injury: Tongue numbness and taste change
A-mma.danger5: Facial artery and vein — where: Crossing the inferior border at the antegonial notch, anterior to the masseter; protect: Stay subperiosteal at the inferior border; injury: Bleeding
A-mma.danger6: Condyle position — where: In the glenoid fossa; the proximal segment can rotate or sag during fixation; protect: Seat the condyle before fixation; check occlusion after MMF release; injury: Malocclusion, relapse, TMJ problems
A-mma.danger7: Nasolacrimal duct and infraorbital nerve — where: Duct opens in the inferior meatus under the inferior turbinate; nerve exits the foramen above the osteotomy; protect: Keep the lateral nasal wall cut low; do not retract hard on the nerve; injury: Epiphora; cheek and lip numbness
A-mma.variant1: Inferior alveolar canal position varies, sometimes near the buccal cortex, increasing nerve risk
A-mma.variant2: Unerupted or impacted third molars complicate the split and are often removed months before surgery
A-mma.variant3: Thin ramus or prior jaw surgery increases unfavorable split risk
A-mma.variant4: Septal deviation or large inferior turbinates may need treatment at the same time
A-mma.supply1: Maxilla after Le Fort I — Ascending palatine (facial) and palatine branch of the ascending pharyngeal artery through the soft palate, and buccal soft tissue pedicle; descending palatine arteries if preserved
A-mma.supply2: Mandible — Inferior alveolar artery (maxillary) in the canal; periosteal and muscular supply from masseter and medial pterygoid
A-mma.supply3: Sensory — Infraorbital (V2) to cheek and upper lip; inferior alveolar and mental nerves (V3) to the lower lip and chin; lingual nerve (V3) to the tongue
A-mma.supply4: Pterygomasseteric sling — Masseter laterally and medial pterygoid medially support the proximal segment
A-mma.pimp1: Q: What structure is the landmark for the medial horizontal cut in a sagittal split? A: The lingula, with the cut placed above and behind it
A-mma.pimp2: Q: Which segment should hold the inferior alveolar nerve after the split? A: The distal (tooth-bearing) segment
A-mma.pimp3: Q: Which artery is at risk in the pterygomaxillary fissure during Le Fort I disjunction? A: The internal maxillary artery (with the pterygoid plexus); the descending palatine artery is at risk along the medial wall
A-mma.pimp4: Q: What maintains blood supply to the maxilla after down-fracture? A: Soft palate vessels (ascending palatine and ascending pharyngeal) and the buccal soft tissue pedicle
A-mma.pimp5: Q: What is the classic amount of advancement in MMA for OSA? A: About 10 mm or more
A-mma.pimp6: Q: Which nerve supplies sensation to the lower lip and chin? A: Mental nerve, the terminal branch of the inferior alveolar nerve (V3)
A-mma.pimp7: Q: Which muscles form the pterygomasseteric sling? A: Masseter and medial pterygoid
A-mma.pimp8: Q: What does counterclockwise rotation of the maxillomandibular complex do? A: Increases chin and tongue base advancement for a given maxillary movement
A-mma.pimp9: Q: Where is the facial artery at risk on the mandible? A: At the antegonial notch, anterior to the masseter insertion
OR-mma.step1: Perform Le Fort I osteotomy of the maxilla and bilateral sagittal split osteotomy of the mandible.
OR-mma.step2: Advance both the maxilla and mandible (commonly 10 mm or more) to enlarge the retropalatal and retrolingual airway, and secure a planned occlusion.
OR-mma.step3: Achieve rigid fixation and confirm a stable, planned bite before closing.

## Reconstructive Palatal Surgery for OSA  (slug: reconstructive-palate, region: Sleep Surgery)

A-reconstructive-palate.summary: Modern palatal surgery for OSA (expansion sphincter pharyngoplasty, lateral pharyngoplasty and related repositioning techniques) widens the retropalatal airway by moving the palatopharyngeus and lateral pharyngeal wall forward and outward rather than removing large amounts of palate. Safety depends on working in the tonsillar fossa without going through the superior constrictor toward the carotid and glossopharyngeal nerve, and on preserving enough soft palate length and muscle to keep velopharyngeal closure.
A-reconstructive-palate.layer1: Oral exposure and survey — anterior pillar (palatoglossus); posterior pillar (palatopharyngeus); tonsils; uvula and soft palate; pterygomandibular raphe; hamulus of the medial pterygoid plate. With a mouth gag in place, palpate the hamulus just posteromedial to the maxillary tuberosity and the pterygomandibular raphe running from it to the mandible. These mark the lateral edge of the soft palate where the muscle will be suspended.
A-reconstructive-palate.layer2: Tonsillectomy — tonsil capsule; loose areolar plane; superior constrictor (tonsil bed); tonsillar branch of the facial artery (inferior pole). Dissect in the capsular plane, leaving superior constrictor fibers intact. The main arterial supply enters at the inferior pole; control it before moving on.
A-reconstructive-palate.layer3: Isolate the palatopharyngeus — posterior pillar mucosa; palatopharyngeus muscle; superior constrictor deep to it. Identify the vertical palatopharyngeus fibers in the posterior pillar and separate them from the constrictor behind. In expansion sphincter pharyngoplasty the muscle is divided inferiorly, keeping its superior attachment and nerve and blood supply.
A-reconstructive-palate.layer4: Superolateral rotation and suspension — submucosal tunnel in the soft palate; arching fibers of the palate near the hamulus and pterygomandibular raphe. Rotate the muscle flap superolaterally and fix it through a tunnel to the palatal tissues near the hamulus. This pulls the lateral wall forward and outward and opens the retropalatal space.
A-reconstructive-palate.layer5: Lateral pharyngeal wall and constrictor (lateral pharyngoplasty) — superior constrictor; parapharyngeal fat (lateral to the constrictor); pharyngobasilar fascia. Some techniques divide part of the superior constrictor and suture it laterally. Lateral to the constrictor are the parapharyngeal space contents, including the ICA; do not dissect deep into this space.
A-reconstructive-palate.layer6: Uvula and soft palate — musculus uvulae; levator veli palatini sling; palatal mucosa. Trim the uvula conservatively if at all. The levator sling in the middle of the soft palate drives velopharyngeal closure and should not be divided.
A-reconstructive-palate.layer7: Mucosal closure and inspection — anterior and posterior pillar mucosa; oropharyngeal inlet. Close mucosa over the repositioned muscle without tension. Inspect the airway, symmetry and hemostasis after releasing the gag.
A-reconstructive-palate.landmark1: Pterygoid hamulus — Palpate just posterior and medial to the maxillary tuberosity on the lateral soft palate as a firm bony point (Anchor region for palatal suspension; the tensor veli palatini tendon turns around it)
A-reconstructive-palate.landmark2: Palatopharyngeus — Within the posterior pillar, vertical fibers that move with the pillar; separate from the horizontal superior constrictor fibers behind (The muscle flap that is repositioned)
A-reconstructive-palate.landmark3: Superior constrictor (tonsil bed) — The horizontal muscle fibers seen in the bed after the tonsil is removed (Boundary between the operation and the parapharyngeal space)
A-reconstructive-palate.landmark4: Pterygomandibular raphe — Palpable band from the hamulus down to the posterior mylohyoid line of the mandible, lateral to the anterior pillar (Joins buccinator and superior constrictor; lateral reference for suspension sutures)
A-reconstructive-palate.landmark5: Inferior pole vessels — At the lower tonsil pole near the base of the tongue (Main bleeding site; tonsillar branch of the facial artery enters here)
A-reconstructive-palate.danger1: Internal carotid artery — where: In the parapharyngeal space posterolateral to the tonsillar fossa, usually more than 1-2 cm away; closer when tortuous or medialized; protect: Stay medial to the superior constrictor; palpate for a pulsatile lateral pharyngeal wall before surgery; injury: Catastrophic hemorrhage
A-reconstructive-palate.danger2: Glossopharyngeal nerve — where: Deep to the inferior tonsil bed, near the stylopharyngeus and constrictor; protect: Avoid deep dissection or deep sutures in the inferior bed; injury: Loss of taste and sensation on the posterior tongue, dysphagia, referred otalgia
A-reconstructive-palate.danger3: Velopharyngeal sphincter — where: Levator sling in the soft palate and the lateral walls; protect: Preserve palate length and levator; avoid over-resection of the uvula and palate; injury: Velopharyngeal insufficiency with nasal regurgitation and hypernasal speech
A-reconstructive-palate.danger4: Nasopharyngeal inlet — where: Posterior pillars and posterior pharyngeal wall; protect: Avoid removing mucosa all around the inlet; injury: Nasopharyngeal stenosis
A-reconstructive-palate.danger5: Tonsillar arteries — where: Inferior pole (tonsillar branch of facial) and upper pole (ascending pharyngeal and lesser palatine branches); protect: Careful hemostasis; avoid deep cautery; injury: Primary or secondary hemorrhage
A-reconstructive-palate.variant1: Tortuous or medialized ICA can lie just behind the tonsillar fossa
A-reconstructive-palate.variant2: Large tonsils versus small tonsils change how much lateral wall can be moved
A-reconstructive-palate.variant3: Thick, long or low-hanging soft palate (high Friedman palate position) limits the benefit of palatal surgery alone
A-reconstructive-palate.variant4: Submucous cleft palate (bifid uvula, notched hard palate, zona pellucida) raises the risk of VPI
A-reconstructive-palate.supply1: Tonsil arterial supply — Tonsillar branch of the facial artery (main), ascending palatine (facial), dorsal lingual (lingual), ascending pharyngeal (ECA), lesser palatine (descending palatine of the maxillary)
A-reconstructive-palate.supply2: Palate arterial supply — Greater and lesser palatine arteries, ascending palatine artery and palatine branch of the ascending pharyngeal artery
A-reconstructive-palate.supply3: Motor — All palatal muscles by the pharyngeal plexus (CN X) except tensor veli palatini (V3)
A-reconstructive-palate.supply4: Sensory — Lesser palatine nerves (V2) for the soft palate; glossopharyngeal nerve for the tonsil and oropharynx
A-reconstructive-palate.pimp1: Q: Which palatal muscle is not supplied by the pharyngeal plexus? A: Tensor veli palatini, supplied by V3
A-reconstructive-palate.pimp2: Q: What muscle forms the posterior tonsillar pillar? A: Palatopharyngeus
A-reconstructive-palate.pimp3: Q: What muscle forms the anterior pillar? A: Palatoglossus
A-reconstructive-palate.pimp4: Q: What forms the bed of the tonsillar fossa? A: The superior constrictor muscle
A-reconstructive-palate.pimp5: Q: What is the main arterial supply of the tonsil? A: Tonsillar branch of the facial artery
A-reconstructive-palate.pimp6: Q: Which nerve can be injured deep to the inferior tonsil bed? A: Glossopharyngeal nerve
A-reconstructive-palate.pimp7: Q: Around which structure does the tensor veli palatini tendon turn? A: The pterygoid hamulus
A-reconstructive-palate.pimp8: Q: Which muscle is the main elevator of the velum? A: Levator veli palatini
A-reconstructive-palate.pimp9: Q: Which two muscles meet at the pterygomandibular raphe? A: Buccinator and superior constrictor
OR-reconstructive-palate.step1: Confirm the level and pattern of retropalatal collapse and choose the planned expansion/lateral-pharyngeal reconstructive technique before incision.
OR-reconstructive-palate.step2: Expose the soft palate/tonsillar fossae and identify palatopharyngeus, palatoglossus, superior constrictor, uvula, and neurovascular tissue to be preserved.
OR-reconstructive-palate.step3: Perform tonsillectomy first when part of the operation, maintaining a clean capsular plane and controlling bleeding before palatal reconstruction.
OR-reconstructive-palate.step4: Mobilize the selected palatopharyngeus or lateral pharyngeal tissue from restrictive attachments without denervating or devascularizing the flap.
OR-reconstructive-palate.step5: Reposition/suspend the muscle anterolaterally to enlarge the retropalatal airway rather than simply resecting central palate tissue.
OR-reconstructive-palate.step6: Close mucosal flaps over the reconstructed muscle with symmetric tension and avoid excessive shortening that would impair velopharyngeal function/swallowing.
OR-reconstructive-palate.step7: Inspect the resulting airway, uvular/palatal symmetry, and hemostasis with suspension released.
OR-reconstructive-palate.step8: Plan monitored postoperative airway care and specifically track hemorrhage, dysphagia, velopharyngeal insufficiency, and residual OSA.

## Deep Neck Abscess Drainage  (slug: deep-neck-drain, region: General ENT / Emergencies)

A-deep-neck-drain.summary: Deep neck infections spread within compartments defined by the layers of the deep cervical fascia, and the danger is spread along continuous spaces to the mediastinum or into the carotid sheath. Drainage needs a route that reaches the collection while defining the carotid sheath first: transoral for medial parapharyngeal/retropharyngeal collections that bulge into the pharynx, and transcervical (along the anterior SCM border) for collections lateral to the great vessels or extending low.
A-deep-neck-drain.layer1: Airway — oropharynx and supraglottis (edema, trismus, deviation); cricothyroid membrane. Assess for awake fiberoptic intubation or awake tracheostomy. Trismus from pterygoid involvement may not relax with induction.
A-deep-neck-drain.layer2: Incision and platysma — skin along anterior SCM border or over the collection; platysma; EJV and great auricular nerve. Transcervical approach: incise along or parallel to the anterior border of SCM at the level of the collection (often near the hyoid).
A-deep-neck-drain.layer3: Superficial layer of deep cervical fascia — investing fascia; SCM (retract posterolaterally); submandibular gland (for submandibular-space collections). Open the investing fascia along SCM and retract the muscle laterally.
A-deep-neck-drain.layer4: Carotid sheath — IJV; common/internal carotid; vagus; ansa cervicalis on the sheath. Identify the sheath before entering the collection. The sheath is a 'Lincoln highway' running from the skull base to the mediastinum and contacts all three fascial layers.
A-deep-neck-drain.layer5: Parapharyngeal space entry — posterior belly of digastric and stylohyoid (superior); styloid process; prestyloid (anterior) compartment; poststyloid (posterior) compartment with ICA, IJV, IX-XII and sympathetic chain. Bluntly follow a finger medially and superiorly toward the styloid, anterior or posterior to the sheath depending on the collection.
A-deep-neck-drain.layer6: Retropharyngeal and danger spaces — buccopharyngeal fascia (visceral layer, front); alar fascia; prevertebral fascia. Retropharyngeal space lies between the visceral (buccopharyngeal) fascia and alar fascia, from the skull base into the upper mediastinum, ending where the alar and visceral fascia fuse (otolaryngology texts commonly cite T1-T2; other sources place the fusion as low as ~T4-T6). The danger space is between alar and prevertebral fascia and runs to the diaphragm.
A-deep-neck-drain.layer7: Visceral (pretracheal) space and lower neck — thyroid; trachea; esophagus; strap muscles. Anterior visceral space infection (e.g., from esophageal perforation or thyroid) descends to the anterior mediastinum (about T4 / pericardium).
A-deep-neck-drain.layer8: Drainage — dependent drain path; source control (dental, tonsil, salivary, foreign body). Break loculations bluntly, irrigate, and place drains. Extend to cervical or thoracic drainage if imaging shows mediastinal extension.
A-deep-neck-drain.landmark1: Carotid sheath — Retract SCM laterally; palpate carotid pulse; IJV lies lateral, carotid medial, vagus posterior between (Must be defined before blunt entry into lateral collections)
A-deep-neck-drain.landmark2: Parapharyngeal space — Follow the posterior belly of digastric toward the mastoid and push a finger medially toward the styloid process, above the hyoid and under the angle of the mandible (Transcervical access to lateral pharyngeal collections)
A-deep-neck-drain.landmark3: Retropharyngeal space (cervical access) — Retract the carotid sheath laterally and the larynx/pharynx medially, then pass a finger behind the pharynx anterior to the prevertebral fascia (Drains retropharyngeal collections extending below the oropharynx)
A-deep-neck-drain.landmark4: Retropharyngeal space (transoral) — Posterior pharyngeal wall bulge, usually off midline; incise vertically on the bulge after needle aspiration (Retropharyngeal collections lie medial to the carotid, so the posterior pharyngeal wall is the safe entry)
A-deep-neck-drain.landmark5: Submandibular space — Below the mylohyoid (submaxillary compartment) and above it (sublingual); a horizontal incision 2 fingerbreadths below the mandible enters the submandibular triangle (Ludwig angina involves bilateral submandibular, sublingual and submental spaces)
A-deep-neck-drain.danger1: Carotid artery / IJV — where: Lateral wall of parapharyngeal and retropharyngeal collections; can be eroded (pseudoaneurysm) or thrombosed (Lemierre); protect: Define the sheath before entry; blunt finger dissection; no blind clamping; injury: Hemorrhage, carotid rupture
A-deep-neck-drain.danger2: Lower cranial nerves and sympathetic chain — where: Poststyloid parapharyngeal space and carotid sheath; protect: Blunt dissection; avoid cautery in the poststyloid compartment; injury: Dysphagia, hoarseness, tongue weakness, Horner syndrome
A-deep-neck-drain.danger3: Marginal mandibular nerve — where: Over the facial vessels in submandibular approaches; protect: Low incision (2 fingerbreadths below the mandible), subplatysmal dissection; injury: Lower lip weakness
A-deep-neck-drain.danger4: Mediastinum via danger and visceral spaces — where: Retropharyngeal → mediastinum; danger space → posterior mediastinum to diaphragm; visceral → anterior mediastinum; protect: Image to the diaphragm; involve thoracic surgery for extension below the carina (approx T4); injury: Descending necrotizing mediastinitis
A-deep-neck-drain.danger5: Recurrent laryngeal nerve / esophagus — where: Low visceral and paratracheal collections; protect: Blunt dissection along the tracheoesophageal groove; injury: Vocal fold paralysis, perforation
A-deep-neck-drain.variant1: Medialized or tortuous ICA in the retropharyngeal region, important before transoral drainage.
A-deep-neck-drain.variant2: In children, retropharyngeal lymph nodes (of Rouvière) suppurate and typically regress by early school age, so the space is more often infected in young children.
A-deep-neck-drain.variant3: A midline raphe between buccopharyngeal and alar fascia makes pediatric retropharyngeal collections lateral of midline.
A-deep-neck-drain.variant4: Odontogenic infection from the second and third molars (roots below the mylohyoid line) passes to the submandibular space; premolar/anterior teeth (roots above) to the sublingual space; the first molar is variable.
A-deep-neck-drain.supply1: Fascial layers — Superficial (investing) layer; middle (visceral/pretracheal with buccopharyngeal) layer; deep layer (alar and prevertebral divisions); carotid sheath formed by all three
A-deep-neck-drain.supply2: Arterial at risk — Carotid artery and ECA branches (lingual, facial, ascending pharyngeal)
A-deep-neck-drain.supply3: Nerves at risk — CN IX-XII and sympathetic trunk in the poststyloid space; marginal mandibular branch; RLN low
A-deep-neck-drain.supply4: Space continuity — Parapharyngeal space communicates with retropharyngeal, submandibular, masticator, parotid and carotid spaces
A-deep-neck-drain.pimp1: Q: Between which fascial layers is the retropharyngeal space? A: Between the buccopharyngeal (visceral) fascia anteriorly and the alar fascia posteriorly
A-deep-neck-drain.pimp2: Q: Between which layers is the danger space and how far does it extend? A: Between the alar and prevertebral fascia; from the skull base to the diaphragm
A-deep-neck-drain.pimp3: Q: What structure divides the parapharyngeal space into pre- and poststyloid compartments? A: The styloid process with its fascia (tensor-vascular-styloid fascia / styloid diaphragm)
A-deep-neck-drain.pimp4: Q: What is contained in the poststyloid parapharyngeal space? A: ICA, IJV, CN IX-XII, sympathetic chain and lymph nodes
A-deep-neck-drain.pimp5: Q: Which spaces does Ludwig angina involve? A: Bilateral submandibular, sublingual and submental spaces
A-deep-neck-drain.pimp6: Q: What is Lemierre syndrome? A: Septic thrombophlebitis of the IJV after oropharyngeal infection, classically Fusobacterium necrophorum, with septic emboli
A-deep-neck-drain.pimp7: Q: Why do pediatric retropharyngeal abscesses appear off midline? A: A midline fibrous raphe (where the buccopharyngeal fascia attaches to the alar fascia) divides the space into two lateral halves, each containing lateral retropharyngeal (Rouvière) nodes
A-deep-neck-drain.pimp8: Q: Which teeth drain to the submandibular space? A: Second and third mandibular molars (roots below the mylohyoid line)
OR-deep-neck-drain.step1: Secure a potentially difficult airway before deep manipulation and review contrast imaging to map the collection relative to carotid sheath, aerodigestive tract, and mediastinum.
OR-deep-neck-drain.step2: Choose transoral versus transcervical access based on the compartment and obtain exposure that permits dependent drainage without crossing uninvolved spaces.
OR-deep-neck-drain.step3: Enter the abscess bluntly after the carotid/nerve relationships are defined; obtain cultures as purulence is encountered.
OR-deep-neck-drain.step4: Break loculations with blunt dissection and suction/irrigate the cavity, avoiding blind deep clamping toward the carotid sheath.
OR-deep-neck-drain.step5: Explore connected spaces only when imaging/operative findings show extension rather than creating unnecessary communication between fascial planes.
OR-deep-neck-drain.step6: Debride clearly nonviable tissue and address a dental/pharyngeal/salivary source when part of the same operation.
OR-deep-neck-drain.step7: Place a dependent drain for a cervical cavity when indicated and secure it away from major vessels.
OR-deep-neck-drain.step8: Confirm airway stability and define postoperative ICU, antibiotic, repeat-imaging, and mediastinal-surveillance needs.

## Esophageal Foreign-Body Endoscopy  (slug: esophageal-fb, region: General ENT / Emergencies)

A-esophageal-fb.summary: Swallowed objects lodge at the physiologic narrowings of the esophagus, most often at the cricopharyngeus in children. Safe removal depends on entering the cricopharyngeus under vision without pushing on the posterior hypopharyngeal wall, on protecting the thin, serosa-free esophageal wall from sharp edges during withdrawal, and on knowing the airway and great vessels next to each level.
A-esophageal-fb.layer1: Positioning and oral entry — teeth and lips; tongue; oropharynx; posterior pharyngeal wall. Neck flexed and head extended; protect the maxillary teeth and advance the rigid scope along the posterior pharyngeal wall.
A-esophageal-fb.layer2: Hypopharynx — epiglottis; arytenoids; piriform sinuses; postcricoid region. Pass behind the arytenoids; lift the larynx gently forward with the scope tip to open the postcricoid space.
A-esophageal-fb.layer3: Cricopharyngeus — cricopharyngeus (horizontal sphincter); Killian dehiscence (posterior, just above); esophageal inlet. The sphincter appears as a closed slit; wait for it to relax under gentle steady pressure rather than forcing. Most pediatric coins stop here or just below at the thoracic inlet.
A-esophageal-fb.layer4: Cervical and upper thoracic esophagus — esophageal wall (mucosa, submucosa, muscularis, adventitia); trachea and membranous wall (anterior); aortic arch and left main bronchus impression (left/anterior). Check the second narrowing at the arch and bronchus; objects here lie close to the trachea and aorta.
A-esophageal-fb.layer5: Object assessment and capture — object; impaction site mucosa; lumen distal to the object. Assess orientation and edges; sheathe sharp points inside the scope or trail them, and use the overtube or hood for broad sharp objects.
A-esophageal-fb.layer6: Distal esophagus and LES — lower esophageal sphincter; diaphragmatic hiatus; GEJ. Survey the rest of the esophagus when safe for second objects or underlying stricture or eosinophilic esophagitis.
A-esophageal-fb.layer7: Second look — impaction site; full esophageal circumference. Reinspect for laceration, perforation, bleeding or retained fragments.
A-esophageal-fb.landmark1: Cricopharyngeus — Closed horizontal slit behind the cricoid lamina at the lower hypopharynx; opens with gentle sustained pressure or patient relaxation (Most common lodgment site in children)
A-esophageal-fb.landmark2: Aortic arch / left main bronchus narrowing — Pulsatile indentation on the left anterolateral wall in the upper thoracic esophagus (adults roughly 22-27 cm from incisors) (Second narrowing; objects here are near the trachea and aorta)
A-esophageal-fb.landmark3: Lower esophageal sphincter — Narrowing at the diaphragmatic hiatus (adults roughly 38-40 cm from incisors), with the Z-line nearby (Third narrowing; food bolus impaction often reveals stricture or EoE)
A-esophageal-fb.landmark4: Sharp point direction — Rotate the object until the point trails or sheathe it within the scope barrel before withdrawal (Avoids lacerating the wall on the way out)
A-esophageal-fb.danger1: Posterior hypopharynx / Killian dehiscence — where: Posterior midline above the cricopharyngeus; protect: No blind force; enter under vision with the larynx lifted; injury: Perforation, retropharyngeal abscess, mediastinitis
A-esophageal-fb.danger2: Esophageal wall at the impaction site — where: Thin wall without serosa, often ulcerated; protect: Gentle grasp, sharp edges sheathed, withdraw with scope as a unit; injury: Laceration, perforation
A-esophageal-fb.danger3: Trachea — where: Anterior to the cervical and upper thoracic esophagus; soft and compressible in infants; protect: Secure airway (intubation); watch ventilation during manipulation; injury: Airway compression, dislodgement into the airway, TEF from chronic impaction
A-esophageal-fb.danger4: Aorta and great vessels — where: Left lateral to the esophagus at the arch level; protect: Avoid traumatic manipulation of chronically impacted objects at this level; image first; injury: Aortoesophageal fistula, hemorrhage
A-esophageal-fb.danger5: Recurrent laryngeal nerves — where: Tracheoesophageal grooves; protect: Recognize risk with chronic impaction and perforation; injury: Vocal fold paralysis
A-esophageal-fb.variant1: Underlying esophageal stricture (prior TE fistula/atresia repair, caustic injury)
A-esophageal-fb.variant2: Eosinophilic esophagitis causing food bolus impaction
A-esophageal-fb.variant3: Vascular ring or aberrant subclavian causing extrinsic narrowing
A-esophageal-fb.variant4: Multiple or stacked coins and magnets
A-esophageal-fb.supply1: Arterial — Inferior thyroid artery (cervical), aortic esophageal and bronchial branches (thoracic), left gastric and inferior phrenic (abdominal)
A-esophageal-fb.supply2: Venous — Inferior thyroid veins, azygos/hemiazygos, left gastric vein
A-esophageal-fb.supply3: Motor — Vagus: pharyngeal plexus/RLN to striated upper esophagus and cricopharyngeus; vagal plexus to smooth muscle distally
A-esophageal-fb.supply4: Wall layers — Mucosa, submucosa, muscularis propria (inner circular, outer longitudinal), adventitia (no serosa)
A-esophageal-fb.pimp1: Q: Where do esophageal foreign bodies lodge most often in children? A: At the cricopharyngeus / thoracic inlet
A-esophageal-fb.pimp2: Q: What are the three narrowings of the esophagus? A: Cricopharyngeus, aortic arch/left main bronchus crossing, and lower esophageal sphincter at the hiatus
A-esophageal-fb.pimp3: Q: How does a coin in the esophagus look on AP film? A: Round (en face), because the esophagus is wider side to side; a tracheal coin tends to appear edge-on on AP
A-esophageal-fb.pimp4: Q: Why does an esophageal perforation spread rapidly? A: The esophagus has no serosa, so contamination tracks into the retrovisceral space and mediastinum
A-esophageal-fb.pimp5: Q: What is the Killian dehiscence? A: Posterior weak area between the thyropharyngeus and cricopharyngeus parts of the inferior constrictor
A-esophageal-fb.pimp6: Q: What is the most dangerous radiopaque object that resembles a coin? A: A button battery (double-ring sign on AP, step-off on lateral)
A-esophageal-fb.pimp7: Q: How do you remove an open safety pin pointing upward? A: Sheathe the point within the scope or convert it so the point trails (e.g., push into the stomach to turn it) before withdrawal
OR-esophageal-fb.step1: Secure the airway when aspiration risk, sharp object, proximal impaction, or patient age requires it and choose rigid versus flexible esophagoscopy based on object/location.
OR-esophageal-fb.step2: Advance under direct visualization to the object without blindly pushing it distally or driving sharp edges into the wall.
OR-esophageal-fb.step3: Assess object orientation, impaction depth, and surrounding mucosa before selecting grasping forceps, basket, net, or protective hood.
OR-esophageal-fb.step4: Capture the safest stable edge and withdraw the object with the scope/protection as needed so sharp or broad surfaces do not shear the esophagus.
OR-esophageal-fb.step5: Re-examine the impaction site for ulceration, laceration, perforation, bleeding, or retained fragments.
OR-esophageal-fb.step6: Survey the remaining esophagus when safe, especially when ingestion history is uncertain or multiple objects are possible.
OR-esophageal-fb.step7: If perforation is suspected, stop routine feeding and escalate imaging/surgical management rather than continuing traumatic instrumentation.
OR-esophageal-fb.step8: Document object, level, mucosal injury, diet/observation plan, and return precautions for chest/neck pain, fever, dyspnea, or bleeding.

## Peritonsillar Abscess Drainage  (slug: pta-drainage, region: General ENT / Emergencies)

A-pta-drainage.summary: A peritonsillar abscess collects between the tonsil capsule and the superior constrictor, usually at the superior pole, displacing the soft palate and uvula to the opposite side. The one relationship that decides safety is depth: the ICA lies posterolateral beyond the constrictor and parapharyngeal fat, so needle and blade depth must be controlled and directed at the superior pole rather than laterally.
A-pta-drainage.layer1: Oropharyngeal survey — soft palate (bulging, edematous); uvula (deviated contralaterally); anterior tonsillar pillar (palatoglossus); tonsil (pushed inferomedially); retromolar trigone. Trismus from medial pterygoid irritation limits opening. Confirm the bulge is superior and anterior to the tonsil (classic) rather than diffuse lateral pharyngeal wall swelling (parapharyngeal).
A-pta-drainage.layer2: Anesthesia — mucosa over the superior pole; anterior pillar. Topical spray then infiltration at the site of maximal fluctuance; keep the needle shallow.
A-pta-drainage.layer3: Needle aspiration — mucosa; anterior pillar / supratonsillar fossa; abscess cavity between tonsil capsule and constrictor. Aim at the superior pole, entering roughly at the junction of a horizontal line through the base of the uvula and a vertical line along the anterior pillar. Limit needle depth (commonly cited ~1 cm, some allow up to 1.5 cm in adults; guard the needle, and be shallower in children) and keep it directed sagittally (straight back), not lateral. If dry, aspirate the middle and then inferior pole.
A-pta-drainage.layer4: Incision and drainage — mucosa; peritonsillar space; tonsil capsule (medial); superior constrictor (lateral wall). Make a shallow mucosal incision at the aspiration site and spread bluntly with a hemostat parallel to the pillar to open loculations.
A-pta-drainage.layer5: Beyond the constrictor (do not enter) — buccopharyngeal fascia; parapharyngeal fat; ICA (posterolateral); medial pterygoid (lateral). Penetrating the superior constrictor enters the parapharyngeal space. The ICA lies posterolateral, classically cited ~2-2.5 cm from the tonsil fossa in adults, but it can be under 1 cm, and in children MRI series report mean distances under 1 cm with minimums of about 2 mm; lateral and posterior needle angulation is the danger direction.
A-pta-drainage.layer6: Reassessment / quinsy tonsillectomy — tonsillar bed; tonsillar branch of facial artery (inferior pole). Persistent fever, trismus or neck swelling suggests parapharyngeal extension and calls for CT and possible transcervical drainage.
A-pta-drainage.landmark1: Aspiration point — Supratonsillar region at the intersection of a horizontal line through the base of the uvula and a vertical line along the anterior pillar (or midway between uvula base and the last maxillary molar) (Overlies the usual superior-pole collection)
A-pta-drainage.landmark2: Anterior tonsillar pillar — Mucosal fold over palatoglossus from the soft palate to the lateral tongue (The abscess bulges just behind and above it)
A-pta-drainage.landmark3: Superior constrictor — Firm muscular floor felt with blunt spread deep to the abscess cavity (Lateral limit; do not breach)
A-pta-drainage.landmark4: Uvula deviation — Uvula pushed toward the unaffected side (Supports a peritonsillar collection on the side the uvula is pushed away from (simple tonsillitis does not deviate the uvula))
A-pta-drainage.danger1: Internal carotid artery — where: Posterolateral to the tonsil fossa beyond the superior constrictor and parapharyngeal fat; may be medialized in some patients; protect: Guarded needle with limited depth; aim sagittally and superiorly; blunt spreading only; injury: Life-threatening hemorrhage
A-pta-drainage.danger2: Tonsillar and palatine vessels — where: Tonsillar branch of facial artery at the inferior pole; ascending and descending palatine arteries; protect: Shallow incision at the superior pole; injury: Bleeding
A-pta-drainage.danger3: Airway / aspiration — where: Pus released into the oropharynx; protect: Upright position, suction ready; injury: Aspiration of pus or blood
A-pta-drainage.danger4: Parapharyngeal space — where: Lateral to the constrictor; protect: Recognize medial displacement of the lateral pharyngeal wall or neck swelling; obtain CT; injury: Missed parapharyngeal abscess with spread to carotid sheath or mediastinum
A-pta-drainage.variant1: Inferior pole peritonsillar abscess (less common, harder to aspirate; may need ultrasound or CT guidance).
A-pta-drainage.variant2: Medialized or tortuous ICA near the tonsil.
A-pta-drainage.variant3: Weber glands (minor salivary glands in the supratonsillar fossa) as a proposed source.
A-pta-drainage.variant4: Bilateral peritonsillar abscess (no uvular deviation; easily missed).
A-pta-drainage.supply1: Arterial — Tonsillar branch of facial artery; ascending palatine (facial); dorsal lingual; ascending pharyngeal; descending/lesser palatine (maxillary)
A-pta-drainage.supply2: Sensory — Glossopharyngeal nerve (tonsil, referred otalgia via Jacobson nerve); lesser palatine nerves (V2) to the soft palate
A-pta-drainage.supply3: Motor — Pharyngeal plexus to palatoglossus, palatopharyngeus and superior constrictor
A-pta-drainage.supply4: Space — Peritonsillar space between the tonsil capsule (medial) and superior constrictor (lateral)
A-pta-drainage.pimp1: Q: Where is a peritonsillar abscess located anatomically? A: Between the tonsil capsule and the superior constrictor muscle, usually at the superior pole
A-pta-drainage.pimp2: Q: Why does PTA cause trismus? A: Inflammation spreads to the medial pterygoid lateral to the superior constrictor
A-pta-drainage.pimp3: Q: Why do patients with PTA have otalgia? A: Referred pain via CN IX (tympanic branch, Jacobson nerve)
A-pta-drainage.pimp4: Q: In which direction is the ICA relative to the tonsil fossa? A: Posterolateral, beyond the constrictor and parapharyngeal fat (classically ~2-2.5 cm in adults; much closer in children and with a medialized ICA)
A-pta-drainage.pimp5: Q: What are Weber glands? A: Minor salivary glands in the supratonsillar space, implicated in PTA pathogenesis
A-pta-drainage.pimp6: Q: Which muscles form the anterior and posterior tonsillar pillars? A: Palatoglossus (anterior) and palatopharyngeus (posterior)
A-pta-drainage.pimp7: Q: What finding suggests parapharyngeal rather than peritonsillar abscess? A: Medial bulge of the lateral pharyngeal wall behind the posterior pillar without soft palate bulge, plus neck swelling or torticollis
OR-pta-drainage.step1: Position with suction and airway equipment available, identify the tonsil, soft palate, uvula, and maximal bulge, and confirm that the airway is stable enough for bedside/OR drainage.
OR-pta-drainage.step2: Topically anesthetize/infiltrate the drainage site while keeping the needle depth controlled to avoid deep lateral vascular injury.
OR-pta-drainage.step3: Aspirate the superior pole region or image-defined collection and confirm pus before enlarging the opening.
OR-pta-drainage.step4: Incise the mucosa at the confirmed abscess site and bluntly spread into the cavity rather than cutting deeply laterally.
OR-pta-drainage.step5: Break loculations with blunt instrumentation and suction the cavity.
OR-pta-drainage.step6: Obtain cultures when clinically useful and irrigate selectively.
OR-pta-drainage.step7: Reassess airway, bleeding, hydration, and ability to tolerate oral intake.
OR-pta-drainage.step8: Escalate to quinsy tonsillectomy or deeper-neck evaluation when drainage is inadequate or disease pattern warrants it.

## Styloidectomy (Transoral / Transcervical)  (slug: styloidectomy, region: General ENT / Emergencies)

A-styloidectomy.summary: Styloidectomy shortens an elongated styloid process or excises a calcified stylohyoid ligament in Eagle syndrome. The styloid sits in the prestyloid–poststyloid boundary of the parapharyngeal space: the internal carotid artery and internal jugular vein lie medial and posterior, the external carotid lateral, and the facial nerve trunk exits the stylomastoid foramen just posterolateral to its base. Safety depends on staying on bone (subperiosteal) and shortening the process rather than chasing its base.
A-styloidectomy.layer1: Transoral: tonsillar fossa — palatine tonsil (or post-tonsillectomy scar); superior constrictor; styloid tip palpated in the fossa. With or without tonsillectomy, palpate the tip through the lateral wall of the fossa and incise the constrictor directly over it.
A-styloidectomy.layer2: Transoral: subperiosteal exposure — periosteum of the styloid; stylopharyngeus / styloglossus / stylohyoid attachments; glossopharyngeal nerve near the stylopharyngeus. Elevate periosteum and muscle attachments circumferentially, staying on bone so the vessels medial and posterior stay protected.
A-styloidectomy.layer3: Transcervical: incision and plane — skin and platysma (upper neck crease, 2 fingerbreadths below the mandible); marginal mandibular branch; submandibular gland; posterior belly of digastric. Raise subplatysmal flaps deep to the investing fascia to protect the marginal mandibular nerve, then retract the gland anteriorly and the digastric to reach the styloid.
A-styloidectomy.layer4: Transcervical: identify the styloid — styloid process palpated deep to the digastric/stylohyoid; external carotid artery (lateral); internal carotid artery and IJV (medial/posterior); hypoglossal nerve (inferior). Follow the stylohyoid muscle up to the process; dissect on the bone.
A-styloidectomy.layer5: Shortening — styloid fractured or rongeured at the planned length; calcified stylohyoid ligament segment. Remove the elongated portion to a length that relieves symptoms; leave the base near the stylomastoid foramen rather than dissecting toward the facial nerve.
A-styloidectomy.layer6: Closure — constrictor / mucosa (transoral); platysma and skin, drain optional (transcervical). Close in layers; check hemostasis in the parapharyngeal space.
A-styloidectomy.landmark1: Styloid tip (transoral) — Palpable as a hard point in the lateral wall of the tonsillar fossa, usually toward the upper pole; a short tip-to-fossa distance on CT predicts a palpable tip (Confirms the target and reproduces the patient's pain preoperatively)
A-styloidectomy.landmark2: Styloid process (transcervical) — Deep to the posterior belly of digastric and stylohyoid; follow the stylohyoid superiorly to its origin on the process (The stylohyoid is the guide to the bone)
A-styloidectomy.landmark3: Facial nerve trunk — Exits the stylomastoid foramen just posterolateral to the styloid base, between the styloid and mastoid; crosses superficial (lateral) to the base (Defines the superior limit of dissection)
A-styloidectomy.landmark4: Internal carotid artery — Medial and posterior to the styloid in the poststyloid compartment; palpate pulsation before deep dissection (Main hazard medial to the process)
A-styloidectomy.landmark5: External carotid artery — Lateral (superficial) to the styloid tip; the styloglossus, stylopharyngeus and styloid tip separate it from the ICA medially (Can be injured during lateral exposure)
A-styloidectomy.landmark6: Glossopharyngeal nerve — Curves around the posterior border of the stylopharyngeus to enter the pharynx and tongue base (Traction or injury causes sensory and taste deficits)
A-styloidectomy.danger1: Internal carotid artery / internal jugular vein — where: Medial and posterior to the styloid in the poststyloid parapharyngeal space; protect: Stay subperiosteal on the bone, shorten rather than resect the base, palpate pulsation, and have vascular control options available; injury: Life-threatening hemorrhage, stroke
A-styloidectomy.danger2: Facial nerve trunk — where: Stylomastoid foramen, posterolateral to the styloid base; protect: Do not dissect to the base; in the transcervical approach avoid deep retraction toward the foramen; injury: Facial weakness
A-styloidectomy.danger3: Marginal mandibular branch — where: Deep to platysma along the lower border of the mandible (transcervical); protect: Low incision and flaps deep to the investing fascia; injury: Lower lip depressor weakness
A-styloidectomy.danger4: Glossopharyngeal, hypoglossal and vagus nerves — where: Near the stylopharyngeus (IX) and in the poststyloid space (X, XII); protect: Avoid deep blind dissection medial to the process; injury: Dysphagia, taste and sensory change, tongue weakness
A-styloidectomy.danger5: Deep neck / parapharyngeal infection — where: Transoral wound communicating with the parapharyngeal space; protect: Layered closure and perioperative antibiotics per protocol; injury: Parapharyngeal abscess
A-styloidectomy.variant1: Bilateral elongation is common even when symptoms are unilateral
A-styloidectomy.variant2: Segmental calcification of the stylohyoid ligament rather than a single long process
A-styloidectomy.variant3: Medially angulated styloid toward the internal carotid artery (vascular/stylocarotid type)
A-styloidectomy.variant4: A styloid tip that is not palpable transorally, which favors a transcervical approach
A-styloidectomy.supply1: Arterial — ICA medial/posterior; ECA lateral; tonsillar branches of the facial artery in the fossa (transoral)
A-styloidectomy.supply2: Muscles on the process — Stylopharyngeus (IX), styloglossus (XII) and stylohyoid (VII)
A-styloidectomy.supply3: Nerves at risk — Facial trunk (stylomastoid foramen), glossopharyngeal (around stylopharyngeus), hypoglossal and vagus (poststyloid)
A-styloidectomy.pimp1: Q: Which three muscles attach to the styloid process and what innervates each? A: Stylopharyngeus (glossopharyngeal), styloglossus (hypoglossal), stylohyoid (facial)
A-styloidectomy.pimp2: Q: Which is the only muscle innervated by the glossopharyngeal nerve? A: Stylopharyngeus
A-styloidectomy.pimp3: Q: From which pharyngeal arch does the styloid process arise? A: The second (hyoid) arch, Reichert cartilage
A-styloidectomy.pimp4: Q: What structure exits just posterolateral to the styloid base? A: The facial nerve, through the stylomastoid foramen
A-styloidectomy.pimp5: Q: Which great vessel lies medial and posterior to the styloid? A: The internal carotid artery (with the IJV posterior)
A-styloidectomy.pimp6: Q: What styloid length is usually called elongated? A: Commonly more than about 25-30 mm; thresholds vary and many elongated styloids are asymptomatic
A-styloidectomy.pimp7: Q: What bedside test supports the diagnosis? A: Palpation of the tip in the tonsillar fossa reproducing the pain, and relief after local anesthetic infiltration at the tip
A-styloidectomy.pimp8: Q: What is the main advantage of the transcervical approach? A: Wider exposure and vascular control, at the cost of a neck scar and marginal mandibular nerve risk
OR-styloidectomy.step1: Transoral: suspend with a mouth gag, perform tonsillectomy if the tonsil is present, and palpate the styloid tip in the fossa.
OR-styloidectomy.step2: Transoral: incise the mucosa and superior constrictor directly over the tip, then strip periosteum and the stylohyoid ligament and muscle attachments circumferentially with an elevator.
OR-styloidectomy.step3: Transoral: follow the process superiorly under direct (or endoscopic/robotic) vision, resect it with a rongeur as far proximally as can be seen safely, smooth the stump, and close the mucosa.
OR-styloidectomy.step4: Transcervical: make a horizontal incision about two fingerbreadths below the mandibular angle, raise a subplatysmal flap deep to the investing fascia to protect the marginal mandibular branch, and retract the submandibular gland anteriorly and sternocleidomastoid posteriorly.
OR-styloidectomy.step5: Transcervical: identify and retract the posterior belly of digastric, palpate the styloid, and identify the ECA, ICA, IJV and hypoglossal nerve before dissecting on the bone.
OR-styloidectomy.step6: Transcervical: dissect subperiosteally along the styloid, detach the stylohyoid ligament, stylohyoid, styloglossus and stylopharyngeus, and resect with a rongeur, stopping short of the base so the facial nerve trunk is not endangered.
OR-styloidectomy.step7: Irrigate, confirm hemostasis with a Valsalva maneuver, consider a drain after cervical exposure, and close in layers.

## Tracheostomy  (slug: tracheostomy, region: General ENT / Emergencies)

A-tracheostomy.summary: A surgical tracheostomy creates an airway below the larynx through the anterior wall of the cervical trachea, usually through the second to fourth rings. Safety depends on staying in the anterior midline (the recurrent laryngeal nerves lie in the tracheoesophageal grooves and the esophagus lies behind the membranous wall), and on keeping the stoma away from the cricoid above and the innominate artery below.
A-tracheostomy.layer1: Positioning and surface landmarks — thyroid notch; cricothyroid membrane; cricoid arch; suprasternal notch; midline of the neck. A shoulder roll and controlled extension bring the trachea out of the chest. Palpate the cricoid and suprasternal notch and mark the incision about midway between them (roughly 1-2 fingerbreadths above the notch). Extension pulls more trachea, and sometimes the innominate artery, up into the neck, so palpate the notch for a pulsatile vessel.
A-tracheostomy.layer2: Skin, subcutaneous fat and platysma — skin; subcutaneous fat; platysma (thin or absent in the midline); anterior jugular veins (paired, just off midline, superficial to the strap muscles). A horizontal incision gives a better scar; a vertical midline incision is faster in an emergency and in children. The anterior jugular veins run vertically on each side of the midline; retract them laterally or ligate them, and look for a communicating jugular venous arch low in the neck.
A-tracheostomy.layer3: Investing fascia and midline raphe — superficial (investing) layer of deep cervical fascia; linea alba / midline raphe between the strap muscles; suprasternal space (of Burns) low in the midline. Find the avascular white raphe between the sternohyoid muscles and divide it vertically. Staying exactly in the midline keeps you away from the vessels and nerves that lie laterally.
A-tracheostomy.layer4: Strap muscles — sternohyoid (superficial, medial); sternothyroid (deep, attaches to the oblique line of the thyroid cartilage). Retract both strap layers laterally with equal tension so the trachea stays centered. Palpate the tracheal rings and the cricoid repeatedly; the trachea may be deviated by a goiter, mass or prior surgery.
A-tracheostomy.layer5: Pretracheal fascia and thyroid isthmus — pretracheal (visceral) layer of the middle deep cervical fascia; thyroid isthmus over rings 2-4; pyramidal lobe (superior, if present); inferior thyroid veins (descend over the trachea below the isthmus); thyroidea ima artery (if present). Use a cricoid hook to lift and stabilize the airway. Either retract the isthmus superiorly (incising the fascia along its lower edge) or divide it between clamps and suture-ligate each cut edge. The inferior thyroid veins and any thyroidea ima vessel lie on the anterior trachea below the isthmus and must be controlled before the airway is opened.
A-tracheostomy.layer6: Anterior tracheal wall — tracheal rings 2-4 (C-shaped hyaline cartilage); annular (intercartilaginous) ligaments; tracheal mucosa (respiratory epithelium). Confirm the level by counting down from the cricoid. Agree with anesthesia on reduced FiO2 and no cautery once the airway is open. Make a window, a Björk (inferiorly based) flap, or a vertical slit without cartilage removal in children. Place stay sutures on each side in children and in any patient in whom a lost tract would be dangerous.
A-tracheostomy.layer7: Lumen and posterior wall — endotracheal tube tip and cuff; posterior membranous wall (trachealis muscle); esophagus directly behind it, slightly to the left. Have anesthesia withdraw the endotracheal tube under vision until its tip is just above the stoma, but not out of the larynx. Insert the tracheostomy tube along the curve of the trachea; pushing straight back can tear the membranous wall or create a false passage into the pretracheal plane.
A-tracheostomy.landmark1: Cricoid cartilage — The firm complete ring just below the soft cricothyroid membrane and thyroid prominence; it is usually the most prominent ring in children and at about the C6 level in adults. A cricoid hook placed beneath its lower edge lifts the trachea. (It is the reference for counting rings. Opening the first ring or the cricoid risks perichondritis and subglottic stenosis.)
A-tracheostomy.landmark2: Midline raphe — The thin, pale, avascular line where the left and right sternohyoid muscles meet; its fibers run vertically and it divides without bleeding (Following it keeps the dissection off the anterior jugular veins and the lateral neck)
A-tracheostomy.landmark3: Thyroid isthmus — A soft, reddish-brown band crossing the upper trachea just below the cricoid, deep to the strap muscles and wrapped in pretracheal fascia; lift its lower edge with a hook to see the rings beneath (It covers the usual stoma site (rings 2-4) and must be retracted or divided)
A-tracheostomy.landmark4: Tracheal rings 2-4 — Count down from the lower edge of the cricoid with a finger or hook; rings feel like a regular corrugation under the pretracheal fascia (This is the target window: low enough to spare the cricoid, high enough to stay away from the innominate artery)
A-tracheostomy.landmark5: Innominate (brachiocephalic) artery — Feel for pulsation in the suprasternal notch and low on the anterior trachea before and after extension; it crosses the anterior trachea from left-inferior to right-superior near the thoracic inlet (A low stoma or a tube tip or cuff resting on it can erode into it)
A-tracheostomy.landmark6: Tracheoesophageal grooves — Not exposed in a standard tracheostomy; they are the posterolateral angles of the trachea where it meets the esophagus (The RLNs run here. Staying anterior and midline protects them.)
A-tracheostomy.danger1: Posterior tracheal wall and esophagus — where: The membranous wall lies directly behind the lumen; the esophagus is immediately posterior and slightly to the left; protect: Open the trachea with a controlled shallow incision, use a hook to lift the anterior wall, and insert the tube following the tracheal curve with the obturator in place; injury: Tracheoesophageal fistula, mediastinitis, false passage
A-tracheostomy.danger2: Innominate artery — where: Crosses the anterior trachea in the lower neck or upper mediastinum, typically around the lower cervical rings; it may ride high into the neck in children, in short necks and with extension; protect: Avoid a stoma below the 3rd-4th ring, choose a tube whose curve keeps the tip and cuff off the anterior wall, and avoid cuff overinflation; injury: Tracheoinnominate fistula; a sentinel bleed may precede massive hemorrhage
A-tracheostomy.danger3: Cricoid cartilage and first ring — where: Immediately above the usual window; protect: Count rings and open at ring 2 or below; do not let the tube flange or a high incision rest on the cricoid; injury: Perichondritis, subglottic stenosis, difficult decannulation
A-tracheostomy.danger4: Recurrent laryngeal nerves — where: In or near the tracheoesophageal grooves, lateral and posterior to the trachea; protect: Do not dissect lateral to the trachea; injury: Vocal-fold paralysis; bilateral injury causes airway obstruction above the stoma
A-tracheostomy.danger5: Pleural apices — where: Rise above the clavicle, higher in children and in patients with emphysema; protect: Stay in the midline and do not dissect low and lateral in the root of the neck; injury: Pneumothorax
A-tracheostomy.danger6: Anterior jugular veins, inferior thyroid veins and thyroid isthmus — where: Superficial and anterior to the trachea along the midline; protect: Ligate or retract the veins and suture-ligate the divided isthmus; injury: Early hemorrhage and hematoma that can compress or obscure the fresh tract
A-tracheostomy.variant1: High-riding innominate artery palpable in the suprasternal notch or seen crossing the cervical trachea
A-tracheostomy.variant2: Thyroidea ima artery ascending on the anterior trachea from the brachiocephalic trunk or aortic arch in a small minority
A-tracheostomy.variant3: Enlarged isthmus or goiter displacing or covering the trachea; tracheal deviation from mass or prior surgery
A-tracheostomy.variant4: Low-lying larynx in older patients and deep trachea in obese or short-necked patients, which may need a longer or adjustable-flange tube
A-tracheostomy.variant5: In infants the cricoid is high, the trachea is soft and easily compressed, and the pleura and innominate artery sit relatively high
A-tracheostomy.supply1: Tracheal blood supply — Segmental branches enter laterally: inferior thyroid artery branches to the upper trachea, bronchial arteries to the lower trachea
A-tracheostomy.supply2: Thyroid isthmus — Branches of the superior and inferior thyroid arteries; occasionally a thyroidea ima artery; inferior thyroid veins drain down to the brachiocephalic veins
A-tracheostomy.supply3: Superficial veins — Anterior jugular veins and the jugular venous arch in the suprasternal space
A-tracheostomy.supply4: Nerves at risk — RLNs in the tracheoesophageal grooves (motor to all intrinsic laryngeal muscles except cricothyroid)
A-tracheostomy.pimp1: Q: Which tracheal rings does the thyroid isthmus usually cover? A: Roughly the 2nd to 4th rings
A-tracheostomy.pimp2: Q: At what vertebral level is the cricoid cartilage in an adult? A: About C6
A-tracheostomy.pimp3: Q: Why avoid the first ring and cricoid? A: Higher risk of perichondritis and subglottic stenosis; the cricoid is the only complete ring
A-tracheostomy.pimp4: Q: What is the immediate maneuver for a tracheoinnominate fistula bleed? A: Overinflate the cuff; if that fails, pass a finger through the stoma and compress the artery anteriorly against the sternum (Utley maneuver), then go to the OR
A-tracheostomy.pimp5: Q: Where does a thyroidea ima artery usually arise? A: Brachiocephalic trunk most often; also the aortic arch or right common carotid
A-tracheostomy.pimp6: Q: What is a Björk flap? A: An inferiorly based flap of anterior tracheal wall sutured to the inferior skin edge to guide tube reinsertion
A-tracheostomy.pimp7: Q: Why a vertical tracheal incision without cartilage excision in children? A: Removing cartilage weakens the small, soft trachea and increases stenosis and suprastomal collapse
A-tracheostomy.pimp8: Q: What lies in the suprasternal space of Burns? A: The jugular venous arch, lymph nodes and fat between two layers of investing fascia above the manubrium
A-tracheostomy.pimp9: Q: What lies directly behind the membranous tracheal wall? A: The esophagus, slightly to the left of midline
OR-tracheostomy.step1: Position with controlled neck extension, mark the cricoid/sternal notch, and make a horizontal or vertical cervical incision over the upper trachea.
OR-tracheostomy.step2: Divide subcutaneous tissue/platysma and separate the strap muscles in the midline, keeping dissection centered on the trachea.
OR-tracheostomy.step3: Retract or divide the thyroid isthmus as needed to expose approximately the second through fourth tracheal rings, controlling thyroid vessels before airway entry.
OR-tracheostomy.step4: Communicate with anesthesia, reduce inspired oxygen when cautery is no longer needed, and confirm the intended tracheal level before opening the airway.
OR-tracheostomy.step5: Create the tracheal opening/flap without injuring the posterior wall and suction blood/secretions under direct vision.
OR-tracheostomy.step6: Insert the tracheostomy tube with the obturator, remove the obturator promptly, and connect ventilation.
OR-tracheostomy.step7: Confirm end-tidal CO2, chest rise, tube depth, and absence of a false passage; bronchoscopy may confirm position when uncertainty exists.
OR-tracheostomy.step8: Secure the flange with sutures/ties, ensure hemostasis, and document tube type/size and first-change plan.
