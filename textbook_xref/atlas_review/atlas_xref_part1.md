
## Canalplasty / Exostosis  (slug: canalplasty, region: Otology / Neurotology)

A-canalplasty.summary: Exostoses are broad-based bony growths of the medial (tympanic) canal, and osteomas are usually single and pedunculated lateral to them; both trap debris and block hearing. Canalplasty removes the bone while preserving canal skin; safety depends on the TMJ immediately anterior to the canal, the mastoid facial nerve just medial to the posterior canal wall, and the tympanic membrane at the medial end.
A-canalplasty.layer1: Approach and skin flaps — canal skin over the exostoses (very thin, under 0.5 mm); periosteum; postauricular incision and vascular strip (when used). Design laterally based skin flaps (anterior and posterior) elevated over the lesions so they can be returned; the skin over an exostosis is extremely thin and tears easily.
A-canalplasty.layer2: Lateral canal and tympanomastoid region — tympanosquamous suture (anterosuperior); tympanomastoid suture (posteroinferior); posterior canal wall. The tympanomastoid suture is a reliable landmark for the facial nerve: the mastoid segment lies medial to its deep end.
A-canalplasty.layer3: Bony lesions — anterior exostosis (often the largest); posterior exostosis; inferior and superior lesions. Drill or chisel from lateral to medial, keeping the lumen centered, and leave a thin shell of bone over the medial lesion to protect the drum until the end.
A-canalplasty.layer4: Medial canal and annulus — tympanic annulus; tympanic membrane; anterior sulcus. Find the drum early (often through a gap posteriorly or inferiorly) and keep it in view. Do not touch the drum or annulus with a spinning burr; vibration transmitted to the chain can damage the cochlea.
A-canalplasty.layer5: Anterior wall — anterior bony canal wall; TMJ capsule and condyle (anterior); glenoid fossa. Thin the anterior wall gradually; through thin bone the condyle may be felt moving when the jaw is opened. Stop if soft tissue appears.
A-canalplasty.layer6: Skin replacement — canal skin flaps; split-thickness skin or fascia grafts for bare areas. Cover exposed bone to prevent stenosis. Pack lightly to hold skin against bone.
A-canalplasty.landmark1: Tympanic membrane — Look for the drum through the gap between exostoses, usually posteriorly or inferiorly; follow the canal skin medially to the annulus (Medial limit; avoid drill contact)
A-canalplasty.landmark2: Tympanomastoid suture — Line on the posteroinferior canal wall between the tympanic bone and mastoid, palpable as a slight ridge (The mastoid facial nerve runs medial to it)
A-canalplasty.landmark3: TMJ / condyle — Anterior to the anterior canal wall; ask anesthesia or an assistant to move the jaw and watch the anterior wall move if very thin (Anterior limit)
A-canalplasty.landmark4: Annulus and anterior sulcus — Medial end of the canal skin; the anterior sulcus is often hidden by the anterior exostosis (Preserve to avoid blunting and conductive loss)
A-canalplasty.landmark5: Lateral process of malleus — First white knob seen at the superior drum once the lesion is cleared (Orientation and a reminder that the chain is close to the drill)
A-canalplasty.danger1: Mastoid facial nerve — where: Medial to the posterior canal wall, just deep to the tympanomastoid suture; at risk with deep posterior and inferior drilling; protect: Drill posteriorly only enough to widen; monitor if needed; injury: Facial paralysis
A-canalplasty.danger2: Temporomandibular joint — where: Directly anterior to the anterior canal wall; protect: Avoid deep anterior drilling; injury: TMJ exposure, pain, herniation of the joint into the canal
A-canalplasty.danger3: Tympanic membrane and ossicular chain — where: Medial end of the canal; protect: Leave a bone shell over the medial lesion until last; avoid burr contact with the malleus or drum; injury: Perforation, sensorineural hearing loss from acoustic trauma
A-canalplasty.danger4: Canal skin — where: Over the lesions; protect: Raise flaps carefully, preserve as much as possible; injury: Canal stenosis, lateralization of the drum
A-canalplasty.danger5: Chorda tympani — where: Posterosuperior sulcus; protect: Careful drilling near the posterosuperior annulus; injury: Taste disturbance
A-canalplasty.variant1: Very large anterior exostoses abutting the TMJ, often requiring a postauricular approach
A-canalplasty.variant2: Osteoma (single, pedunculated, lateral) versus exostoses (multiple, broad-based, bilateral, medial)
A-canalplasty.variant3: Thin or dehiscent tympanic bone toward the TMJ
A-canalplasty.variant4: Associated keratosis obturans or canal cholesteatoma with bony erosion
A-canalplasty.supply1: Canal arteries — Deep auricular branch of maxillary artery, posterior auricular and superficial temporal branches
A-canalplasty.supply2: Canal sensation — Auriculotemporal (V3) anterior and superior; Arnold nerve (X) posterior and inferior; facial nerve sensory fibers
A-canalplasty.supply3: Facial nerve — Mastoid segment medial to the posterior canal wall
A-canalplasty.pimp1: Q: How do exostoses differ from osteomas? A: Exostoses: multiple, bilateral, broad-based, medial (near the tympanic annulus), linked to cold-water exposure. Osteomas: usually single, unilateral, pedunculated, lateral at the tympanosquamous or tympanomastoid suture
A-canalplasty.pimp2: Q: What lies anterior to the bony canal? A: The TMJ (condyle and glenoid fossa)
A-canalplasty.pimp3: Q: Which suture is the landmark for the facial nerve exit? A: The tympanomastoid suture; the nerve lies medial to its deep end
A-canalplasty.pimp4: Q: What are the fissures of Santorini? A: Gaps in the cartilaginous canal that allow infection or tumor to spread to the parotid and infratemporal region
A-canalplasty.pimp5: Q: What is the foramen of Huschke? A: A persistent defect in the anteroinferior tympanic bone that can connect the canal to the TMJ / parotid
A-canalplasty.pimp6: Q: Approximately how long is the adult EAC? A: About 2.5 cm; lateral one-third cartilage, medial two-thirds bone
OR-canalplasty.step1: Establish circumferential canal skin exposure and elevate skin flaps so the bony lesion can be removed without sacrificing healthy canal epithelium.
OR-canalplasty.step2: Identify the tympanic membrane/annulus medially and the temporomandibular joint/anterior canal boundary before drilling.
OR-canalplasty.step3: Reduce obstructing bone with controlled drilling/chiseling directed away from the tympanic membrane and facial nerve, keeping the canal lumen centered.
OR-canalplasty.step4: Work sequentially around the canal rather than creating a deep blind trough beside the annulus.
OR-canalplasty.step5: Smooth the bony canal and ensure adequate diameter from meatus to annulus while avoiding over-thinning the anterior wall.
OR-canalplasty.step6: Replace preserved canal skin to cover exposed bone and minimize circumferential raw surfaces.
OR-canalplasty.step7: Inspect the tympanic membrane for injury and confirm hemostasis.
OR-canalplasty.step8: Pack the canal to support skin flaps without excessive pressure and close any postauricular incision.

## Cochlear Implantation  (slug: cochlear-implant, region: Otology / Neurotology)

A-cochlear-implant.summary: A cochlear implant places an electrode array into scala tympani of the basal turn, reached through a cortical mastoidectomy and the facial recess to the round window. Safety depends on opening the facial recess between the mastoid facial nerve medially and the chorda tympani laterally, and on finding the true round window membrane so the array enters scala tympani rather than scala vestibuli or a false passage.
A-cochlear-implant.layer1: Incision and flap — postauricular skin; temporoparietal fascia / galea; temporalis muscle and pericranium; mastoid periosteum (Palva flap). Keep the skin flap thick enough (local practice often targets under about 6 to 7 mm over the magnet, after any thinning) and the incision away from the device edge. In infants the mastoid tip is absent and the facial nerve exit is superficial.
A-cochlear-implant.layer2: Cortical mastoidectomy — temporal line / tegmen; sigmoid sinus; posterior canal wall (kept thin but intact); antrum. Leave a bony overhang at the cortex edge posteriorly and superiorly to hold the lead. The posterior canal wall should be thinned for a wider facial recess view without breaching it.
A-cochlear-implant.layer3: Antrum and incus — lateral semicircular canal; incus short process; fossa incudis / incus buttress. The short process points to the facial recess. The facial nerve second genu sits just medial and inferior to it.
A-cochlear-implant.layer4: Facial recess (posterior tympanotomy) — mastoid facial nerve (medial); chorda tympani (lateral); incus buttress (superior); chorda-facial angle (inferior). Drill a triangular window inferior to the incus buttress, keeping a thin bone shell over the facial nerve. Thin the posterior canal wall laterally to widen the recess rather than drilling toward the nerve.
A-cochlear-implant.layer5: Middle ear through the recess — incudostapedial joint and stapes (superior); promontory; round window niche (inferior, often under a bony overhang); pyramidal eminence. The round window niche lies about 2 mm below the stapes, facing posterolaterally and inferiorly. Drill away the bony overhang (lip) of the niche to see the true membrane.
A-cochlear-implant.layer6: Cochlear entry — round window membrane; scala tympani of the basal turn; osseous spiral lamina and basilar membrane. Insert via the membrane or a cochleostomy placed anteroinferior to the round window. The true membrane lies deep in the niche at an angle to its opening; the osseous spiral lamina begins close to its superior margin, so a superior trajectory risks injuring it.
A-cochlear-implant.layer7: Receiver-stimulator well — parietal/squamous temporal bone; temporalis muscle (anterior); dura underneath thinned bone in children. Seat the device posterosuperior to the mastoid, typically at an angle behind the auricle. In young children the skull can be very thin; drill to dura carefully and protect it.
A-cochlear-implant.landmark1: Facial recess — Triangle between the incus buttress above, the mastoid facial nerve medially and the chorda tympani laterally; start just below the fossa incudis and lateral to the line of the nerve (Corridor to the round window)
A-cochlear-implant.landmark2: Mastoid facial nerve — Runs from below the lateral canal's anterior-inferior edge down toward the stylomastoid foramen; seen as a pink line under thin bone, often with a vessel on its surface (Medial limit of the recess)
A-cochlear-implant.landmark3: Chorda tympani — Branches from the facial nerve in the mastoid segment and runs superolaterally toward the drum; it forms the lateral edge of the recess (Lateral limit; preserve it to protect taste)
A-cochlear-implant.landmark4: Round window niche — About 2 mm inferior to the stapes on the promontory, through the inferior part of the recess; it faces posterolaterally and may be hidden by its overhanging lip (Entry point to scala tympani)
A-cochlear-implant.landmark5: Stapedius tendon and pyramidal eminence — Visible in the upper recess with the stapes head; the round window lies below and slightly anterior (Orientation inside a narrow recess)
A-cochlear-implant.landmark6: Basal turn trajectory — From the round window, the basal turn runs anteriorly beneath the promontory; direct the array anteriorly along that axis, parallel to the promontory surface, not superiorly toward the osseous spiral lamina (Scala tympani placement and avoiding a basal-turn false passage)
A-cochlear-implant.danger1: Facial nerve — where: Medial wall of the facial recess; may be anteriorly or laterally displaced in malformed cochleae; protect: Monitoring; diamond burrs with irrigation parallel to the nerve; leave a bony shell; injury: Facial paresis or paralysis; heat injury from burrs
A-cochlear-implant.danger2: Chorda tympani — where: Lateral wall of the facial recess; protect: Widen by thinning the canal wall laterally, not by removing the chorda; sacrifice only if the recess is too narrow; injury: Taste disturbance
A-cochlear-implant.danger3: Posterior canal wall and tympanic membrane — where: Lateral to the facial recess; protect: Thin but do not breach; keep the drum annulus intact; injury: Canal defect, electrode extrusion into the canal, cholesteatoma
A-cochlear-implant.danger4: Cochlear microanatomy (basilar membrane, osseous spiral lamina, modiolus) — where: Immediately past the round window; protect: Slow insertion along the scala tympani axis; avoid suction of perilymph; stop at resistance; injury: Scalar translocation, loss of residual hearing, modiolar fracture
A-cochlear-implant.danger5: Dura and sigmoid sinus — where: Tegmen superiorly, sigmoid posteriorly, dura under the receiver well; protect: Careful drilling; in children, use tie-down or a subperiosteal pocket instead of a deep well; injury: CSF leak, epidural hematoma, venous bleeding
A-cochlear-implant.danger6: Internal carotid artery — where: Anteromedial to the basal turn; protect: Do not drill an anterior cochleostomy too far forward; injury: Hemorrhage
A-cochlear-implant.variant1: Inner ear malformations (incomplete partition types I to III, common cavity, cochlear hypoplasia) with abnormal facial nerve course and CSF gusher risk
A-cochlear-implant.variant2: Enlarged vestibular aqueduct with a perilymph or CSF gusher at cochleostomy
A-cochlear-implant.variant3: Cochlear ossification after meningitis, often starting in the basal turn near the round window
A-cochlear-implant.variant4: Anteriorly placed sigmoid sinus or low tegmen narrowing access
A-cochlear-implant.variant5: Narrow facial recess or aberrant facial nerve requiring an alternative approach
A-cochlear-implant.supply1: Cochlear blood supply — Labyrinthine artery (usually from AICA) dividing into common cochlear and anterior vestibular arteries
A-cochlear-implant.supply2: Mastoid facial nerve supply — Stylomastoid artery (posterior auricular)
A-cochlear-implant.supply3: Cochlear nerve — Inferior-anterior quadrant of the IAC fundus
A-cochlear-implant.supply4: Scalp flap — Posterior auricular and occipital arteries; superficial temporal artery anteriorly
A-cochlear-implant.pimp1: Q: Which scala should the electrode occupy and why? A: Scala tympani, to lie close to the spiral ganglion cells in the modiolus and away from the basilar membrane
A-cochlear-implant.pimp2: Q: What are the boundaries of the facial recess? A: Facial nerve medially, chorda tympani laterally, incus buttress superiorly
A-cochlear-implant.pimp3: Q: How many turns does the human cochlea have? A: About 2.5 to 2.75 turns
A-cochlear-implant.pimp4: Q: What lies in the anterior-inferior quadrant of the IAC fundus? A: The cochlear nerve; facial is anterior-superior, superior vestibular is posterior-superior, inferior vestibular is posterior-inferior
A-cochlear-implant.pimp5: Q: What is Bill's bar? A: The vertical crest at the IAC fundus separating the facial nerve (anterior) from the superior vestibular nerve (posterior)
A-cochlear-implant.pimp6: Q: Which malformation has the highest gusher risk and why? A: Incomplete partition type III (X-linked), because the modiolus is absent and the IAC communicates widely with the cochlea
A-cochlear-implant.pimp7: Q: Where does cochlear ossification after meningitis usually start? A: The scala tympani of the basal turn, near the round window
A-cochlear-implant.pimp8: Q: What artery supplies the cochlea? A: The labyrinthine (internal auditory) artery, usually a branch of AICA; it is an end artery
OR-cochlear-implant.step1: Plan the postauricular incision/receiver position and expose mastoid cortex while protecting soft-tissue thickness over the future receiver-stimulator.
OR-cochlear-implant.step2: Perform cortical mastoidectomy and identify tegmen, sigmoid sinus, lateral semicircular canal, incus, and facial recess landmarks.
OR-cochlear-implant.step3: Open the facial recess between facial nerve and chorda tympani to obtain a direct line of sight to the round-window niche.
OR-cochlear-implant.step4: Prepare the receiver-stimulator well/pocket and secure the device so the electrode reaches the cochlea without tension.
OR-cochlear-implant.step5: Expose the round-window membrane or create the planned cochleostomy while avoiding unnecessary trauma to the osseous spiral lamina/inner ear.
OR-cochlear-implant.step6: Insert the electrode slowly to the intended depth, managing resistance rather than forcing the array.
OR-cochlear-implant.step7: Seal the cochlear entry, coil excess lead safely in the mastoid cavity, and complete impedance/neural-response testing when used.
OR-cochlear-implant.step8: Reinspect facial-nerve/chorda integrity and hemostasis, then close the soft tissue in layers without pressure over the implant.

## Jugular Foramen Tumor Approach  (slug: jugular-foramen-tumor, region: Otology / Neurotology)

A-jugular-foramen-tumor.summary: Jugular foramen tumors (paraganglioma, schwannoma, meningioma) grow from the jugular bulb and lower cranial nerve compartment. That compartment sits between the facial nerve laterally, the vertical petrous ICA anteriorly, the labyrinth superiorly and the neck below. Safe removal needs proximal and distal control of the IJV, sigmoid sinus and ICA from the neck and mastoid. CN IX-XI run on the medial wall of the jugular bulb, so preserving that wall is how nerve function is kept.
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.layer1: Cervical exposure — SCM; posterior belly of digastric; IJV; ICA and ECA; CN X, XI, XII; occipital artery; transverse process of C1. Use a postauricular incision extended into the neck. Identify the IJV, ICA and CN X-XII below the tumor first. XI crosses the IJV at about the C1 transverse process. XII loops forward beneath the occipital artery. The ascending pharyngeal artery, the main tumor feeder, arises medially from the proximal ECA.
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.layer2: Mastoidectomy and facial nerve — mastoid facial nerve; stylomastoid foramen; digastric ridge; sigmoid sinus; facial recess and retrofacial cells. Perform a complete mastoidectomy, then skeletonize the mastoid facial segment to the stylomastoid foramen. Depending on the approach, leave the nerve in a fallopian bridge (infralabyrinthine / transjugular), or reroute it anteriorly (Fisch infratemporal type A) to expose the jugular bulb and vertical ICA.
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.layer3: Infralabyrinthine / retrofacial corridor — posterior SCC (superior limit); jugular bulb dome; retrofacial and infralabyrinthine air cells; cochlear aqueduct. Drill between the mastoid facial nerve (anterior), posterior canal (superior) and sigmoid / jugular bulb (posterior-inferior). Keep the otic capsule intact when hearing is to be preserved.
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.layer4: Venous control — sigmoid sinus (proximal); IJV (distal); condylar emissary vein. Occlude the sigmoid extraluminally (packing between bone and dura, or ligation) above the tumor. Ligate the IJV in the neck. The bulb still bleeds from the inferior petrosal sinus and condylar veins after this.
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.layer5: Jugular bulb and medial wall — lateral wall of jugular bulb; inferior petrosal sinus openings (medial wall); CN IX, X, XI (deep to the medial wall); Jacobson's nerve / inferior tympanic canaliculus. Open the lateral wall and remove intraluminal tumor. Control inferior petrosal sinus bleeding with Surgicel packing rather than suction. Leave the medial venous wall when the nerves function, because IX-XI run just deep to it.
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.layer6: Petrous ICA — caroticojugular spine; vertical petrous ICA; carotid canal periosteum; horizontal petrous ICA (Fisch C3-C4 extension). The vertical ICA lies anterior to the jugular bulb and is separated from it by the caroticojugular spine. Follow it from the neck upward into the carotid canal. Dissect tumor in a subperiosteal or subadventitial plane only with proximal control in hand.
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.layer7: Intradural / posterior fossa extension — posterior fossa dura; lower cranial nerve rootlets; PICA; vertebral artery (inferomedial); hypoglossal canal. Intradural tumor is approached through the presigmoid / retrosigmoid dura. The hypoglossal canal lies inferomedial to the jugular foramen above the occipital condyle.
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.layer8: Reconstruction — Eustachian tube; EAC (blind-sac closure in a type A approach); abdominal fat; temporalis / SCM rotation flaps. Obliterate the Eustachian tube and air cells, close the dura watertight, and fill dead space with fat and vascularized muscle.
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.landmark1: IJV and CN XI in the upper neck — Palpate the C1 transverse process between the mastoid tip and the mandibular angle. XI crosses the IJV here, usually superficial (lateral / anterior) to it (about 80% in cadaveric series), deep (medial / posterior) to it in about 20%, and rarely through a split vein. (Distal venous control and nerve preservation start here)
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.landmark2: Cervical ICA — Posteromedial to the ECA and medial to the IJV, deep to the posterior belly of digastric and the styloid. Follow it superiorly; it has no branches in the neck. (Proximal arterial control before the tumor is mobilized)
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.landmark3: CN X, XII, IX in the neck — X lies in the carotid sheath between and posterior to the ICA and IJV. XII emerges between them, passes lateral to the ICA and ECA and loops under the occipital artery. IX passes forward between ICA and ECA deep to the styloid with stylopharyngeus. (Tracing nerves from the neck upward identifies them before they enter the tumor)
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.landmark4: Facial nerve at the stylomastoid foramen — At the anterior end of the digastric ridge, medial to the tympanomastoid suture (Must be skeletonized or rerouted before working at the bulb)
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.landmark5: Jugular bulb — Inferior to the posterior canal and medial to the mastoid facial nerve, reached through retrofacial / infralabyrinthine cells. It is continuous with the sigmoid above. (Center of the tumor in glomus jugulare)
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.landmark6: Caroticojugular spine — Bony crest between the jugular fossa and carotid canal on the skull-base undersurface. The inferior tympanic canaliculus (Jacobson's nerve) is on it. (Separates the jugular bulb from the vertical ICA; erosion indicates carotid involvement)
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.danger1: Internal carotid artery — where: Vertical petrous segment directly anterior to the jugular bulb across the caroticojugular spine; the cervical ICA medial to the IJV; protect: Preoperative angiography, embolization and balloon test occlusion when encasement is expected. Proximal cervical control before tumor work, and a subperiosteal or subadventitial plane.; injury: Exsanguinating hemorrhage, stroke
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.danger2: Jugular bulb, inferior petrosal sinus and condylar veins — where: Medial bulb wall openings, between IX and X; protect: Proximal sigmoid occlusion and distal IJV ligation, then Surgicel packing of the IPS openings. Avoid blind suction or deep packing that compresses the nerves.; injury: Brisk venous bleeding, air embolism, nerve compression from overpacking
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.danger3: CN IX-XI (and XII via the hypoglossal canal) — where: Deep to the medial wall of the jugular bulb (Rhoton's intrajugular, or neural, compartment); XII separately, inferomedial in the hypoglossal canal; protect: Preserve the medial venous wall when nerves function, monitor X and XII, and leave adherent tumor when needed; injury: Dysphagia, aspiration, vocal fold paralysis, shoulder drop, tongue weakness. Acute combined deficits are poorly compensated, especially in elderly patients.
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.danger4: Facial nerve — where: Mastoid segment and stylomastoid foramen, lateral to the bulb; protect: Fallopian bridge where possible. If rerouting, handle with its soft-tissue cuff at the stylomastoid foramen.; injury: Weakness, frequently temporary after anterior rerouting; permanent paralysis if injured
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.danger5: Vertebral artery — where: Posteroinferior, near the C1 transverse foramen and occipital condyle in far-lateral extensions; protect: Identify it in the suboccipital triangle before condylar drilling; injury: Hemorrhage, posterior circulation stroke
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.danger6: Dura / CSF — where: Posterior fossa dura medial to the sigmoid and bulb; intradural tumor; protect: Watertight closure, Eustachian tube obliteration, and fat plus vascularized flap; injury: CSF leak, meningitis
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.variant1: Dominant ipsilateral sigmoid sinus or hypoplastic/absent contralateral transverse sinus, which makes sigmoid sacrifice dangerous
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.variant2: High or dehiscent jugular bulb
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.variant3: CN XI passing medial (deep) to the IJV
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.variant4: Large condylar emissary vein, or inferior petrosal sinus draining by multiple channels
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.variant5: Multicentric paragangliomas (carotid body, vagal, contralateral), particularly in SDHx mutation carriers
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.supply1: Tumor supply (paraganglioma) — Ascending pharyngeal artery (inferior tympanic branch) chiefly; also stylomastoid (posterior auricular/occipital), caroticotympanic (ICA), and middle meningeal branches
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.supply2: Venous — Sigmoid sinus and inferior petrosal sinus to the jugular bulb to the IJV; condylar emissary veins
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.supply3: Jugular foramen nerves — IX with Jacobson's nerve; X with Arnold's nerve; XI. XII is separate in the hypoglossal canal.
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.supply4: Sympathetics — Superior cervical ganglion posteromedial to the ICA at C2 (Horner if injured)
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.pimp1: Q: Classic compartments of the jugular foramen and their contents? A: Classic two-part scheme: pars nervosa (anteromedial) with IX, Jacobson's nerve and the inferior petrosal sinus; pars vascularis (posterolateral) with X, XI, Arnold's nerve and the jugular bulb. Rhoton's three-part scheme: petrosal (IPS), sigmoid (bulb) and intrajugular (IX, X, XI) compartments.
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.pimp2: Q: Main arterial supply of a glomus jugulare? A: Ascending pharyngeal artery (inferior tympanic branch)
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.pimp3: Q: Where do Jacobson's and Arnold's nerves travel? A: Jacobson's (IX tympanic branch) in the inferior tympanic canaliculus. Arnold's (X auricular branch) in the mastoid canaliculus.
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.pimp4: Q: Name the syndromes: IX-XI; IX-XII; IX-XII plus sympathetics. A: Vernet; Collet-Sicard; Villaret
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.pimp5: Q: Key maneuver and cost of the Fisch infratemporal type A approach? A: Anterior rerouting of the facial nerve; cost is conductive hearing loss (blind-sac EAC closure) and risk of facial weakness
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.pimp6: Q: Why does the bulb keep bleeding after sigmoid and IJV ligation? A: Inflow from the inferior petrosal sinus and condylar veins on the medial wall
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.pimp7: Q: What does Fisch class C imply? A: Erosion of the carotid canal, graded C1-C4 by extent along the ICA
A-jugular-foramen-tumor.pimp8: Q: Order of XII and the occipital artery in the neck? A: XII hooks around beneath the occipital artery (its SCM branch) and then passes forward lateral to ICA and ECA
OR-jugular-foramen-tumor.step1: Plan the skull-base corridor from imaging and preoperative lower-cranial-nerve/hearing function, then obtain cervical/mastoid exposure with control of the sigmoid-jugular and carotid compartments required by tumor extent.
OR-jugular-foramen-tumor.step2: Identify the facial nerve, labyrinth, sigmoid sinus, jugular bulb, internal carotid artery, and lower cranial nerves before drilling into the jugular foramen.
OR-jugular-foramen-tumor.step3: Skeletonize the sigmoid sinus/jugular bulb and infralabyrinthine or infratemporal corridor with diamond drilling, preserving hearing structures when the chosen approach is hearing-sparing.
OR-jugular-foramen-tumor.step4: Obtain proximal/distal vascular control when a hypervascular paraganglioma or vessel-encasing tumor makes hemorrhage a major risk.
OR-jugular-foramen-tumor.step5: Debulk the tumor internally and dissect its capsule from lower cranial nerves, carotid artery, brainstem/dura, and facial nerve in small controlled segments.
OR-jugular-foramen-tumor.step6: Control tumor feeders and venous bleeding directly rather than avulsing the lesion from the jugular bulb or carotid adventitia.
OR-jugular-foramen-tumor.step7: Decide deliberately whether adherent tumor should be left to preserve a critical nerve/vessel instead of pursuing unsafe gross-total removal.
OR-jugular-foramen-tumor.step8: Obliterate opened air cells, obtain watertight skull-base closure, reconstruct the soft-tissue defect, and document postoperative CN IX-XII, facial, hearing, aspiration, and vascular surveillance.

## Mastoidectomy for Cholesteatoma  (slug: mastoidectomy, region: Otology / Neurotology)

A-mastoidectomy.summary: Cholesteatoma spreads from the attic or posterior mesotympanum through the aditus into the antrum and mastoid, and hides in recesses the canal view cannot reach. Mastoidectomy opens these spaces from behind; safety rests on drilling within the triangle bounded by the tegmen above, sigmoid sinus behind and posterior canal wall in front, and on finding the lateral semicircular canal and incus short process early, because they locate the second genu and mastoid segment of the facial nerve.
A-mastoidectomy.layer1: Postauricular soft tissue — postauricular skin and subcutaneous tissue; temporalis fascia and muscle (superior); musculoperiosteal (Palva or T-shaped) flap; mastoid cortex. In young children the mastoid tip is undeveloped and the facial nerve exits the stylomastoid foramen superficially, so keep the incision posterior and avoid extending it low and anterior.
A-mastoidectomy.layer2: Cortical landmarks — linea temporalis (temporal line, inferior edge of temporalis); spine of Henle (suprameatal spine); Macewen triangle (cribriform area); mastoid tip. The antrum lies deep to Macewen's triangle, roughly 12 to 15 mm deep in adults (some sources quote up to about 20 mm). Start drilling along the temporal line and parallel to the posterior canal wall.
A-mastoidectomy.layer3: Cortical mastoidectomy — mastoid air cells; tegmen mastoideum (superior, middle fossa dura); sigmoid sinus plate (posterior); posterior bony canal wall (anterior); sinodural angle (Citelli angle). Saucerize the edges so the deepest point is always visible. Thin the tegmen and sinus plates without exposing dura or sinus unless needed. Körner's septum (petrosquamous lamina) may mimic the antrum's medial wall; drill through it.
A-mastoidectomy.layer4: Antrum and aditus — mastoid antrum; lateral (horizontal) semicircular canal prominence on the antrum floor-medial wall; aditus ad antrum; incus short process in the fossa incudis. The lateral canal is a smooth, dense, yellow-white bony ridge. The incus short process points posteriorly toward it, at the aditus. These two together set the depth and level of the facial nerve's second genu.
A-mastoidectomy.layer5: Epitympanum (attic) — incus body and malleus head; scutum (lateral attic wall); tegmen tympani; cog (anterior attic ridge); supratubal recess. Atticotomy removes the scutum from lateral; the cog is a bony ridge hanging from the tegmen in front of the malleus head that separates the main attic from the anterior epitympanic (supratubal) recess, and it lies just above the cochleariform process and tympanic facial nerve near the geniculate.
A-mastoidectomy.layer6: Facial recess (posterior tympanotomy) — mastoid segment of facial nerve (medial); chorda tympani (lateral); incus buttress / fossa incudis (superior); stapes, round window and promontory seen through the recess. Open the recess just below the incus buttress, lateral to the facial nerve, using a diamond burr with irrigation parallel to the nerve.
A-mastoidectomy.layer7: Mesotympanum and hidden recesses — sinus tympani (medial to the facial nerve, between ponticulus and subiculum); pyramidal eminence and stapedius tendon; hypotympanum; Prussak space (between pars flaccida and malleus neck). Sinus tympani is the classic site of residual disease; it cannot be seen from the mastoid. Inspect with an angled endoscope via the canal.
A-mastoidectomy.layer8: Canal wall down (when indicated) — posterior canal wall and facial ridge; mastoid tip; meatoplasty (conchal cartilage). Lower the facial ridge to the level of the nerve (the mastoid segment sits deep to the ridge) and remove the mastoid tip and overhangs for a smooth self-cleaning cavity. Inadequate meatoplasty and a high facial ridge are the common anatomic causes of a wet cavity.
A-mastoidectomy.landmark1: Mastoid antrum — Drill deep to Macewen's triangle (posterosuperior to the spine of Henle), staying just below the temporal line and parallel to the canal; the antrum lies roughly 12 to 15 mm deep in adults (some sources up to about 20 mm) (Gateway from the mastoid to the attic and the first deep landmark)
A-mastoidectomy.landmark2: Lateral semicircular canal — Smooth, dense, compact bony bulge in the floor and medial wall of the antrum, its anterior (ampullated) end is higher, so it slopes from anterosuperior to posteroinferior at about 30 degrees to the horizontal; it does not have air cells (Its anterior-inferior edge marks the level of the second genu of the facial nerve)
A-mastoidectomy.landmark3: Incus short process — In the aditus, just anterior to the lateral canal, pointing posteriorly toward the fossa incudis (Directs you to the facial recess; the facial nerve lies just medial and inferior to its tip)
A-mastoidectomy.landmark4: Mastoid segment of facial nerve — From the second genu below the lateral canal, follow a line toward the stylomastoid foramen; also find the digastric ridge in the mastoid tip, whose anterior end points to the stylomastoid foramen (Saucerize lateral to it and open the facial recess safely)
A-mastoidectomy.landmark5: Tegmen (middle fossa plate) — Follow the temporal line medially; the bone becomes denser and pinkish as dura shows through, and the tegmen slopes down medially (Superior limit; thinning it improves exposure, exposing dura risks CSF leak or encephalocele)
A-mastoidectomy.landmark6: Sigmoid sinus — Blue-grey line through thinned posterior bone; follow it to the sinodural angle where it meets the superior petrosal sinus and tegmen (Posterior limit; an anterior (forward) sinus narrows the mastoid)
A-mastoidectomy.landmark7: Cochleariform process — Hook-shaped spur in the medial attic-mesotympanum, anterior to the oval window, where the tensor tympani tendon turns laterally to the malleus neck (The tympanic facial nerve lies just superior to it, and the geniculate ganglion is just anterior-superior)
A-mastoidectomy.danger1: Facial nerve (tympanic, second genu, mastoid segments) — where: Tympanic segment above the oval window and below the lateral canal; second genu under the lateral canal's anterior-inferior edge, medial to the incus short process; mastoid segment descending medial to the posterior canal wall and facial ridge; protect: Identify the lateral canal first; drill with large diamond burrs and copious irrigation parallel to the nerve; use monitoring; never remove matrix blindly from a dehiscent canal; injury: Facial paralysis
A-mastoidectomy.danger2: Lateral semicircular canal — where: Medial wall of the antrum; the most common site of labyrinthine fistula from cholesteatoma; protect: Look for a fistula before lifting matrix there; leave matrix over a fistula until the end or exteriorize it; injury: Sensorineural hearing loss, vertigo
A-mastoidectomy.danger3: Middle fossa dura (tegmen) — where: Superior mastoid and attic roof; may be low-lying; protect: Thin, do not perforate; use diamond burrs near it; injury: CSF leak, encephalocele, epidural bleeding
A-mastoidectomy.danger4: Sigmoid sinus and emissary vein — where: Posterior mastoid; emissary vein pierces the cortex posteriorly; protect: Keep a thin bone shell; bone wax or gentle pressure for emissary bleeding; injury: Venous hemorrhage, air embolism, sinus thrombosis
A-mastoidectomy.danger5: Stapes and oval window — where: Posterior mesotympanum; often surrounded by matrix; protect: Remove matrix from the stapes last and in the direction of the stapedius tendon (posterior to anterior), not by pulling on the superstructure; injury: SNHL, perilymph leak
A-mastoidectomy.danger6: Jugular bulb and carotid — where: Hypotympanum floor (bulb) and anterior protympanum (carotid); protect: Gentle work in the hypotympanum and protympanum; injury: Major hemorrhage
A-mastoidectomy.variant1: Low-lying tegmen or anterior (forward-lying) sigmoid sinus that narrows the mastoid corridor, common in sclerotic, poorly pneumatized mastoids
A-mastoidectomy.variant2: Dehiscent tympanic facial nerve, or erosion of the facial canal by cholesteatoma
A-mastoidectomy.variant3: Labyrinthine fistula, most often the lateral semicircular canal
A-mastoidectomy.variant4: Körner's septum separating squamous and petrous cells, which can be mistaken for the antral floor
A-mastoidectomy.variant5: Deep sinus tympani extending medial to the facial nerve beyond endoscopic reach
A-mastoidectomy.supply1: Mastoid and middle ear arteries — Stylomastoid (posterior auricular), anterior tympanic (maxillary), superior petrosal and petrosquamous (middle meningeal), caroticotympanic (ICA)
A-mastoidectomy.supply2: Facial nerve blood supply — Superficial petrosal branch of the middle meningeal artery (geniculate region) and stylomastoid artery (mastoid segment)
A-mastoidectomy.supply3: Venous drainage — Sigmoid sinus via the mastoid emissary vein; superior petrosal sinus at the sinodural angle
A-mastoidectomy.supply4: Sensory — Middle ear: Jacobson nerve (IX); postauricular skin: great auricular and lesser occipital nerves
A-mastoidectomy.pimp1: Q: What are the boundaries of Macewen's (suprameatal) triangle? A: Temporal line superiorly, posterosuperior canal wall anteriorly, and a tangent from the posterior canal wall up to the temporal line posteriorly; the antrum lies deep to it
A-mastoidectomy.pimp2: Q: What are the boundaries of the facial recess? A: Facial nerve medially, chorda tympani laterally, incus buttress (fossa incudis) superiorly
A-mastoidectomy.pimp3: Q: What are the boundaries of the sinus tympani? A: Between the ponticulus superiorly and the subiculum inferiorly, medial to the facial nerve and pyramidal eminence
A-mastoidectomy.pimp4: Q: What are the boundaries of Prussak's space? A: Lateral: pars flaccida; medial: malleus neck; superior: lateral malleolar fold; inferior: lateral process of the malleus
A-mastoidectomy.pimp5: Q: Which semicircular canal is most commonly eroded by cholesteatoma? A: Lateral (horizontal) semicircular canal
A-mastoidectomy.pimp6: Q: What is the most common site of facial canal dehiscence? A: Tympanic segment above the oval window, near the cochleariform process / geniculate region
A-mastoidectomy.pimp7: Q: What is the Trautmann triangle? A: Area between the superior petrosal sinus, sigmoid sinus and bony labyrinth: the presigmoid posterior fossa dura
A-mastoidectomy.pimp8: Q: What is the Citelli angle? A: The sinodural angle, between the sigmoid sinus and the middle fossa (tegmen) dura
A-mastoidectomy.pimp9: Q: What does the digastric ridge point to? A: Its anterior end leads to the stylomastoid foramen and the facial nerve exit
A-mastoidectomy.pimp10: Q: Name the three classic causes of a wet canal-wall-down cavity. A: High facial ridge, small meatus, and residual mastoid tip or undrained cells (also exposed eustachian tube or middle ear mucosa)
OR-mastoidectomy.step1: Use the planned canal/postauricular approach, elevate the tympanomeatal flap as needed, and define disease extent in the middle ear before drilling toward hidden spaces.
OR-mastoidectomy.step2: Perform cortical mastoidectomy with constant orientation to the tegmen, sigmoid sinus, ear canal, lateral semicircular canal, and mastoid segment of the facial nerve.
OR-mastoidectomy.step3: Open the antrum and epitympanum, identify the short process/body of incus, and connect mastoid and middle-ear disease pathways under direct vision.
OR-mastoidectomy.step4: Remove cholesteatoma matrix systematically from epitympanum, facial recess, sinus tympani, hypotympanum, and mastoid while preserving labyrinth, facial nerve, and dura.
OR-mastoidectomy.step5: Assess ossicular erosion and remove/reconstruct only as required for complete disease clearance and hearing goals.
OR-mastoidectomy.step6: If canal-wall-down surgery is required, lower the facial ridge and exteriorize disease spaces while creating a smooth, self-cleaning cavity; if canal-wall-up, preserve/reconstruct the posterior canal wall appropriately.
OR-mastoidectomy.step7: Reinspect all hidden recesses with microscope/angled endoscope, ensuring no residual matrix remains on critical structures.
OR-mastoidectomy.step8: Complete tympanic membrane/ossicular reconstruction as planned, obliterate/reconstruct selectively, and pack/close without obstructing drainage.

## Middle Fossa Skull-Base Approach  (slug: middle-fossa-skull-base, region: Otology / Neurotology)

A-middle-fossa-skull-base.summary: The middle fossa approach reaches the IAC, labyrinthine facial nerve, geniculate ganglion and superior semicircular canal from above, by lifting the temporal lobe dura off the petrous floor, while preserving the cochlea and labyrinth. Safety rests on mapping the floor before drilling: the GSPN and facial hiatus anteromedially, the arcuate eminence (superior canal) posteriorly, the middle meningeal artery anteriorly, and the tightly packed cochlea, labyrinthine facial segment and superior canal ampulla at the lateral IAC.
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.layer1: Incision and temporalis — preauricular-temporal skin; superficial temporal artery and auriculotemporal nerve; frontal branch of facial nerve (anterior); temporalis fascia and muscle; squamous temporal bone. Keep the incision behind the superficial temporal artery and the frontal branch. Split or reflect the temporalis to expose the squama above the zygomatic root.
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.layer2: Craniotomy — squamous temporal bone; zygomatic root; middle meningeal artery grooves; temporal lobe dura. Center a roughly 4 x 5 cm window with about two-thirds anterior and one-third posterior to the canal, with its inferior edge at the floor of the fossa (level of the zygomatic root).
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.layer3: Extradural elevation — temporal dura; petrous ridge; superior petrosal sinus; arcuate eminence; GSPN and facial hiatus; middle meningeal artery at foramen spinosum. Elevate dura from posterior to anterior to avoid lifting the GSPN and pulling on the geniculate ganglion. Place the retractor tip at the petrous ridge, medial to the IAC.
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.layer4: Localizing the IAC — meatal plane; superior semicircular canal (blue line); GSPN to geniculate ganglion; IAC porus medially. Options: follow the GSPN back to the geniculate ganglion then the labyrinthine segment to the IAC (House; puts the facial nerve at most risk); blue-line the superior canal and place the IAC about 60 degrees anterior to its long axis (Fisch; the angle varies and blue-lining risks opening the canal); or bisect the roughly 120-degree angle between the GSPN and the superior canal / arcuate eminence (Garcia-Ibanez). Whichever is used, start drilling medially at the porus where there is more room, then follow the canal laterally.
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.layer5: Skeletonizing the IAC — IAC roof; porus (medial); fundus (lateral); Bill's bar; cochlea (anterior to the labyrinthine facial segment); superior canal ampulla (posterior). Drill 270 degrees medially at the porus, but only about 90 degrees laterally at the fundus where the cochlea lies anterior and the superior canal ampulla lies posterior to the labyrinthine facial segment.
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.layer6: IAC contents — IAC dura; facial nerve (anterior-superior); superior vestibular nerve (posterior-superior); cochlear and inferior vestibular nerves (inferior); AICA loop / labyrinthine artery. From above, the facial nerve and superior vestibular nerve are seen first, separated by Bill's bar at the fundus. The cochlear nerve is deep (inferior) to the facial nerve, which is why the facial nerve is at greater risk from a middle fossa approach.
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.layer7: Closure — opened air cells; bone or fascia graft over the IAC; temporal dura; craniotomy bone flap. Seal air cells with bone wax or fascia and muscle to prevent CSF otorhinorrhea through the middle ear and Eustachian tube.
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.landmark1: Arcuate eminence — Rounded bulge on the posteromedial floor; the superior canal lies under it but not always at its summit (Posterior reference; the canal can be blue-lined to confirm)
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.landmark2: Greater superficial petrosal nerve — Anteromedial on the floor, running anteromedially from the facial hiatus toward the foramen lacerum, parallel to the petrous ridge; often accompanied by the superficial petrosal artery (Leads back to the geniculate ganglion)
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.landmark3: Middle meningeal artery / foramen spinosum — Anterior limit of the elevation, posterolateral to foramen ovale (V3) (Anterior limit; bleeding source)
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.landmark4: Petrous ridge and superior petrosal sinus — Medial edge of the floor; retractor tip sits here (Anchor for the retractor and medial limit)
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.landmark5: IAC porus — Medially, along the bisector of the GSPN-arcuate eminence angle near the petrous ridge, where there is a wide safe margin (Safe first point to find the IAC before following laterally)
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.landmark6: Bill's bar — At the lateral IAC fundus between the facial nerve (anterior) and the superior vestibular nerve (posterior) (Separates the two nerves you see from above)
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.danger1: Facial nerve (geniculate ganglion, labyrinthine and meatal segments) — where: Geniculate may be dehiscent on the floor; the labyrinthine segment is the narrowest part of the fallopian canal at the lateral IAC; the meatal segment sits anterosuperior, closest to the surgeon from above; protect: Elevate dura posterior to anterior; monitor; open the IAC dura posteriorly over the superior vestibular nerve; injury: Facial paralysis
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.danger2: Cochlea (basal turn) — where: Anterior and medial to the labyrinthine facial segment, just in front of the lateral IAC, in the angle between GSPN and labyrinthine facial nerve; protect: Limit anterior drilling at the fundus; injury: Deafness
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.danger3: Superior semicircular canal — where: Under the arcuate eminence, posterior to the lateral IAC; protect: Blue-line rather than open it; limit posterior drilling at the fundus; injury: Hearing loss, vertigo
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.danger4: Temporal lobe and vein of Labbé — where: Above the dura; the vein of Labbé drains posteriorly toward the transverse-sigmoid junction; protect: Minimal retraction, keep the craniotomy anterior enough, mannitol or lumbar drainage as needed; injury: Seizures, aphasia (dominant side), hematoma
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.danger5: Middle meningeal artery — where: Foramen spinosum and dural grooves; protect: Coagulate or pack the foramen; injury: Epidural hematoma
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.danger6: Petrous ICA (horizontal segment) — where: Under the floor medial to the GSPN, sometimes dehiscent; protect: Avoid drilling anteromedial to the GSPN; injury: Major hemorrhage
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.danger7: AICA / labyrinthine artery — where: Within the IAC; protect: Avoid coagulation in the IAC; injury: Hearing loss, brainstem ischemia
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.variant1: Dehiscent geniculate ganglion or GSPN on the floor
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.variant2: Superior canal dehiscence or a very thin canal roof
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.variant3: Arcuate eminence that does not overlie the superior canal
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.variant4: Dehiscent petrous ICA under the GSPN
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.variant5: Pneumatized petrous apex or air cells around the IAC (CSF leak risk)
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.supply1: Dura — Middle meningeal artery (maxillary via foramen spinosum)
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.supply2: Geniculate region facial nerve — Superficial petrosal branch of the middle meningeal artery
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.supply3: Inner ear — Labyrinthine artery from AICA
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.supply4: GSPN fibers — Preganglionic parasympathetic (superior salivatory nucleus) to the pterygopalatine ganglion; join the deep petrosal to form the vidian nerve
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.pimp1: Q: What are the segments of the facial nerve, and which is narrowest? A: Intracranial, meatal, labyrinthine, tympanic, mastoid, extratemporal; the labyrinthine segment (narrowest at the meatal foramen) is the narrowest
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.pimp2: Q: What is the first branch of the facial nerve? A: The greater superficial petrosal nerve, from the geniculate ganglion
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.pimp3: Q: Which IAC nerve lies anterosuperiorly, closest to the surgeon from above, and is therefore most at risk in the middle fossa approach? A: The facial nerve (the superior vestibular nerve is also superior but lies posterior, behind Bill's bar)
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.pimp4: Q: What are the boundaries of the Kawase (posteromedial) triangle? A: GSPN laterally, petrous ridge / superior petrosal sinus medially, V3 / trigeminal ganglion anteriorly, and the IAC / arcuate eminence posteriorly (the cochlea at the posterolateral apex)
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.pimp5: Q: What is the Glasscock (posterolateral) triangle? A: Area bounded by the GSPN, a line from foramen spinosum to the arcuate eminence and V3, used to find the horizontal petrous ICA
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.pimp6: Q: What does the arcuate eminence overlie? A: The superior semicircular canal (approximately)
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.pimp7: Q: Which approach is best for a small intracanalicular tumor with good hearing? A: Middle fossa approach
A-middle-fossa-skull-base.pimp8: Q: Where is the cochlea relative to the labyrinthine facial nerve? A: Anteromedial, in the angle between the labyrinthine facial segment and the GSPN
OR-middle-fossa-skull-base.step1: Position and perform the temporal craniotomy centered over the middle fossa, then elevate dura from posterior to anterior with minimal fixed temporal-lobe retraction.
OR-middle-fossa-skull-base.step2: Identify the arcuate eminence, greater superficial petrosal nerve, petrous ridge, and middle meningeal/foramen spinosum landmarks before drilling.
OR-middle-fossa-skull-base.step3: Use the chosen landmark strategy to localize the internal auditory canal and skeletonize it with diamond drilling while protecting cochlea, labyrinth, and facial nerve.
OR-middle-fossa-skull-base.step4: Open the IAC dura only after the bony limits are defined and identify facial/cochlear/vestibular nerve relationships under magnification.
OR-middle-fossa-skull-base.step5: Perform the intended tumor, facial-nerve, or superior-canal work with continuous awareness of the geniculate ganglion and labyrinthine facial segment.
OR-middle-fossa-skull-base.step6: Seal any opened petrous air cells and reconstruct drilled defects to prevent postoperative CSF egress to the middle ear/mastoid.
OR-middle-fossa-skull-base.step7: Obtain meticulous dural hemostasis and watertight closure, allowing the temporal lobe to return without focal compression.
OR-middle-fossa-skull-base.step8: Replace the craniotomy and close layers while documenting immediate facial, hearing, vestibular, and neurologic assessment.

## Ossiculoplasty  (slug: ossiculoplasty, region: Otology / Neurotology)

A-ossiculoplasty.summary: Ossiculoplasty rebuilds the lever and coupling between the tympanic membrane and the oval window when the chain is eroded, fixed or discontinuous. The anatomic decision is whether a stapes superstructure is present (PORP on the stapes head) or absent (TORP on the footplate), and safety depends on the facial nerve above the oval window and a mobile footplate that must not be pushed into the vestibule.
A-ossiculoplasty.layer1: Tympanomeatal flap — posterior canal skin; annulus; chorda tympani. Raise a generous posterior flap; in revision cases the drum may be thin, retracted or adherent to the promontory.
A-ossiculoplasty.layer2: Ossicular survey — malleus manubrium and head; incus body, long and lenticular process; incudostapedial joint; stapes head, crura and footplate. Find the gap. The most common defect is erosion of the incus long or lenticular process with an intact stapes and malleus.
A-ossiculoplasty.layer3: Mobility testing — malleus; stapes; round window reflex. Palpate each ossicle separately. Check for attic fixation of the malleus head and incus body (tympanosclerosis, epitympanic fixation) and for footplate fixation.
A-ossiculoplasty.layer4: Preparing the reconstruction path — oval window niche; tympanic facial nerve canal (above); promontory (below and medial); posterior canal wall (lateral). The prosthesis must not touch the facial canal, promontory or posterior canal wall. Remove adhesions and disease from the niche carefully.
A-ossiculoplasty.layer5: Measurement — stapes head or footplate; manubrium or drum undersurface. Measure with the drum in its physiologic position. Typical PORP lengths are shorter than TORP lengths; avoid an overlong prosthesis that tents the drum.
A-ossiculoplasty.layer6: Prosthesis placement and cartilage cap — stapes head / footplate; manubrium (if present); thin cartilage graft under the TM. Seat a PORP on the stapes head or a TORP on the center of the footplate, angle the top toward the malleus handle or drum, and interpose cartilage between prosthesis and drum to reduce extrusion.
A-ossiculoplasty.landmark1: Stapes superstructure — Look below the tympanic facial nerve and in front of the pyramidal eminence; the stapedius tendon points to the stapes neck (Decides PORP versus TORP)
A-ossiculoplasty.landmark2: Footplate — Between the crura, or if crura are gone, the oval floor in the niche beneath the facial nerve; it may be covered by mucosa or tympanosclerosis (Base for a TORP; must be mobile)
A-ossiculoplasty.landmark3: Malleus manubrium — Embedded in the drum, running from the lateral process to the umbo; check whether it is medialized (Affects prosthesis angle and stability)
A-ossiculoplasty.landmark4: Incus remnant — In the posterosuperior quadrant and attic; the body sits in the epitympanum behind the malleus head (May be sculpted and interposed as an autograft)
A-ossiculoplasty.landmark5: Tympanic facial nerve — Horizontal ridge above the oval window from cochleariform process to pyramidal eminence; may be dehiscent (Avoid contact between prosthesis and nerve)
A-ossiculoplasty.danger1: Facial nerve, tympanic segment — where: Directly above the oval window, sometimes dehiscent or overhanging; protect: Identify it before working in the niche; keep instruments and prosthesis off it; injury: Facial weakness
A-ossiculoplasty.danger2: Footplate and vestibule — where: Oval window; can be thin or loosened by disease; protect: Do not press the TORP hard on the footplate; use a shoe or cartilage footplate shield when indicated; injury: SNHL, vertigo, perilymph fistula
A-ossiculoplasty.danger3: Chorda tympani — where: Posterosuperior quadrant; protect: Preserve during scutum removal; injury: Taste change
A-ossiculoplasty.danger4: Tympanic membrane — where: Over the prosthesis head; protect: Cartilage cap and correct length; injury: Extrusion, perforation
A-ossiculoplasty.danger5: Round window — where: Posteroinferior; protect: Keep packing and debris out; injury: Loss of round window function, reduced hearing gain
A-ossiculoplasty.variant1: Dehiscent or low-lying tympanic facial nerve narrowing the oval window niche
A-ossiculoplasty.variant2: Congenital ossicular anomalies (fused malleus-incus, absent or fixed stapes) seen in syndromes or isolated
A-ossiculoplasty.variant3: Malleus head fixation in the attic, which mimics otosclerosis
A-ossiculoplasty.variant4: Tympanosclerosis around the stapes and footplate
A-ossiculoplasty.variant5: Persistent stapedial artery
A-ossiculoplasty.supply1: Ossicular supply — Anterior tympanic artery (maxillary) and mucosal vessels; the incus long process is a watershed area
A-ossiculoplasty.supply2: Middle ear sensory — Jacobson nerve (IX)
A-ossiculoplasty.supply3: Stapedius — Facial nerve
A-ossiculoplasty.supply4: Tensor tympani — V3 (nerve to medial pterygoid)
A-ossiculoplasty.pimp1: Q: What is the most common ossicular defect in chronic otitis media? A: Erosion of the incus long or lenticular process
A-ossiculoplasty.pimp2: Q: What is the difference between a PORP and a TORP? A: A PORP sits on a present stapes head; a TORP sits on the footplate when the superstructure is absent
A-ossiculoplasty.pimp3: Q: What are the two main mechanisms of middle ear impedance matching? A: Area ratio of TM to footplate (about 17:1 effective) and the ossicular lever (about 1.3:1); the TM curvature adds a smaller contribution
A-ossiculoplasty.pimp4: Q: Which ossicles develop from the first arch and which from the second? A: First (Meckel): malleus head and neck, incus body and short process; second (Reichert): manubrium, incus long process, stapes superstructure
A-ossiculoplasty.pimp5: Q: Which muscle inserts on the malleus and which nerve supplies it? A: Tensor tympani, whose tendon turns around the cochleariform process to insert on the upper manubrium; V3 (nerve to medial pterygoid)
A-ossiculoplasty.pimp6: Q: Why place cartilage between the prosthesis and the TM? A: To reduce extrusion through the drum
A-ossiculoplasty.pimp7: Q: What is the Austin-Kartush classification? A: Based on malleus (M) and stapes superstructure (S): A M+S+, B M+S-, C M-S+, D M-S-; E = ossicular head fixation, F = stapes fixation
OR-ossiculoplasty.step1: Elevate the tympanomeatal flap and expose the ossicular chain while preserving viable tympanic membrane and middle-ear mucosa.
OR-ossiculoplasty.step2: Assess malleus, incus, stapes superstructure/footplate mobility, and disease before selecting partial versus total ossicular reconstruction.
OR-ossiculoplasty.step3: Remove only nonviable or disease-involved ossicular segments and clear the prosthesis path of adhesions/cholesteatoma.
OR-ossiculoplasty.step4: Measure the required reconstruction height with the tympanic membrane returned toward its physiologic position rather than estimating an overlong prosthesis.
OR-ossiculoplasty.step5: Place cartilage protection when indicated and seat the prosthesis securely on the stapes head or footplate with stable contact to the tympanic membrane/malleus.
OR-ossiculoplasty.step6: Check that the reconstruction moves freely and is not impinging on the promontory, facial canal, or canal wall.
OR-ossiculoplasty.step7: Return the tympanomeatal flap and confirm the graft/prosthesis relationship before packing.
OR-ossiculoplasty.step8: Pack the canal gently enough to support the repair without displacing the reconstruction.

## Retrosigmoid Skull-Base Approach  (slug: retrosigmoid-skull-base, region: Otology / Neurotology)

A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.summary: A suboccipital craniotomy just posterior to the sigmoid sinus and inferior to the transverse sinus opens the cerebellopontine angle (CPA) from behind, giving access to the brainstem, cranial nerves V-XI and the porus of the internal auditory canal (IAC) while leaving the labyrinth intact. Safety depends on locating the transverse-sigmoid junction so that neither sinus is torn, and on knowing that the facial nerve usually lies on the anterior (hidden) face of a vestibular schwannoma, with AICA looping close to the porus.
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.layer1: Positioning and soft tissue — skin; occipitalis / posterior auricular muscle; SCM, splenius capitis and longissimus capitis insertions; occipital artery; lesser occipital nerve; mastoid emissary vein. Retroauricular curvilinear or lazy-S incision a few cm behind the postauricular crease. Reflect the muscles as one musculoperiosteal layer for a watertight closure. The occipital artery runs medially in the lower field, deep to splenius capitis. The mastoid emissary vein exits a foramen near the posterior mastoid and drains into the sigmoid; it marks the sinus and should be waxed rather than torn.
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.layer2: Bone landmarks and craniotomy — asterion; digastric groove; mastoid air cells; transverse sinus (superior edge); sigmoid sinus (anterior edge). The asterion approximates the transverse-sigmoid junction, though the relationship varies. Place the first burr hole slightly inferior and medial to the asterion. Strip dura off the inner table before using the craniotome. Expose the posterior edge of the sigmoid sinus anteriorly and the inferior edge of the transverse sinus superiorly; together they form the superolateral corner of the window. Wax any opened mastoid cells at once.
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.layer3: Dural opening and CSF release — posterior fossa dura; petrosal (anterior) surface of cerebellar hemisphere; cisterna magna / lateral cerebellomedullary cistern. Open the dura as a flap based on the sigmoid sinus. Elevate the inferolateral cerebellum gently superomedially to open the lateral cerebellomedullary cistern near the lower cranial nerves and release CSF. The cerebellum then falls away medially, so fixed retraction is minimal.
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.layer4: CPA survey: lower neurovascular complex — CN IX, X, XI entering the jugular foramen; PICA; flocculus; choroid plexus at the foramen of Luschka. The lower complex is the most inferior and comes into view first as the cerebellum relaxes. IX is the most rostral fibre bundle at the jugular foramen; X follows as many rootlets just below it. Choroid plexus protruding from the foramen of Luschka sits just posterior to IX/X and points up toward the VII/VIII root entry zone.
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.layer5: CPA survey: middle and upper complexes — CN VII / VIII complex and AICA; nervus intermedius; CN V and superior petrosal vein (Dandy) near the tentorium; CN VI deep, ascending along the clivus anterior to the VII/VIII complex. VII leaves the brainstem at the pontomedullary junction, anterior and slightly rostral to VIII. AICA loops around or into the porus. Superiorly, the superior petrosal vein drains into the superior petrosal sinus close to CN V. Preserve it where possible.
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.layer6: Tumor debulking and arachnoid dissection — tumor capsule; arachnoid planes; brainstem surface; facial nerve (usually anterior / anterosuperior on the capsule). Stimulate the posterior capsule to confirm there is no nerve on it, then debulk from inside. Fold the capsule in on itself and develop the arachnoid plane medially at the brainstem, where VII and VIII are found at the root entry zone. Then work laterally toward the porus.
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.layer7: Drilling the posterior IAC wall — posterior lip of porus acusticus; IAC dura; endolymphatic duct / operculum and vestibular aqueduct; posterior semicircular canal and crus commune (lateral limit); jugular bulb (inferior); retrofacial / petrous air cells. Coagulate and incise the dura over the posterior IAC and drill the posterior wall with a diamond burr. The lateral limit is set by the posterior canal and crus commune, which lie lateral and slightly posterior; measure the safe drilling distance on preoperative CT. The operculum of the endolymphatic duct is a useful warning sign. A high jugular bulb can limit inferior drilling.
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.layer8: IAC contents and lateral dissection — transverse (falciform) crest; superior and inferior vestibular nerves (posterior); facial nerve (anterosuperior); cochlear nerve (anteroinferior); labyrinthine artery. The vestibular nerves lie posterior and are seen first. Dissect tumor medial-to-lateral within the canal, or lateral-to-medial once the fundus is visible, so traction does not avulse the cochlear nerve at the modiolus.
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.layer9: Closure — opened air cells (posterior IAC, mastoid); dura; bone flap / cranioplasty; musculoperiosteal layer. Seal all opened air cells with wax, muscle or fat; a missed cell is the usual route for CSF rhinorrhea through the middle ear and Eustachian tube. Close the dura watertight or patch it, replace the bone or perform cranioplasty, and close the muscle in layers.
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.landmark1: Transverse-sigmoid junction — The asterion (meeting point of the lambdoid, parietomastoid and occipitomastoid sutures) usually overlies or lies close to the junction, but the relationship varies between patients, so treat it as an approximation. A line from the zygomatic root to the inion approximates the transverse sinus. Follow the mastoid emissary vein anteriorly to the sigmoid. Confirm on CT or MRV. (Defines the superolateral corner of the craniotomy and prevents a sinus tear)
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.landmark2: Facial nerve root exit zone — At the pontomedullary junction, anterior to and slightly above the VIII root entry. Look just rostral to the flocculus and the choroid plexus tuft from the foramen of Luschka. Confirm with low-current stimulation before dissecting. (The proximal nerve is the most constant place to identify VII when tumor has distorted it)
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.landmark3: Facial nerve on the tumor capsule — Expect it on the anterior, often anterosuperior, capsule surface, splayed thin. Map the posterior capsule with the stimulator before every incision. (VII is hidden behind the tumor in this view)
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.landmark4: Lower cranial nerves (IX-XI) — Inferior part of the field, entering the jugular foramen. IX is the most superior and is separated from X by a dural septum; XI rises from below with spinal rootlets. (Keep cottonoids and retraction above them to avoid dysphagia and aspiration)
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.landmark5: Posterior IAC wall and lateral drilling limit — Porus on the posterior petrous face above the jugular foramen. Drill laterally only as far as the CT-measured distance to the posterior canal and crus commune. Stop if the endolymphatic duct operculum or blue-lined canal appears. (Hearing preservation requires an intact labyrinth)
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.landmark6: Superior petrosal vein (Dandy) — Upper CPA near the tentorium and CN V, draining into the superior petrosal sinus (Avulsion causes brisk bleeding; sacrifice carries a small venous infarct risk)
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.landmark7: AICA loop — Middle complex, often looping around VII/VIII at or into the porus. It gives off the labyrinthine artery. (Must be dissected off the capsule rather than coagulated)
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.danger1: Sigmoid and transverse sinuses — where: Anterior and superior edges of the craniotomy, immediately deep to the bone; protect: Burr hole inferomedial to the asterion, strip dura from the inner table before cutting, and drill over the sinus with a diamond burr. If a tear occurs, use head-up positioning, irrigation and hemostatic packing.; injury: Hemorrhage, venous air embolism, and sinus thrombosis or venous infarction, especially if the sinus is dominant
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.danger2: AICA and labyrinthine artery — where: Looping on VII/VIII at the porus; the labyrinthine artery enters the IAC; protect: Sharp arachnoid dissection off the capsule, no coagulation of loops near the porus, and papaverine for vasospasm; injury: Hearing loss from cochlear ischemia; AICA infarct of the lateral pons and middle cerebellar peduncle
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.danger3: Facial nerve — where: Anterior or anterosuperior capsule surface, and anterosuperior at the fundus; protect: Map with the stimulator, debulk before dissecting the capsule, dissect in small segments along the nerve axis, and accept residual tumor if the nerve is inseparable; injury: Facial paralysis
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.danger4: CN IX-XI — where: Inferior CPA at the jugular foramen, just below the tumor's inferior pole; protect: Arachnoid dissection, cottonoid protection, minimal bipolar near the nerves, and monitoring of X if used; injury: Dysphagia, aspiration, hoarseness, shoulder weakness
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.danger5: Labyrinth (posterior canal, crus commune, vestibule) — where: Lateral to the posterior IAC wall; protect: CT-guided drilling limit and diamond burr; stop at the operculum or vestibular aqueduct; injury: Sensorineural hearing loss
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.danger6: Cerebellum and brainstem — where: Medial; retracted to open the CPA; protect: Release CSF first, use minimal fixed retraction, and preserve petrosal and brainstem veins where possible; injury: Cerebellar edema or hematoma, ataxia, brainstem injury
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.variant1: Dominant (usually right) transverse/sigmoid sinus, or hypoplastic contralateral sinus, which raises the stakes of sinus injury
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.variant2: High-riding jugular bulb reaching the IAC floor, which limits inferior drilling of the posterior IAC wall
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.variant3: AICA loop extending into the IAC, which is adherent to tumor and at risk
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.variant4: Large mastoid emissary vein or anteriorly placed sigmoid sinus
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.variant5: Pneumatized posterior IAC lip and retrofacial cells, which increase CSF leak risk
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.supply1: CPA arteries — SCA (upper complex, CN V), AICA (middle complex, VII/VIII, labyrinthine artery usually from AICA), PICA (lower complex, IX-XI)
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.supply2: Venous drainage — Superior petrosal vein to superior petrosal sinus; transverse to sigmoid sinus to jugular bulb; mastoid emissary vein to sigmoid
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.supply3: Scalp and muscle — Occipital and posterior auricular arteries; lesser and greater occipital nerves (incisional headache and numbness)
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.supply4: IAC nerves — VII anterosuperior, cochlear anteroinferior, superior and inferior vestibular posterior
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.pimp1: Q: Which sutures meet at the asterion? A: Lambdoid, parietomastoid and occipitomastoid sutures
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.pimp2: Q: Which artery usually gives off the labyrinthine (internal auditory) artery? A: AICA
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.pimp3: Q: Arrange the nerves in the lateral IAC. A: Facial anterosuperior, cochlear anteroinferior, superior vestibular posterosuperior, inferior vestibular posteroinferior (seven-up, Coke down)
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.pimp4: Q: What separates the superior and inferior compartments, and the facial from the superior vestibular nerve, at the fundus? A: Transverse (falciform) crest horizontally; Bill's bar (vertical crest) separates VII from the SVN
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.pimp5: Q: In which direction does a vestibular schwannoma most often displace the facial nerve? A: Anteriorly, most often anterosuperiorly
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.pimp6: Q: What does choroid plexus at the foramen of Luschka help you find? A: The VII/VIII root entry zone just above it, and IX/X just anterior to it
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.pimp7: Q: Name the three neurovascular complexes of the CPA. A: Upper: SCA with CN III, IV, V. Middle: AICA with CN VI, VII, VIII. Lower: PICA with CN IX, X, XI, XII (Rhoton)
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.pimp8: Q: Main advantages of retrosigmoid over translabyrinthine? A: Hearing can be preserved and the CPA and brainstem are widely seen. Downsides are limited view of the lateral fundus, cerebellar retraction and more postoperative headache
A-retrosigmoid-skull-base.pimp9: Q: Through which route does a CSF leak after retrosigmoid surgery usually present as rhinorrhea? A: Opened posterior IAC or mastoid air cells, then the middle ear, then the Eustachian tube
OR-retrosigmoid-skull-base.step1: Position to expose the retrosigmoid region and create a craniotomy/craniectomy with the transverse-sigmoid junction defined and venous sinus protected.
OR-retrosigmoid-skull-base.step2: Open dura and release cisternal CSF to relax the cerebellum before using fixed retraction.
OR-retrosigmoid-skull-base.step3: Identify the lower cranial nerves, VII-VIII complex, brainstem, petrosal veins, and tumor/neurovascular target under the microscope.
OR-retrosigmoid-skull-base.step4: Open the arachnoid planes and internally debulk a mass when needed before attempting circumferential capsule mobilization.
OR-retrosigmoid-skull-base.step5: For IAC pathology, drill the posterior canal wall to the needed extent with orientation to labyrinth/endolymphatic structures and protect the facial/cochlear nerves.
OR-retrosigmoid-skull-base.step6: Dissect the lesion from brainstem and cranial nerves in small segments, using facial-nerve stimulation and avoiding avulsion of adherent vessels/nerves.
OR-retrosigmoid-skull-base.step7: Seal opened mastoid/petrous air cells meticulously and achieve watertight dural closure to reduce CSF leak.
OR-retrosigmoid-skull-base.step8: Replace/repair the bony defect and close layers with postoperative facial, hearing, vestibular, lower-cranial-nerve, and neurologic assessment.

## Stapedotomy  (slug: stapedotomy, region: Otology / Neurotology)

A-stapedotomy.summary: Otosclerosis fixes the stapes footplate in the oval window, most often starting at the fissula ante fenestram anterior to the window. Stapedotomy bypasses the fixed footplate with a piston from the incus long process into a small fenestra; safety depends on the tympanic facial nerve just above the oval window, the chorda in the posterosuperior quadrant, and the saccule and utricle that lie within the vestibule beneath the footplate.
A-stapedotomy.layer1: Tympanomeatal flap — posterior canal skin; annulus; chorda tympani at the iter chordae posterius. Use a posterior flap long enough to cover the scutum curetted area. Enter inferiorly and lift the annulus up to the posterosuperior quadrant.
A-stapedotomy.layer2: Posterosuperior scutum removal — posterosuperior bony canal wall (scutum); chorda tympani; incus long process. Curette or drill bone lateral to the chorda until you can see the pyramidal eminence posteriorly and the facial nerve canal above the oval window. Push the chorda down or up rather than cutting it.
A-stapedotomy.layer3: Exposure of the oval window niche — incus long and lenticular process; incudostapedial joint; stapes head, anterior and posterior crura; pyramidal eminence and stapedius tendon; tympanic facial nerve canal (above); promontory (below). Check mobility of the malleus and incus to exclude malleus head fixation, and confirm footplate fixation by palpating the stapes and watching the round-window reflex.
A-stapedotomy.layer4: Measurement — lateral surface of the incus long process; footplate. Measure from the lateral surface of the incus long process to the footplate; the piston length is usually this distance plus about 0.25 to 0.5 mm into the vestibule. Usual adult range is about 4 to 5 mm.
A-stapedotomy.layer5: Superstructure removal — stapedius tendon; incudostapedial joint; posterior crus; anterior crus. Divide the stapedius tendon, separate the incudostapedial joint, then fracture or laser the crura and remove the superstructure toward the promontory, away from the facial nerve.
A-stapedotomy.layer6: Fenestration — footplate (central or posterior-central usually); vestibule (perilymph); utricle (posterosuperior, elliptical recess); saccule (anteroinferior, spherical recess). Make a small fenestra (often 0.6 to 0.8 mm) in the central or posterior-central footplate, away from its posterosuperior corner. Do not suction directly over the open vestibule. Classic teaching names the saccule as the end organ most at risk, but 3D micro-CT work places the utricular macula as close as about 0.6 mm under the posterosuperior footplate; the central and inferior footplate is the safest zone.
A-stapedotomy.layer7: Prosthesis placement — incus long process; fenestra; tissue seal (vein, fat or perichondrium). Crimp the prosthesis loop on the incus without devascularizing the long process; it should move freely without touching the facial canal or promontory.
A-stapedotomy.landmark1: Pyramidal eminence — Bony cone on the posterior wall of the mesotympanum at the level of the stapes head, from which the stapedius tendon runs forward to the stapes neck (Posterior boundary of exposure; you have removed enough scutum when you see it)
A-stapedotomy.landmark2: Tympanic facial nerve — Horizontal tube running just above the oval window, from the cochleariform process anteriorly to the pyramidal eminence posteriorly; may be dehiscent and overhang the footplate (Must be seen before working in the niche)
A-stapedotomy.landmark3: Chorda tympani — Exits the posterosuperior sulcus under the annulus and runs medial to the drum and lateral to the incus long process toward the malleus neck (Preserve during scutum removal)
A-stapedotomy.landmark4: Incus long process / lenticular process — Descends from the incus body behind the malleus handle to articulate with the stapes head at the lenticular process (Attachment for the piston)
A-stapedotomy.landmark5: Footplate — Oval floor between the crura, in the oval window niche below the facial nerve and above the promontory (Site of fenestra; anterior footplate is the usual site of the otosclerotic focus)
A-stapedotomy.landmark6: Round window niche — Posteroinferior to the promontory, under the subiculum and below the oval window (Watch for the round-window reflex to confirm stapes fixation or mobility)
A-stapedotomy.danger1: Facial nerve, tympanic segment — where: Directly above the oval window; may be dehiscent and prolapse over the footplate; protect: Identify before laser or drill; direct laser beams away from it; ensure the piston clears it; injury: Facial paralysis (rare), often from laser or direct trauma
A-stapedotomy.danger2: Chorda tympani — where: Posterosuperior; often stretched across the operative field; protect: Mobilize; cut only if exposure is impossible; injury: Taste disturbance, dry mouth on that side
A-stapedotomy.danger3: Vestibule (saccule and utricle) — where: Immediately medial to the footplate; published footplate-to-end-organ distances range from about 0.6 mm (utricle, posterosuperior footplate) to about 2 to 3 mm (from the footplate center), so the margin is small and variable; protect: Small fenestra, no suction in the open vestibule, piston no more than about 0.5 mm into the vestibule; injury: SNHL, vertigo, perilymph fistula
A-stapedotomy.danger4: Incus long process — where: Lateral to the stapes, with a tenuous blood supply; protect: Gentle crimping; avoid excessive heat from laser; injury: Incus necrosis, late conductive hearing loss, prosthesis displacement
A-stapedotomy.danger5: Persistent stapedial artery — where: Runs through the obturator foramen between the stapes crura; protect: Recognize a vessel between the crura; abort or modify if large; injury: Bleeding that is difficult to control
A-stapedotomy.danger6: Tympanic membrane — where: Flap and annulus; protect: Gentle elevation; avoid tearing at the posterosuperior annulus; injury: Perforation
A-stapedotomy.variant1: Dehiscent or overhanging tympanic facial nerve narrowing the oval window niche
A-stapedotomy.variant2: Persistent stapedial artery between the crura
A-stapedotomy.variant3: Obliterative otosclerosis with a thick, biscuit footplate requiring drilling
A-stapedotomy.variant4: Malleus head fixation or congenital ossicular anomalies that mimic otosclerosis
A-stapedotomy.variant5: Enlarged vestibular aqueduct or X-linked stapes gusher anatomy (dilated IAC, absent bony separation from the cochlea); stapedotomy carries risk of perilymph gusher
A-stapedotomy.supply1: Ossicular blood supply — Anterior tympanic (maxillary) branches to malleus and incus; the incus long process and lenticular process depend on a tenuous nutrient and mucosal supply, which explains their vulnerability to erosion and necrosis
A-stapedotomy.supply2: Stapedius muscle — Facial nerve (nerve to stapedius from the mastoid segment)
A-stapedotomy.supply3: Tensor tympani — V3 via the nerve to the medial pterygoid
A-stapedotomy.supply4: Taste and secretomotor — Chorda tympani
A-stapedotomy.pimp1: Q: What is the fissula ante fenestram? A: A cleft of fibrous tissue in the otic capsule anterior to the oval window; it is the most common site of otosclerosis
A-stapedotomy.pimp2: Q: What is the Carhart notch? A: A dip in bone conduction at about 2 kHz in otosclerosis, due to loss of ossicular resonance, that improves after surgery
A-stapedotomy.pimp3: Q: How far should a stapedotomy piston project into the vestibule, and why? A: No more than about 0.5 mm (typically 0.25 to 0.5 mm), because the saccule and utricle lie only about 0.6 to 3 mm medial to the footplate
A-stapedotomy.pimp4: Q: What innervates the stapedius muscle? A: Facial nerve, via the nerve to stapedius from the mastoid segment
A-stapedotomy.pimp5: Q: From which pharyngeal arch does the stapes superstructure develop? A: Second arch (Reichert cartilage); the footplate's vestibular part derives in part from the otic capsule
A-stapedotomy.pimp6: Q: What vessel may pass between the crura of the stapes? A: A persistent stapedial artery (embryonic branch from the second arch artery)
A-stapedotomy.pimp7: Q: What CT finding would make you worry about a perilymph gusher? A: X-linked deafness anatomy: bulbous IAC fundus with deficient bone between the IAC and the cochlear basal turn; also an enlarged vestibular aqueduct
A-stapedotomy.pimp8: Q: What is the usual piston length? A: Incus to footplate distance plus about 0.25 to 0.5 mm, typically 4 to 5 mm in adults
OR-stapedotomy.step1: Elevate the tympanomeatal flap and enter the middle ear while preserving the annulus and chorda tympani as anatomy allows.
OR-stapedotomy.step2: Curette/sculpt the posterosuperior canal wall only enough to visualize the incus long process, stapes superstructure, pyramidal eminence, and facial nerve.
OR-stapedotomy.step3: Confirm ossicular mobility and stapes fixation before committing to footplate fenestration.
OR-stapedotomy.step4: Measure the incus-to-footplate distance and select the prosthesis length before disrupting the stapes superstructure.
OR-stapedotomy.step5: Divide the stapedial tendon and posterior crus/superstructure in a controlled sequence, minimizing manipulation of the footplate.
OR-stapedotomy.step6: Create the footplate fenestra at the planned site with laser/drill/perforator while avoiding suction or instrument plunge into the vestibule.
OR-stapedotomy.step7: Place and secure the prosthesis from incus to fenestra, confirm free piston/ossicular motion, and seal the fenestra as indicated.
OR-stapedotomy.step8: Return the tympanomeatal flap, ensure hemostasis and atraumatic canal packing, and avoid pressure that displaces the prosthesis.

## Tegmen CSF Leak / Encephalocele Repair  (slug: tegmen-repair, region: Otology / Neurotology)

A-tegmen-repair.summary: Spontaneous (often obesity and intracranial hypertension related), chronic-ear, traumatic or iatrogenic defects in the tegmen tympani or tegmen mastoideum let dura, CSF and temporal lobe herniate into the middle ear or mastoid. Repair seals the floor of the middle cranial fossa from above (middle fossa craniotomy), from below (transmastoid), or both; safety depends on the ossicles and facial nerve just under the tegmen tympani, the GSPN and geniculate ganglion anteromedially on the fossa floor, and the temporal lobe and vein of Labbé above.
A-tegmen-repair.layer1: Incision and soft tissue — postauricular or preauricular-temporal skin; temporoparietal fascia / superficial temporal artery; temporalis fascia and muscle; pericranium (vascularized flap option). Plan an incision that serves both a mastoidectomy and a small temporal craniotomy. Preserve temporalis fascia and pericranium as graft or flap material.
A-tegmen-repair.layer2: Transmastoid exposure — mastoid cortex; tegmen mastoideum; sigmoid sinus; antrum, lateral semicircular canal and incus short process. Perform a cortical mastoidectomy and follow the tegmen medially and anteriorly. Defects are often multiple and may be at the tegmen tympani over the attic, out of view from the mastoid.
A-tegmen-repair.layer3: Epitympanum beneath the tegmen tympani — malleus head and incus body; tegmen tympani; tympanic facial nerve below the lateral canal. An encephalocele often sits on the incus or malleus. Resect herniated, non-functional brain only with bipolar at its neck, and protect the ossicles; incus removal may be needed for access.
A-tegmen-repair.layer4: Middle fossa craniotomy (when used) — squamous temporal bone above the zygomatic root; middle meningeal artery branches; middle fossa dura. Center the craniotomy over the zygomatic root, two-thirds anterior and one-third posterior to the canal, with its lower edge near the floor of the fossa.
A-tegmen-repair.layer5: Extradural floor elevation — dura of the temporal lobe; tegmen tympani and mastoideum; arcuate eminence; petrous ridge and superior petrosal sinus; GSPN and facial hiatus (anteromedial); foramen spinosum / middle meningeal artery (anterior). Elevate dura from posterior to anterior. Defects and bony dehiscences are often multiple across the floor. Stop the anterior elevation before the GSPN unless needed, and lift dura off the GSPN rather than pulling the nerve.
A-tegmen-repair.layer6: Multilayer repair — bone (calvarial or cartilage) graft over the defect; fascia inlay and onlay; pericranial or temporalis flap; hydroxyapatite or bone cement (selected). Place a rigid layer extradurally between dura and the floor, with soft-tissue layers. Avoid graft contact with the ossicles to prevent conductive loss.
A-tegmen-repair.layer7: Mastoid and middle ear restoration — ossicular chain; aditus ad antrum; tympanic membrane. Keep the aditus open unless obliterating. Check the drum and chain; clear any CSF and effusion.
A-tegmen-repair.landmark1: Tegmen defect (from below) — Follow the tegmen mastoideum medially from the temporal line; look for pulsating soft tissue, a grey bulge or a pool of clear fluid; examine the tegmen over the attic (Defines the repair and whether a combined approach is needed)
A-tegmen-repair.landmark2: Arcuate eminence — Rounded bump on the middle fossa floor posteromedial to the tegmen, usually overlying the superior semicircular canal (correlation is imperfect) (Posteromedial limit and the marker for the labyrinth)
A-tegmen-repair.landmark3: GSPN and facial hiatus — Anteromedially on the floor, running parallel to the petrous ridge, emerging from the facial hiatus toward the foramen lacerum; often seen as a white strand with an adjacent vessel (Traction injures the geniculate ganglion and facial nerve)
A-tegmen-repair.landmark4: Middle meningeal artery / foramen spinosum — Anterior limit of the floor elevation, posterolateral to foramen ovale (Bleeding source; marks the anterior extent)
A-tegmen-repair.landmark5: Incus and malleus — Directly below the tegmen tympani; through the mastoid, the incus short process at the aditus; from above, through a thin tegmen just lateral to the arcuate eminence (Avoid displacement during repair)
A-tegmen-repair.landmark6: Petrous ridge / superior petrosal sinus — Medial edge of the floor where dura becomes adherent, with the sinus in the tentorial attachment (Medial limit of the floor elevation)
A-tegmen-repair.danger1: Facial nerve and geniculate ganglion — where: Geniculate ganglion may be dehiscent on the fossa floor (in a minority of temporal bones); the tympanic segment lies below the tegmen tympani; protect: Elevate dura posterior to anterior; avoid traction on the GSPN; monitor; injury: Facial paresis
A-tegmen-repair.danger2: Ossicular chain — where: Immediately under the tegmen tympani; protect: Do not wedge graft material onto the incus or malleus; injury: Conductive hearing loss
A-tegmen-repair.danger3: Superior semicircular canal — where: Under the arcuate eminence; protect: Avoid drilling on or near the eminence; injury: SNHL, vertigo
A-tegmen-repair.danger4: Temporal lobe and vein of Labbé — where: Above the dura; the vein of Labbé enters the transverse sinus near the transverse-sigmoid junction; protect: Minimal retraction, CSF release through a lumbar drain if used; injury: Seizures, aphasia (dominant side), venous infarction
A-tegmen-repair.danger5: Sigmoid sinus and superior petrosal sinus — where: Posterior mastoid and petrous ridge; protect: Keep bone over the sinus; gentle dura elevation near the ridge; injury: Bleeding, sinus thrombosis
A-tegmen-repair.danger6: Middle meningeal artery — where: Foramen spinosum and dural grooves; protect: Coagulate before dividing; injury: Epidural hematoma
A-tegmen-repair.variant1: Multiple tegmen defects or a widely thin "honeycomb" tegmen, common in spontaneous leaks
A-tegmen-repair.variant2: Arachnoid granulations or pits on the floor of the middle fossa
A-tegmen-repair.variant3: Coexisting superior semicircular canal dehiscence
A-tegmen-repair.variant4: Dehiscent geniculate ganglion
A-tegmen-repair.variant5: Associated defects at other sites (posterior fossa plate, anterior skull base) with intracranial hypertension
A-tegmen-repair.supply1: Dura of middle fossa — Middle meningeal artery (maxillary via foramen spinosum)
A-tegmen-repair.supply2: Facial nerve near the geniculate — Superficial petrosal branch of the middle meningeal artery
A-tegmen-repair.supply3: Temporalis and pericranial flaps — Deep temporal arteries (temporalis); superficial temporal and posterior auricular branches
A-tegmen-repair.supply4: GSPN — Preganglionic parasympathetic fibers from the facial nerve to the pterygopalatine ganglion (lacrimation)
A-tegmen-repair.pimp1: Q: What is the most common cause of spontaneous temporal bone CSF leak? A: Thinning of the tegmen with raised CSF pressure, often in obese middle-aged women with idiopathic intracranial hypertension
A-tegmen-repair.pimp2: Q: What marker confirms CSF in middle ear or nasal fluid? A: Beta-2 transferrin (or beta-trace protein)
A-tegmen-repair.pimp3: Q: What fibers travel in the GSPN? A: Preganglionic parasympathetic fibers from the superior salivatory nucleus to the pterygopalatine ganglion (lacrimal gland, nasal glands)
A-tegmen-repair.pimp4: Q: What does the GSPN join to form the vidian nerve? A: The deep petrosal nerve (sympathetic), at the foramen lacerum
A-tegmen-repair.pimp5: Q: What lies under the arcuate eminence? A: The superior semicircular canal (approximately)
A-tegmen-repair.pimp6: Q: Which artery passes through foramen spinosum? A: The middle meningeal artery
A-tegmen-repair.pimp7: Q: What is the vein of Labbé? A: The inferior anastomotic vein, draining the temporal lobe surface to the transverse sinus
OR-tegmen-repair.step1: Confirm the diagnosis and map the defect before incision: beta-2 transferrin (or beta-trace) on the fluid, high-resolution temporal-bone CT for the tegmen tympani/mastoideum defect(s), and MRI when an encephalocele or meningoencephalocele is suspected. Look for multiple defects and for elevated intracranial pressure (obesity, empty sella, papilledema) that predisposes to spontaneous leaks and recurrence.
OR-tegmen-repair.step2: Choose the approach from defect site and size: transmastoid for small, posterior or lateral tegmen mastoideum defects; middle-fossa craniotomy for large, multiple or anterior (tegmen tympani) defects and for encephaloceles; a combined transmastoid/middle-fossa approach when exposure from below alone is inadequate.
OR-tegmen-repair.step3: Transmastoid: perform a cortical mastoidectomy, thin the tegmen to identify the defect and any herniated dura/brain, and protect the ossicular chain (incus body and short process in the attic) and the facial nerve. Avoid drilling on exposed dura.
OR-tegmen-repair.step4: Middle fossa: through a temporal craniotomy above the zygomatic root, elevate the temporal lobe dura extradurally from posterior to anterior, identify the defect(s), and protect the greater superficial petrosal nerve and a possibly dehiscent geniculate ganglion anteromedially and the middle meningeal artery at the foramen spinosum.
OR-tegmen-repair.step5: Manage herniated tissue: amputate non-functional herniated brain/gliotic tissue at the defect with bipolar cautery rather than forcing it back, and repair dural tears primarily or with graft.
OR-tegmen-repair.step6: Reconstruct in layers with broad overlap: an intracranial (dural-side) fascia or dural-substitute layer, a rigid layer (bone, cartilage or calvarial graft) across the bony defect, and an extracranial fascia/temporalis or pedicled flap layer, with tissue sealant as an adjunct.
OR-tegmen-repair.step7: Close and plan aftercare: restore the mastoid/middle-ear space as needed, consider lumbar drainage selectively, and evaluate and treat elevated intracranial pressure (weight loss, acetazolamide, neuro-ophthalmology) to lower recurrence risk.

## Translabyrinthine Skull-Base Approach  (slug: translabyrinthine-skull-base, region: Otology / Neurotology)

A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.summary: The translabyrinthine approach removes the mastoid and the entire bony labyrinth to reach the internal auditory canal (IAC) and cerebellopontine angle from lateral, with almost no cerebellar retraction. Hearing on that side is lost. The facial nerve can be identified at the fundus, lateral to the tumor. Safety depends on keeping the fallopian canal intact as the labyrinth is drilled around the second genu and mastoid segment, and on respecting the sigmoid sinus and jugular bulb that frame the corridor.
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.layer1: Incision and flap — postauricular skin; temporalis fascia; musculoperiosteal (Palva-type) flap; spine of Henle; mastoid cortex. Use a wide C-shaped postauricular incision placed further posterior than for a routine mastoid so the sigmoid sinus and posterior fossa dura can be exposed. Keep a vascularized musculoperiosteal flap for closure.
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.layer2: Complete mastoidectomy — tegmen mastoideum (superior); sigmoid sinus (posterior); sinodural angle (Citelli); posterior bony EAC wall (anterior, thinned but intact); antrum and lateral semicircular canal; short process of incus; digastric ridge. Saucerize widely. Thin the bone over the middle fossa dura and the sigmoid. Identify the lateral canal in the antrum floor and the incus in the fossa incudis; together they give the depth and orientation of the facial nerve.
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.layer3: Decompress sigmoid sinus and posterior fossa dura — sigmoid sinus; superior petrosal sinus (along the upper petrous ridge); presigmoid posterior fossa dura; endolymphatic sac (inferior to Donaldson's line); Trautmann triangle. Remove bone a few mm behind the sigmoid; a thin island of bone (Bill's island) may be left on the sinus. Skeletonize the presigmoid dura medially toward the IAC. The superior petrosal sinus runs along the junction of the middle and posterior fossa dura and marks the upper edge of the corridor.
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.layer4: Skeletonize the mastoid facial nerve — second genu (below and posterior to the lateral canal); mastoid segment of the facial nerve; digastric ridge pointing to the stylomastoid foramen; facial recess and retrofacial air cells. Leave a thin shell of fallopian canal bone; do not uncover the sheath. The nerve runs anterior and lateral to the posterior canal, so the mastoid segment forms the anterior wall of the labyrinthectomy.
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.layer5: Labyrinthectomy — lateral SCC; posterior SCC (its ampulla lies inferior, near the mastoid facial nerve); superior SCC and subarcuate artery (center of the arc); crus commune; ampullae of the lateral and superior canals (anterior, near the tympanic and labyrinthine facial segments). Open the lateral canal first and follow it posteriorly to the posterior canal, then the crus commune and superior canal. Leave the anterior lateral-canal ampulla bone for last; it lies just above the tympanic facial segment and second genu.
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.layer6: Vestibule — vestibule (medial to the oval window, deep to the lateral canal ampulla); saccular (spherical) and utricular (elliptical) recesses; medial wall of vestibule (backs onto the IAC fundus). Opening the vestibule shows how deep the IAC is: its medial wall is the lateral end of the canal. Stay posterior to the second genu and below the labyrinthine facial segment.
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.layer7: Skeletonize the IAC — superior trough (between IAC and middle fossa dura); inferior trough (between IAC and jugular bulb); cochlear aqueduct (inferior limit); IAC dura (porus to fundus, about 270 degrees); transverse crest and Bill's bar at the fundus. Drill the troughs above and below with a diamond burr until the IAC is a blue-lined cylinder. Work medial to lateral toward the fundus, where the bone is densest and the labyrinthine facial nerve lies anterosuperior. The cochlear aqueduct marks the safe inferior limit; CN IX lies just medial and inferior to it.
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.layer8: Dural opening and tumor removal — superior vestibular nerve (posterior to Bill's bar); facial nerve (anterosuperior at the fundus; anterior on the tumor medially); cochlear nerve; AICA; brainstem; lower cranial nerves. Find the facial nerve at the fundus by reflecting the superior vestibular nerve inferiorly and posteriorly off Bill's bar. Debulk and then dissect the capsule off VII medially, in small segments with stimulation.
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.layer9: Closure — incus and tensor tympani region; aditus / Eustachian tube orifice; abdominal fat graft; musculoperiosteal flap. Remove the incus and pack the aditus and Eustachian tube (muscle or fascia, sometimes bone wax). Fill the defect with abdominal fat and close in layers to prevent CSF otorhinorrhea.
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.landmark1: Lateral semicircular canal — Dense, smooth, yellow-white bone in the antrum floor, medial to the short process of the incus (Sets the depth and plane of the facial nerve and the start of the labyrinthectomy)
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.landmark2: Mastoid facial nerve / second genu — Below and slightly anterior to the lateral canal, medial to the short process of the incus. It descends along a line from the inferior lateral canal to the anterior end of the digastric ridge. (Forms the anterior boundary of the labyrinthectomy and the IAC exposure)
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.landmark3: Endolymphatic sac — Thickened dura between the sigmoid and the posterior canal, inferior to Donaldson's line (the plane of the lateral canal extended posteriorly through the posterior canal) (Confirms the presigmoid dura and the posterior canal position)
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.landmark4: Subarcuate artery — Runs through the center of the superior canal arc in the petromastoid canal toward the posterior fossa dura (Bleeds when drilled; marks the arc of the superior canal)
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.landmark5: IAC — Deep to the vestibule, running roughly parallel to the EAC in the axial plane. Define it by drilling troughs above (toward the middle fossa dura) and below (toward the jugular bulb), then thin the bone to blue. (Must be skeletonized before the dura is opened)
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.landmark6: Bill's bar — Vertical crest at the superior fundus. The facial nerve is anterior to it and the superior vestibular nerve posterior. (The most reliable place to identify VII before tumor dissection)
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.landmark7: Cochlear aqueduct — Small channel inferior to the IAC that opens medially near the jugular foramen and may release CSF when opened (Marks the inferior limit of drilling; CN IX lies just below)
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.danger1: Facial nerve (mastoid segment, second genu, labyrinthine segment) — where: Anterior wall of the labyrinthectomy, and anterosuperior at the fundus within the narrow labyrinthine canal; protect: Leave fallopian canal bone, use a large diamond burr with copious irrigation parallel to the nerve, and identify at Bill's bar before tumor dissection; injury: Facial paralysis; heat injury causes delayed palsy
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.danger2: Sigmoid sinus and jugular bulb — where: Posterior and inferior to the corridor; a high bulb can reach the IAC floor; protect: Skeletonize with diamond, leave Bill's island or eggshell bone, and press down a high bulb with bone wax or Surgicel; injury: Hemorrhage, air embolism, and loss of inferior exposure
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.danger3: Superior petrosal sinus and middle fossa dura — where: Along the upper petrous ridge and the sinodural angle; protect: Gentle decompression and bipolar or packing for bleeding; injury: Venous bleeding, temporal lobe retraction injury
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.danger4: Petrous ICA — where: Anteromedial to the cochlea; not exposed in the standard corridor, only in transotic or transcochlear extensions; protect: Stay posterior to the cochlea and do not drill anterior to the fundus without intent; injury: Catastrophic hemorrhage, stroke
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.danger5: AICA, brainstem and lower cranial nerves — where: Medial CPA; IX-XI inferior near the jugular foramen and cochlear aqueduct; protect: Sharp arachnoid dissection, keep the inferior drilling above the cochlear aqueduct, and low bipolar settings; injury: Brainstem infarct, dysphagia, aspiration, hoarseness
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.danger6: CSF pathway via the middle ear — where: Aditus, Eustachian tube, perilabyrinthine and petrous apex cells; protect: Remove the incus, pack the aditus and Eustachian tube, wax cells, and place a fat graft; injury: CSF otorhinorrhea, meningitis
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.variant1: Anteriorly placed (forward) sigmoid sinus or contracted sclerotic mastoid, which narrows the presigmoid corridor
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.variant2: High or dehiscent jugular bulb approaching the IAC
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.variant3: Low-lying middle fossa dura / tegmen
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.variant4: Anomalous or dehiscent facial nerve course
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.variant5: Large mastoid emissary vein or prominent subarcuate artery
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.supply1: Arterial — Labyrinthine artery (usually from AICA) to the inner ear; subarcuate artery; AICA in the CPA; stylomastoid artery to the mastoid facial nerve
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.supply2: Venous — Sigmoid sinus, superior petrosal sinus, jugular bulb, mastoid emissary vein
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.supply3: Neural (IAC) — VII anterosuperior, cochlear anteroinferior, SVN and IVN posterior; nervus intermedius between VII and VIII
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.supply4: Facial nerve segments — Meatal, labyrinthine (narrowest), geniculate, tympanic, second genu, mastoid
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.pimp1: Q: Define Donaldson's line and why it matters. A: The plane of the lateral canal extended posteriorly, bisecting the posterior canal. The endolymphatic sac lies inferior to it.
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.pimp2: Q: What are the boundaries of Trautmann triangle? A: Sigmoid sinus (posterior), superior petrosal sinus / middle fossa dura (superior) and the bony labyrinth / posterior canal (anterior)
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.pimp3: Q: What forms the crus commune? A: The non-ampullated ends of the superior and posterior canals
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.pimp4: Q: Which canal ampullae lie closest to the tympanic and labyrinthine facial nerve? A: The lateral and superior canal ampullae, anteriorly
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.pimp5: Q: Narrowest segment of the fallopian canal? A: Labyrinthine segment, at the meatal foramen
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.pimp6: Q: How do you identify the facial nerve at the fundus? A: Bill's bar: VII lies anterior to it; reflect the superior vestibular nerve posteriorly and inferiorly
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.pimp7: Q: What is the inferior limit of drilling, and what lies just beyond it? A: The cochlear aqueduct; CN IX at the jugular foramen
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.pimp8: Q: Why remove the incus and pack the Eustachian tube? A: To block CSF from the middle ear reaching the nasopharynx
A-translabyrinthine-skull-base.pimp9: Q: What runs through the arc of the superior canal? A: The subarcuate artery in the petromastoid canal
OR-translabyrinthine-skull-base.step1: Perform a wide cortical mastoidectomy and identify tegmen, sigmoid sinus, posterior canal wall, facial nerve, and all three semicircular canals.
OR-translabyrinthine-skull-base.step2: Skeletonize the sigmoid sinus and middle/posterior fossa dura as needed, then exenterate the labyrinth deliberately because hearing preservation is not possible with this corridor.
OR-translabyrinthine-skull-base.step3: Identify the vestibule and follow the labyrinthine anatomy toward the internal auditory canal while preserving the facial nerve.
OR-translabyrinthine-skull-base.step4: Skeletonize the IAC circumferentially to the fundus with diamond drilling, protecting the facial nerve and avoiding uncontrolled dural entry.
OR-translabyrinthine-skull-base.step5: Open the IAC/posterior-fossa dura, identify the facial nerve, and internally debulk the vestibular schwannoma or other target before capsule dissection.
OR-translabyrinthine-skull-base.step6: Dissect tumor from the facial nerve and brainstem in small segments with stimulation, accepting deliberate residual tumor if the nerve is inseparable and preservation is the priority.
OR-translabyrinthine-skull-base.step7: Obliterate the Eustachian tube/middle-ear pathway and exposed petrous air cells as required, then pack the mastoid defect with abdominal fat or planned reconstruction.
OR-translabyrinthine-skull-base.step8: Achieve watertight closure and document immediate facial-nerve function, CSF-leak surveillance, vestibular rehabilitation, and wound monitoring.

## Myringoplasty / Tympanoplasty  (slug: tympanoplasty, region: Otology / Neurotology)

A-tympanoplasty.summary: Tympanoplasty closes a tympanic membrane perforation (and, in type I and above, restores the middle-ear sound path) with a graft that must contact a vascularized remnant. Safety depends on raising the tympanomeatal flap in the subperiosteal plane, lifting the annulus out of its sulcus without tearing it, and recognizing the chorda tympani and incus just under the posterosuperior canal wall; success depends most on anterior graft support, where the anterior canal bulge and anterior sulcus are hard to see.
A-tympanoplasty.layer1: Approach and canal injection — postauricular skin, temporalis fascia and periosteum (postauricular); incisura terminalis between tragus and helix (endaural); vascular strip (endaural/postauricular); bony canal skin. Injection of the four canal quadrants at the bone-cartilage junction blanches the skin. Harvest temporalis fascia lateral to the muscle, above the linea temporalis, before canal work.
A-tympanoplasty.layer2: Freshen the perforation — squamous epithelium rolled over the perforation edge; fibrous layer; mucosal undersurface. Remove a rim of the edge and the undersurface epithelium so the graft meets raw, vascularized drum.
A-tympanoplasty.layer3: Tympanomeatal flap — canal skin with periosteum; posterior bony canal; tympanic annulus (fibrocartilaginous) in the tympanic sulcus. Incise canal skin about 5 to 8 mm lateral to the annulus, along the posterior canal wall from about the 12 o'clock to the 6 o'clock position. Elevate on bone with a round knife, staying under periosteum, toward the annulus.
A-tympanoplasty.layer4: Entering the middle ear — annulus lifted from the sulcus; middle-ear mucosa; chorda tympani emerging from the iter chordae posterius at the posterosuperior sulcus. Enter at the posterior-inferior annulus where the chorda is not, then raise the annulus superiorly. The chorda emerges from bone just medial to the posterosuperior annulus and crosses under the malleus neck.
A-tympanoplasty.layer5: Middle ear inspection — incus long process and lenticular process; stapes and stapedius tendon; round window niche; promontory with Jacobson nerve; Eustachian tube orifice in the protympanum. Test chain mobility by gently palpating the malleus while watching the round-window reflex. Remove the posterosuperior scutum with a curette only if needed to see the incudostapedial joint.
A-tympanoplasty.layer6: Graft support and placement — protympanum and Eustachian tube (anterior); malleus manubrium; anterior sulcus and anterior canal wall; drum remnant. Underlay: graft medial to the remnant and malleus (or lateral to the manubrium), supported by absorbable packing in the middle ear. Keep packing out of the ET and round window niche. Tuck the graft anteriorly under the remnant or annulus for anterior support.
A-tympanoplasty.layer7: Flap replacement and canal packing — tympanomeatal flap; canal skin incisions; lateral canal. Return the flap so the annulus sits back in its sulcus without folds. Blunting at the anterior sulcus and lateralization of the graft are the classic anatomic failures.
A-tympanoplasty.landmark1: Tympanic annulus and sulcus — Elevate the flap medially on bone until the grey, firm fibrous ring appears sitting in its bony groove; lift it with a fine pick inferiorly first (Correct entry into the middle ear without tearing the drum)
A-tympanoplasty.landmark2: Chorda tympani — Posterosuperior, just medial to the annulus (about 12 to 3 o'clock in a left ear, 9 to 12 in a right ear); it runs medial to the drum, lateral to the incus long process, and medial to the malleus neck (Preserve for taste; it is often stretched over the scutum when exposing the incus)
A-tympanoplasty.landmark3: Malleus manubrium and lateral process — White handle embedded in the drum; the lateral process is the first bump seen at the upper canal (Graft anchor; decides underlay medial or lateral to the handle)
A-tympanoplasty.landmark4: Incus long process and incudostapedial joint — Behind and deep to the posterosuperior scutum; curette a little of the scutum lateral to the chorda to see it (Assess chain integrity and avoid unintentional dislocation)
A-tympanoplasty.landmark5: Eustachian tube orifice — Anterosuperior protympanum, medial to the anterior annulus, above and in front of the promontory (Do not obstruct it with packing; it is the aeration source for the graft)
A-tympanoplasty.landmark6: Round window niche — Posteroinferior to the promontory, below and behind the stapes, partly under the subiculum (Keep it free of packing; site for the round-window reflex)
A-tympanoplasty.danger1: Chorda tympani — where: Posterosuperior quadrant, crossing the middle ear between incus and malleus, just medial to the drum; protect: Elevate the annulus inferiorly first; curette scutum away from it; mobilize rather than cut; injury: Ipsilateral metallic taste or dysgeusia, usually temporary if stretched, permanent if divided
A-tympanoplasty.danger2: Facial nerve, tympanic segment — where: Runs above the oval window and stapes, just superior to the cochleariform process anteriorly; dehiscence is found in about half of temporal bones on histology (about 55 percent in the Baxter and Moreano series) but is recognized far less often at surgery (roughly 5 to 30 percent in surgical series); protect: No blind instrumentation above the stapes; keep the suction off exposed nerve; injury: Facial paresis or paralysis
A-tympanoplasty.danger3: Stapes and inner ear — where: Posterosuperior mesotympanum, deep to the incus long process; protect: Avoid manipulation of the incus or stapes while removing adhesions; do not suction near the oval window; injury: Sensorineural hearing loss, vertigo
A-tympanoplasty.danger4: Jugular bulb — where: Hypotympanum floor; can be high or dehiscent; protect: Gentle elevation of the inferior annulus; look for blue mucosa; injury: Venous bleeding
A-tympanoplasty.danger5: Temporomandibular joint and anterior canal wall — where: Directly anterior to the bony canal; protect: When widening for an anterior bulge, drill carefully and watch for joint capsule; injury: TMJ exposure, pain, herniation into the canal
A-tympanoplasty.variant1: Dehiscent tympanic facial nerve over the oval window
A-tympanoplasty.variant2: Prominent anterior canal wall bulge hiding the anterior annulus, often requiring canalplasty for anterior grafting
A-tympanoplasty.variant3: High or dehiscent jugular bulb
A-tympanoplasty.variant4: Chorda tympani taking a high or anomalous course
A-tympanoplasty.variant5: Pre-existing ossicular erosion (most commonly the incus long process) or tympanosclerosis fixing the chain
A-tympanoplasty.supply1: TM and canal arteries — Deep auricular and anterior tympanic (maxillary artery); stylomastoid (posterior auricular); the vascular strip carries supply from superficial temporal and posterior auricular branches
A-tympanoplasty.supply2: Middle ear sensation — Jacobson nerve (CN IX) through the tympanic plexus on the promontory
A-tympanoplasty.supply3: Canal sensation — Auriculotemporal (V3) anterior and superior; Arnold nerve (X) posterior and inferior; facial nerve sensory fibers posterior
A-tympanoplasty.supply4: Taste — Chorda tympani (VII, nervus intermedius) to the anterior two-thirds of the tongue; also preganglionic parasympathetic to submandibular and sublingual glands
A-tympanoplasty.pimp1: Q: Describe the Wullstein tympanoplasty types I through V. A: I: graft on intact chain; II: graft on incus or malleus remnant; III: graft on stapes head (myringostapediopexy); IV: graft on mobile footplate with round window shielded; V: fenestration of the horizontal canal (Va) or stapedectomy (Vb) for fixed footplate
A-tympanoplasty.pimp2: Q: Where does the chorda tympani enter and exit the middle ear? A: Enters through the iter chordae posterius (posterior canaliculus); exits anteriorly through the iter chordae anterius / petrotympanic (Glaserian) fissure
A-tympanoplasty.pimp3: Q: Which part of the ossicular chain is most often eroded in chronic otitis media? A: The long process (lenticular process) of the incus, because of its tenuous blood supply
A-tympanoplasty.pimp4: Q: What is the lateral boundary of the facial recess? A: The chorda tympani (medial is the facial nerve, superior the incus buttress / fossa incudis)
A-tympanoplasty.pimp5: Q: What is Jacobson's nerve and where does it lie? A: Tympanic branch of CN IX on the promontory; it carries parasympathetic fibers that become the lesser petrosal nerve to the otic ganglion (parotid)
A-tympanoplasty.pimp6: Q: Which fascia is used most often and where is it harvested? A: Temporalis fascia, deep (true) temporal fascia above the linea temporalis
A-tympanoplasty.pimp7: Q: What causes lateralization of a graft? A: Loss of contact with the malleus or anterior annulus support, typically with an overlay graft and anterior sulcus blunting
A-tympanoplasty.pimp8: Q: Where are the iter chordae and why do they matter in flap elevation? A: Posterosuperior bony sulcus; the chorda emerges there, just deep to the elevated annulus
OR-tympanoplasty.step1: Freshen the perforation edge and choose transcanal/endaural/postauricular exposure that gives complete visualization of the perforation and annulus.
OR-tympanoplasty.step2: Elevate the tympanomeatal flap in the correct canal-wall plane and lift the annulus to enter the middle ear without tearing remaining drum.
OR-tympanoplasty.step3: Inspect the middle ear, ossicular chain, chorda tympani, and disease; address adhesions or ossicular problems only as indicated.
OR-tympanoplasty.step4: Prepare the graft and middle-ear support so the graft will contact a vascularized drum remnant without blocking the Eustachian tube or round window.
OR-tympanoplasty.step5: Place the graft using the planned underlay/overlay/cartilage technique, ensuring secure anterior and inferior support where failures commonly occur.
OR-tympanoplasty.step6: Return the tympanomeatal flap over the graft and verify that the graft remains flat, medial/lateral as intended, and free of folds.
OR-tympanoplasty.step7: Support the reconstruction with minimal middle-ear/canal packing needed to prevent migration.
OR-tympanoplasty.step8: Confirm canal hemostasis and close the approach without creating canal stenosis or pressure injury.

## Vestibular Schwannoma Approach Planning  (slug: vestibular-schwannoma, region: Otology / Neurotology)

A-vestibular-schwannoma.summary: Vestibular schwannomas arise from the vestibular nerves (the inferior more often in modern series) in the IAC and grow medially into the cerebellopontine angle, displacing the facial nerve, usually anteriorly. The approach (retrosigmoid, translabyrinthine or middle fossa) is chosen by tumor size, hearing status and how far laterally the tumor fills the IAC fundus; in every approach the decisive relationships are the facial nerve's position on the tumor capsule, the fundus anatomy (Bill's bar, transverse crest), and the AICA and lower cranial nerves at the CPA.
A-vestibular-schwannoma.layer1: Retrosigmoid exposure — asterion (junction of lambdoid, parietomastoid and occipitomastoid sutures); transverse-sigmoid junction; posterior fossa dura; mastoid emissary vein. The asterion approximates the transverse-sigmoid junction (variable); the craniotomy lies behind the sigmoid and below the transverse sinus. Open the cisterna magna to release CSF and let the cerebellum fall away.
A-vestibular-schwannoma.layer2: Translabyrinthine exposure — complete mastoidectomy with sigmoid decompression; lateral, posterior and superior semicircular canals; vestibule; IAC skeletonized 270 degrees; presigmoid dura (Trautmann triangle). Labyrinthectomy is done with the facial nerve in view: the lateral canal sits over the second genu, the posterior canal's ampulla lies medial to the mastoid facial nerve, and the superior canal ampulla lies near the labyrinthine facial segment.
A-vestibular-schwannoma.layer3: Middle fossa exposure — temporal craniotomy; arcuate eminence; GSPN; petrous ridge; IAC roof. Used for small intracanalicular tumors with useful hearing. The IAC lies roughly along the bisector of the angle (about 120 degrees) between the GSPN and the superior canal / arcuate eminence (meatal plane); confirm by finding it medially near the porus first.
A-vestibular-schwannoma.layer4: Cerebellopontine angle — tumor capsule; petrosal (Dandy) vein; trigeminal nerve (superior); lower cranial nerves IX, X, XI (inferior); AICA loop; flocculus and choroid plexus near the foramen of Luschka. CN IX to XI lie at the inferior pole; CN V lies above. The AICA loop can enter the IAC or adhere to the capsule; its labyrinthine branch supplies the inner ear.
A-vestibular-schwannoma.layer5: Internal debulking and capsule dissection — arachnoid plane; facial nerve (usually anterior or anterosuperior); cochlear nerve (anteroinferior); brainstem root entry zone. Find the facial nerve at the brainstem (root exit zone just anterior to CN VIII at the pontomedullary junction) and at the fundus, then dissect toward the porus, where the nerve is thinnest and most adherent.
A-vestibular-schwannoma.layer6: IAC and fundus — transverse (falciform) crest; vertical crest (Bill's bar); superior and inferior vestibular nerves; facial and cochlear nerves. Facial nerve: anterior-superior. Cochlear: anterior-inferior. Superior vestibular: posterior-superior. Inferior vestibular: posterior-inferior. Drill the posterior IAC wall (retrosigmoid) only lateral as far as the labyrinth allows: the posterior canal and the vestibular aqueduct/common crus lie close to the posterior lip.
A-vestibular-schwannoma.layer7: Closure — dura; petrous and mastoid air cells; abdominal fat graft (translab); Eustachian tube / aditus. Wax opened retrosigmoid and perimeatal air cells; in a translabyrinthine closure, pack the aditus and ET and obliterate the cavity with fat.
A-vestibular-schwannoma.landmark1: Transverse-sigmoid junction — Approximated by the asterion; confirm with a burr hole just below and medial to it, or with image guidance (Defines the superior-anterior corner of a retrosigmoid craniotomy)
A-vestibular-schwannoma.landmark2: Facial nerve at the brainstem — At the pontomedullary junction, anterior to the CN VIII root, about 1 to 2 mm anterosuperior to its entry; the flocculus and choroid plexus of the lateral recess lie just behind (Medial anchor for dissection)
A-vestibular-schwannoma.landmark3: Bill's bar — At the lateral IAC fundus above the transverse crest, separating facial (anterior) from superior vestibular (posterior) nerves (Identify the facial nerve laterally: lift the superior vestibular nerve and the facial nerve lies anterior to Bill's bar)
A-vestibular-schwannoma.landmark4: IAC on the middle fossa floor — Medially near the porus, roughly along the bisector of the roughly 120-degree angle between the GSPN and the superior canal (Garcia-Ibanez); Fisch places it about 60 degrees anterior to the long axis of the blue-lined superior canal / arcuate eminence (the true angle varies, about 35 to 75 degrees); House follows the GSPN back to the geniculate and the labyrinthine segment to the IAC; confirm before drilling laterally (Labyrinthine facial segment and cochlea are tightly packed laterally)
A-vestibular-schwannoma.landmark5: Lower cranial nerves — At the inferior pole of the tumor, entering the jugular foramen (Protect during debulking of the inferior pole)
A-vestibular-schwannoma.landmark6: Labyrinth (retrosigmoid) — Posterior semicircular canal and common crus lie lateral to the posterior lip of the porus; the vestibular aqueduct/endolymphatic sac is inferolateral (Limit of posterior IAC drilling for hearing preservation)
A-vestibular-schwannoma.danger1: Facial nerve — where: Usually anterior to the tumor (also anterosuperior or anteroinferior), flattened and thin at the porus; protect: Monitoring and stimulation; identify at the brainstem and fundus; leave a remnant if adherent; injury: Facial paralysis
A-vestibular-schwannoma.danger2: Cochlear nerve and labyrinthine artery — where: Anteroinferior IAC; the labyrinthine artery is a branch of AICA running in the IAC; protect: Sharp dissection toward the fundus; avoid cautery on the vascular loop; injury: Hearing loss
A-vestibular-schwannoma.danger3: AICA — where: CPA; may loop into the IAC; protect: Dissect off the capsule; do not coagulate arteries on the brainstem; injury: Brainstem / cerebellar infarction
A-vestibular-schwannoma.danger4: Lower cranial nerves (IX, X, XI) — where: Inferior pole of the tumor; protect: Gentle retraction, arachnoid preservation; injury: Dysphagia, aspiration, hoarseness
A-vestibular-schwannoma.danger5: Sigmoid and transverse sinus; vein of Labbé — where: Edge of the craniotomy or mastoid; protect: Bone removal over the sinus with diamond burr; protect the vein of Labbé near the transverse-sigmoid junction; injury: Venous infarction, bleeding, air embolism
A-vestibular-schwannoma.danger6: Labyrinth (in hearing preservation) — where: Posterior IAC lip, superior canal under the middle fossa floor; protect: Limit drilling; use image-guided measurements; injury: Deafness
A-vestibular-schwannoma.danger7: Petrosal (Dandy) vein and trigeminal nerve — where: Superior CPA; protect: Preserve the vein where possible; injury: Venous infarction, facial numbness
A-vestibular-schwannoma.variant1: High jugular bulb limiting translabyrinthine or retrosigmoid exposure of the inferior IAC
A-vestibular-schwannoma.variant2: Anterior sigmoid sinus or low tegmen reducing the translabyrinthine corridor
A-vestibular-schwannoma.variant3: Facial nerve displaced superiorly or inferiorly, or splayed thin over the capsule
A-vestibular-schwannoma.variant4: AICA loop entering the IAC
A-vestibular-schwannoma.variant5: Cystic tumors that adhere more tightly to nerves
A-vestibular-schwannoma.supply1: CPA arteries — AICA (from the basilar artery) with the labyrinthine artery to the inner ear; PICA lower
A-vestibular-schwannoma.supply2: Venous — Petrosal (Dandy) vein to the superior petrosal sinus; sigmoid and transverse sinuses
A-vestibular-schwannoma.supply3: Facial nerve — Labyrinthine artery in the IAC; superficial petrosal artery at the geniculate
A-vestibular-schwannoma.supply4: IAC nerve quadrants — Facial anterior-superior, cochlear anterior-inferior, superior vestibular posterior-superior, inferior vestibular posterior-inferior
A-vestibular-schwannoma.pimp1: Q: Which nerve most often gives rise to vestibular schwannoma? A: The inferior vestibular nerve in most modern surgical series (about 90 percent in Khrais et al. 2008); older teaching emphasized the superior, and some series find a more even split
A-vestibular-schwannoma.pimp2: Q: What is the Obersteiner-Redlich zone? A: The transition from central (glial) to peripheral (Schwann cell) myelin on the nerve, usually near the porus or medial IAC; classic teaching placed tumor origin there, but histologic studies show most tumors arise more laterally in the IAC
A-vestibular-schwannoma.pimp3: Q: Where does the facial nerve usually lie relative to the tumor? A: Anterior (anterosuperior most often)
A-vestibular-schwannoma.pimp4: Q: Describe the IAC fundus quadrants. A: 'Seven-up, Coke down': facial anterior-superior, cochlear anterior-inferior; vestibular nerves posterior (superior and inferior)
A-vestibular-schwannoma.pimp5: Q: What separates superior from inferior structures at the fundus? A: The transverse (falciform) crest
A-vestibular-schwannoma.pimp6: Q: What is Trautmann's triangle? A: Presigmoid posterior fossa dura bounded by the superior petrosal sinus, sigmoid sinus and bony labyrinth
A-vestibular-schwannoma.pimp7: Q: Which approach sacrifices hearing? A: Translabyrinthine; retrosigmoid and middle fossa can preserve it
A-vestibular-schwannoma.pimp8: Q: What clinical sign reflects facial nerve sensory fiber compression? A: Hitselberger sign: numbness of the posterior-superior canal wall
A-vestibular-schwannoma.pimp9: Q: What artery supplies the inner ear? A: Labyrinthine artery, usually from AICA
OR-vestibular-schwannoma.step1: Position and expose according to the chosen retrosigmoid, translabyrinthine, or middle-fossa corridor, confirming hearing-preservation intent before irreversible labyrinthine work.
OR-vestibular-schwannoma.step2: Obtain the bony corridor with constant orientation to sigmoid/transverse sinus, labyrinth, IAC, facial nerve, and lower cranial nerves as relevant to approach.
OR-vestibular-schwannoma.step3: Open dura and release CSF to achieve cerebellar/temporal-lobe relaxation rather than relying on fixed retraction.
OR-vestibular-schwannoma.step4: Identify the tumor–nerve interface and internally debulk large tumor before circumferential capsule dissection.
OR-vestibular-schwannoma.step5: Dissect the capsule from brainstem, cochlear nerve, and facial nerve in small segments, using stimulation and preserving the facial-nerve vascular supply.
OR-vestibular-schwannoma.step6: Open the IAC to the required extent and remove the intracanalicular component while protecting labyrinth and fundal nerves when hearing preservation is intended.
OR-vestibular-schwannoma.step7: Inspect for residual tumor in the fundus/brainstem interface and decide deliberately if a tiny adherent remnant is safer than nerve injury.
OR-vestibular-schwannoma.step8: Achieve watertight dural/air-cell closure, obliterate opened petrous air cells, and complete approach-specific reconstruction.

## Endoscopic CSF Leak Repair / Nasoseptal Flap  (slug: csf-nasoseptal, region: Rhinology / Skull Base)

A-csf-nasoseptal.summary: Anterior skull-base CSF leaks occur where the bone is thinnest (lateral lamella of the cribriform plate, the ethmoid roof, and the lateral recess of a well-pneumatized sphenoid), and are closed endoscopically with multilayer grafts or a vascularized nasoseptal flap. Safety depends on recognizing the lateral lamella and anterior ethmoid artery in the ethmoid roof, the optic nerve and ICA on the lateral sphenoid wall, and on preserving the posterior septal artery at the sphenoid face before the flap is needed.
A-csf-nasoseptal.layer1: Nasal cavity survey — septum; inferior and middle turbinates; middle meatus; sphenoethmoidal recess and natural sphenoid ostium; choana. Decongest and find the natural sphenoid ostium medial to the superior turbinate, about 1 to 1.5 cm above the choana. This also locates the flap pedicle, which runs below the ostium.
A-csf-nasoseptal.layer2: Nasoseptal flap harvest — septal mucoperichondrium / mucoperiosteum; posterior septal artery (branch of sphenopalatine); rostrum of sphenoid; olfactory strip (upper 1 to 2 cm of septum). Superior incision about 1 to 2 cm below the septal roof to spare olfactory epithelium; inferior incision along the maxillary crest or nasal floor; anterior vertical cut as needed. Pedicle lies between the sphenoid ostium and the choana on the sphenoid face. Store the flap in the nasopharynx or maxillary sinus.
A-csf-nasoseptal.layer3: Ethmoidectomy and skull-base exposure — uncinate process and ethmoid bulla; basal lamella of middle turbinate; lamina papyracea (lateral); ethmoid roof (fovea ethmoidalis); lateral lamella of the cribriform plate (medial); anterior and posterior ethmoid arteries. Dissect the skull base from posterior to anterior, where it is highest posteriorly in the sphenoid and descends anteriorly. The lateral lamella is the thinnest part of the skull base and the commonest iatrogenic leak site.
A-csf-nasoseptal.layer4: Sphenoidotomy — sphenoid face and intersinus septum; sella; optic canal (superolateral); ICA prominence (lateral, paraclival and parasellar); opticocarotid recess; lateral recess (beyond V2 and the vidian canal); planum sphenoidale. Intersinus septa often insert onto the carotid canal; never twist or rongeur them off forcibly. The lateral recess requires a transpterygoid approach when the defect lies lateral to the foramen rotundum line.
A-csf-nasoseptal.layer5: Defect preparation — defect margins; dura; encephalocele / meningocele neck; surrounding mucosa. Remove 5 to 10 mm of mucosa around the defect so grafts adhere to bone. Reduce encephaloceles with bipolar at the neck after controlling vessels.
A-csf-nasoseptal.layer6: Reconstruction — inlay graft (intradural or extradural); onlay graft; nasoseptal flap on bare bone; fat or fascia. The flap must lie on bone without mucosa beneath it, without pedicle twist, and with the periosteal side against the defect.
A-csf-nasoseptal.landmark1: Sphenoid natural ostium — Sphenoethmoidal recess medial to the superior turbinate, roughly 1 to 1.5 cm above the choanal arch along the sphenoid face (Entry to the sphenoid and the upper limit of the flap pedicle)
A-csf-nasoseptal.landmark2: Posterior septal artery — Crosses the sphenoid face between the ostium and the choana, running from the sphenopalatine foramen medially (The flap pedicle; preserve during sphenoidotomy (open the ostium superiorly and medially, not inferiorly))
A-csf-nasoseptal.landmark3: Anterior ethmoid artery — In the ethmoid roof behind the frontal recess, about one cell behind the bulla lamella / anterior to the basal lamella; it runs posterolateral to anteromedial and may hang in a mesentery below the roof (Avoid transection and orbital hematoma; marks the anterior limit of many defects)
A-csf-nasoseptal.landmark4: Lateral lamella of the cribriform plate — Vertical thin plate at the medial ethmoid roof, lateral to the middle turbinate attachment; its height relates to Keros type (Commonest leak site; avoid medial dissection here)
A-csf-nasoseptal.landmark5: Optic nerve and ICA in the sphenoid — Optic canal at the superolateral sphenoid wall; ICA below and behind it; the opticocarotid recess (lateral OCR) lies between them (Protect when exposing posterior and lateral defects)
A-csf-nasoseptal.landmark6: Foramen rotundum / vidian canal — V2 in the lateral sphenoid wall above the vidian canal; the vidian canal leads posteriorly to the anterior genu of the petrous ICA (Lateral recess defects lie lateral to this line; the vidian canal guides ICA localization in transpterygoid work)
A-csf-nasoseptal.danger1: Internal carotid artery — where: Lateral sphenoid wall, parasellar and paraclival; bony dehiscence in a minority; septa may insert on it; protect: Image guidance; no forceful septum removal; Doppler if uncertain; injury: Catastrophic hemorrhage, stroke, pseudoaneurysm
A-csf-nasoseptal.danger2: Optic nerve — where: Superolateral sphenoid wall; may lie in an Onodi (sphenoethmoidal) cell; protect: Recognize an Onodi cell on CT; avoid heat and pressure on the canal; injury: Blindness
A-csf-nasoseptal.danger3: Anterior ethmoid artery — where: Ethmoid roof, sometimes free in a mesentery; protect: Bipolar coagulation; avoid tearing; injury: Retraction into the orbit with retrobulbar hematoma; CSF leak
A-csf-nasoseptal.danger4: Lamina papyracea and orbit — where: Lateral ethmoid wall; protect: Gentle lateral dissection; avoid packing pressure on the orbit; injury: Orbital fat exposure, medial rectus injury, hematoma
A-csf-nasoseptal.danger5: Olfactory epithelium — where: Upper septum and superior turbinate, cribriform region; protect: Keep the flap's superior incision 1 to 2 cm below the septal roof; injury: Hyposmia, anosmia
A-csf-nasoseptal.danger6: Nasoseptal flap pedicle — where: Sphenoid face below the ostium; protect: Harvest early; widen the sphenoidotomy superiorly; avoid kinking; injury: Flap necrosis, reconstruction failure
A-csf-nasoseptal.variant1: Keros type III olfactory fossa (deep cribriform, tall lateral lamella)
A-csf-nasoseptal.variant2: Asymmetric ethmoid roof heights
A-csf-nasoseptal.variant3: Onodi cell with the optic nerve in the posterior ethmoid
A-csf-nasoseptal.variant4: Dehiscent ICA or optic canal in the sphenoid; septum inserting on the ICA
A-csf-nasoseptal.variant5: Extensive lateral sphenoid pneumatization (lateral recess beyond V2), a common site of spontaneous leaks; a persistent lateral craniopharyngeal (Sternberg) canal has been proposed as one cause
A-csf-nasoseptal.supply1: Nasoseptal flap — Posterior septal artery, from the sphenopalatine artery (internal maxillary)
A-csf-nasoseptal.supply2: Ethmoid roof — Anterior and posterior ethmoid arteries from the ophthalmic artery (ICA)
A-csf-nasoseptal.supply3: Sphenoid region — ICA, vidian artery and nerve, V2 in foramen rotundum
A-csf-nasoseptal.supply4: Olfaction — Olfactory epithelium on the upper septum, superior turbinate and cribriform plate
A-csf-nasoseptal.pimp1: Q: What are the Keros types? A: Depth of the olfactory fossa: type I 1 to 3 mm, type II 4 to 7 mm, type III 8 to 16 mm
A-csf-nasoseptal.pimp2: Q: What artery supplies the nasoseptal flap? A: Posterior septal artery, a branch of the sphenopalatine artery
A-csf-nasoseptal.pimp3: Q: Where is the most common site of iatrogenic CSF leak in FESS? A: Lateral lamella of the cribriform plate (also posterior ethmoid roof)
A-csf-nasoseptal.pimp4: Q: What is an Onodi cell? A: A posterior ethmoid cell that pneumatizes lateral or superior to the sphenoid and may contain the optic nerve
A-csf-nasoseptal.pimp5: Q: What is the opticocarotid recess? A: The lateral recess between the optic canal and the parasellar ICA on the sphenoid wall, from pneumatization of the optic strut
A-csf-nasoseptal.pimp6: Q: What does the vidian nerve contain, and where does it lead posteriorly? A: GSPN (parasympathetic) plus deep petrosal (sympathetic); its canal leads to the anterior genu of the petrous ICA at the foramen lacerum
A-csf-nasoseptal.pimp7: Q: What sinonasal finding distinguishes CSF from other fluid at the bedside, and what test confirms it? A: Clear unilateral watery drainage with a halo sign is suggestive; beta-2 transferrin confirms
A-csf-nasoseptal.pimp8: Q: Where does the anterior ethmoid artery enter the nasal cavity? A: Through the anterior ethmoidal foramen from the orbit, crossing the ethmoid roof posterolateral to anteromedial to reach the lateral lamella and cribriform
OR-csf-nasoseptal.step1: Map the defect on preoperative imaging and endoscopy, then obtain complete endoscopic exposure while defining the orbit, skull base, sphenoid, carotid/optic structures, and planned vascularized-flap pedicle.
OR-csf-nasoseptal.step2: Harvest the nasoseptal or alternative flap before sacrificing its pedicle territory when a vascularized reconstruction is planned, preserving the posterior septal vascular supply.
OR-csf-nasoseptal.step3: Localize the actual CSF defect and remove only enough mucosa/bone around it to create a stable reconstruction bed.
OR-csf-nasoseptal.step4: Reduce herniated tissue only as required and obtain controlled hemostasis without injuring intracranial or neurovascular structures.
OR-csf-nasoseptal.step5: Place the chosen inlay/onlay grafts with broad contact to healthy margins, tailoring multilayer reconstruction to defect size, flow, and location.
OR-csf-nasoseptal.step6: Rotate the vascularized flap without pedicle twist or compression and cover the entire defect with viable tissue.
OR-csf-nasoseptal.step7: Support the reconstruction with the planned buttress/packing while avoiding excessive pressure on the flap pedicle or orbit.
OR-csf-nasoseptal.step8: Perform a final leak/reconstruction inspection and document postoperative CSF precautions and lumbar-drain strategy when used.
OR-csf-nasoseptal.step9: When the skull-base defect is exposed or an unexpected intraoperative CSF leak is encountered, stop nonessential dissection and define the exact defect, its low- versus high-flow character, surrounding bony/dural margins, and adjacent orbit, optic nerve and carotid anatomy before enlarging or manipulating the site further.
OR-csf-nasoseptal.step10: Match reconstruction to the defect rather than using one closure for every leak: a small low-flow defect may be closed with a deliberate multilayer free-graft construct, whereas a large/high-flow defect or high-risk tissue bed should escalate to multilayer reconstruction with a vascularized pedicled flap when available. Seat each layer on stable healthy margins and preserve the flap pedicle without twist, tension or compression.
OR-csf-nasoseptal.step11: After reconstruction, inspect the entire defect and flap contact, support the construct without excessive orbital or pedicle pressure, and assess for persistent leakage under an appropriate physiologic challenge. A lumbar drain is selective adjunctive CSF diversion for chosen high-risk situations—not a substitute for a mechanically sound closure and not an automatic requirement for every endonasal leak.
OR-csf-nasoseptal.step12: If brisk arterial hemorrhage obscures the field or raises concern for carotid or another major-vessel injury, abandon the routine leak-repair sequence and execute the vascular-injury rescue plan. Maintain controlled visualization/temporary hemostasis and obtain vascular/skull-base help rather than applying blind deep cautery or unplanned instrumentation adjacent to a suspected arterial injury.

## Frontal Sinusotomy / Draf II-III  (slug: draf, region: Rhinology / Skull Base)

A-draf.summary: The frontal sinus drains down an hourglass pathway, the frontal recess, which is bounded by the orbit laterally, the skull base and anterior ethmoid artery posteriorly, the middle turbinate medially, and the agger nasi and frontal beak anteriorly. Variable cells crowd the recess. Draf procedures remove these cells and then widen the outflow: IIa between the lamina and the middle turbinate, IIb from the lamina to the septum, and III joining both sides across the midline. The safe working zone is anterior and medial, away from the skull base and the AEA.
A-draf.layer1: Anterior ethmoid and agger nasi — middle turbinate axilla and anterior attachment; agger nasi cell; superior uncinate attachment. The agger nasi lies anterior and superior to the middle turbinate axilla, deep to the maxillary line. Removing its posterior and medial walls ('uncapping the egg') opens the lower frontal recess.
A-draf.layer2: Map frontal cells on CT — agger nasi / supra agger / supra agger frontal cells (anterior); suprabullar / suprabullar frontal / supraorbital ethmoid cells (posterior); frontal septal cell (medial). Using the IFAC framework, decide on multiplanar CT whether the drainage pathway runs medial, lateral or posterior to each cell before removing partitions. A supraorbital ethmoid cell can look like a second frontal sinus, and the AEA often runs along its posterior wall.
A-draf.layer3: Clear the recess from below upward — lamina papyracea / medial orbital wall (lateral); skull base and AEA (posterior); frontal beak (anterior); middle turbinate (medial). Use angled scopes and curved instruments. Fracture cell walls forward and medially away from the skull base, and remove the fragments without stripping mucosa circumferentially.
A-draf.layer4: Frontal ostium (Draf IIa) — frontal sinus ostium (narrowest point of the hourglass); posterior table; frontal sinus floor. Identify the true lumen (with image guidance or a seeker that passes freely and transilluminates). IIa means the opening runs from the lamina to the middle turbinate.
A-draf.layer5: Medial extension (Draf IIb) — anterior middle turbinate attachment; frontal sinus floor medial to the turbinate; nasal septum (medial limit); olfactory fossa (posteromedial). Resect the anterior middle turbinate attachment and drill the frontal floor medially to the septum. The posterior limit is the skull base: the olfactory fossa lies just behind and medial to the frontal floor.
A-draf.layer6: Midline opening (Draf III / EMLP) — superior septum (septal window); frontal intersinus septum; frontal beak / nasofrontal process; first olfactory fibres; periosteum over the frontal process of the maxilla and skin (lateral-anterior limits). Create a septal window below the frontal floor. Drill the floor and beak across the midline to form the 'frontal T'. The first olfactory fibre marks the posterior limit. Laterally, drill until the periosteum of the frontal process or the orbit is reached on each side.
A-draf.layer7: Final check — frontal opening; skull base; orbit; remaining mucosa. Use 45-70 degree scopes to confirm no residual cell caps or obstructing partitions remain. Preserve mucosa, especially posteriorly, to limit circumferential scarring.
A-draf.landmark1: Agger nasi cell — Anterior and superior to the middle turbinate axilla, beneath the maxillary line; it is the most anterior ethmoid cell (Its posterior wall forms the anterior wall of the frontal recess; removing it is the first step)
A-draf.landmark2: Frontal drainage pathway — On sagittal, coronal and axial CT, trace the outflow from the frontal ostium down past each cell, then follow it endoscopically with a seeker. It is medial to the uncinate when the uncinate attaches to the lamina. (Cells are removed in relation to the pathway, not blindly)
A-draf.landmark3: Anterior ethmoid artery — Posterior border of the frontal recess, usually just behind the skull-base attachment of the bulla lamella (roof of the suprabullar recess) or along the back of a supraorbital ethmoid cell; position varies. On coronal CT, look for the notch in the medial orbital wall. (Posterior limit of frontal dissection)
A-draf.landmark4: First olfactory fibre — Elevate septal mucosa just posterior to the frontal T at the anterior cribriform plate; the first fibres run into the olfactory fossa (Posterior limit of the Draf III drillout)
A-draf.landmark5: Frontal beak — Thick bone anterior to the frontal ostium, felt as a ledge; drill it forward to increase the anteroposterior diameter (The main bony obstruction to a wide opening)
A-draf.landmark6: Lateral limit — The lamina / medial orbital wall in Draf II, and the periosteum of the frontal process of the maxilla in Draf III (Defines how wide drilling can go)
A-draf.danger1: Anterior ethmoid artery — where: Posterior frontal recess at or just below the skull base; it may run free in a mesentery; protect: Locate on CT, keep posterior dissection minimal, and use bipolar if cut. Never avulse it.; injury: Retrobulbar hematoma (canthotomy), intracranial bleeding
A-draf.danger2: Skull base / lateral lamella / posterior table — where: Posterior and posteromedial to the frontal recess; the olfactory fossa sits just behind the frontal floor medially; protect: Work anteriorly, drill the beak rather than the posterior table, and respect the first olfactory fibre; injury: CSF leak, anosmia, intracranial injury
A-draf.danger3: Orbit (lamina papyracea, periorbita) — where: Lateral wall of the recess; protect: Fracture cells medially and identify the lamina before lateral drilling; injury: Orbital fat injury, hematoma, diplopia
A-draf.danger4: Lacrimal sac and duct — where: Anteroinferior to the lateral drilling in Draf III; protect: Stay above and behind the sac and identify the frontal process periosteum; injury: Epiphora
A-draf.danger5: Frontal recess mucosa — where: Circumference of the new opening; protect: Preserve mucosa, especially posteriorly, use mucosal grafts or flaps in Draf III, and avoid opposing raw surfaces; injury: Cicatricial restenosis, mucocele
A-draf.variant1: IFAC frontal cells: supra agger frontal cell, suprabullar frontal cell, frontal septal cell
A-draf.variant2: Supraorbital ethmoid cell (pneumatizing over the orbit), with the AEA along its posterior wall
A-draf.variant3: Uncinate attaching to the skull base or middle turbinate (frontal drains into the infundibulum)
A-draf.variant4: Hypoplastic or aplastic frontal sinus, or a narrow anteroposterior diameter (limits a Draf III)
A-draf.variant5: Low-lying skull base / Keros III olfactory fossa
A-draf.supply1: Arterial — AEA (ophthalmic); supraorbital and supratrochlear arteries to the frontal sinus mucosa and bone
A-draf.supply2: Venous — Diploic veins and foramina of Breschet in the posterior table drain to the dural sinuses (intracranial spread)
A-draf.supply3: Sensory — Supraorbital and supratrochlear nerves (V1)
A-draf.supply4: Olfactory — First olfactory fibres just posterior to the frontal floor medially
A-draf.pimp1: Q: Boundaries of the frontal recess? A: Anterior: agger nasi / frontal beak. Posterior: bulla lamella / skull base with the AEA. Lateral: lamina papyracea. Medial: middle turbinate / lateral lamella.
A-draf.pimp2: Q: Define Draf I, IIa, IIb, III. A: I: anterior ethmoidectomy clearing the recess without touching the ostium. IIa: ostium opened between lamina and middle turbinate. IIb: lamina to septum. III: bilateral IIb plus superior septectomy and removal of the intersinus septum (EMLP).
A-draf.pimp3: Q: What is the posterior limit of a Draf III? A: The first olfactory fibre / anterior olfactory fossa
A-draf.pimp4: Q: Where does the frontal sinus drain when the uncinate attaches to the lamina? A: Medial to the uncinate, directly into the middle meatus (the uncinate forms the recessus terminalis)
A-draf.pimp5: Q: Name the anterior IFAC cells. A: Agger nasi, supra agger cell, supra agger frontal cell
A-draf.pimp6: Q: What are the foramina of Breschet? A: Venous foramina in the frontal sinus mucosa and posterior table; a route for intracranial infection
A-draf.pimp7: Q: What is Pott puffy tumor? A: Frontal bone osteomyelitis with a subperiosteal abscess of the forehead, a complication of frontal sinusitis
A-draf.pimp8: Q: When does the frontal sinus appear radiographically? A: It is absent at birth and begins to pneumatize in early childhood; it usually becomes visible on imaging at roughly 5-8 years (sources vary) and keeps enlarging until late adolescence
OR-draf.step1: Expose the frontal recess after completing the necessary anterior ethmoid dissection and identify the agger nasi/frontal cells, orbit, middle turbinate, and skull base.
OR-draf.step2: Use multiplanar CT orientation to determine whether drainage is medial/lateral to individual frontal cells before removing partitions.
OR-draf.step3: Open cells from below upward, keeping the orbit as the lateral boundary and the skull base/posterior table as the posterior boundary.
OR-draf.step4: Identify the true frontal sinus lumen and enlarge the drainage pathway without circumferential mucosal stripping.
OR-draf.step5: For a Draf IIb-type opening, extend medially to the nasal septum and laterally toward the lamina while preserving the posterior skull-base boundary.
OR-draf.step6: Remove residual frontal-beak or cell partitions only to the extent needed for a stable, widely visible outflow tract.
OR-draf.step7: Inspect with angled endoscopes to confirm the entire intended frontal opening and absence of residual obstructing cells.
OR-draf.step8: Obtain hemostasis and preserve mucosa/avoid opposing raw surfaces to limit cicatricial restenosis.

## Endoscopic Sinus Surgery / Ethmoidectomy  (slug: endoscopic-sinus-surgery, region: Rhinology / Skull Base)

A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.summary: Ethmoidectomy opens a stack of cells that sits between two thin walls: the lamina papyracea laterally and the ethmoid roof / lateral lamella of the cribriform plate superiorly and medially. Safe dissection keeps the orbit identified laterally at all times, enters the posterior ethmoid low and medial through the basal lamella, and uses the skull base as a known superior boundary. The skull base generally slopes downward from anterior to posterior (and from lateral to medial toward the cribriform plate), so it is identified first posteriorly, where the roof is lower and the bone thicker, and then followed forward; the slope varies between patients and sides and is measured on sagittal CT. The anterior ethmoid artery and any Onodi cell, with the optic nerve and carotid next to it, are checked on CT before surgery.
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.layer1: Middle meatus entry — middle turbinate (medial limit); uncinate process; ethmoid infundibulum; natural maxillary ostium. Medialize the middle turbinate gently, without fracturing its skull-base attachment. Uncinectomy and maxillary antrostomy reveal the orbital floor and the medial orbital wall, which give a lateral reference at a known height.
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.layer2: Ethmoid bulla — ethmoid bulla (second lamella); lamina papyracea (lateral); suprabullar and retrobullar recesses (sinus lateralis); anterior ethmoid artery (in or behind the suprabullar recess). Enter the bulla medially and inferiorly. Take down its lateral wall to show the lamina, which runs in roughly the same sagittal plane as the medial maxillary wall. If the bulla lamella does not reach the skull base, a suprabullar recess lies above it. The AEA usually crosses the roof in this region, just behind the bulla lamella / in the roof of the suprabullar recess, at the posterior limit of the frontal recess; its exact position is confirmed on CT.
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.layer3: Basal lamella — basal (ground) lamella of the middle turbinate (third lamella); vertical (coronal) and horizontal (axial) portions. The basal lamella separates the anterior ethmoid from the posterior ethmoid. Penetrate its vertical portion inferiorly and medially, just above the horizontal part and lateral to the turbinate, to stay far from the skull base and orbit. Preserve the horizontal portion so the middle turbinate stays stable.
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.layer4: Posterior ethmoid — posterior ethmoid cells (drain into the superior meatus); superior turbinate; posterior ethmoid artery; skull base (lower here). The posterior ethmoid cells are fewer, larger and lower in height. Identify the skull base posteriorly, where the bone is thicker, and follow it forward. The posterior ethmoid artery crosses the roof just anterior to the anterior sphenoid wall.
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.layer5: Sphenoid face and Onodi check — anterior wall of sphenoid; Onodi (sphenoethmoidal) cell; optic nerve; ICA. The last posterior ethmoid cell borders the sphenoid face. A cell extending superolateral to the sphenoid is an Onodi cell, and its lateral wall may carry the optic nerve and occasionally the ICA. Enter the sphenoid medially and inferiorly.
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.layer6: Skull base skeletonization, posterior to anterior — fovea ethmoidalis; lateral lamella of cribriform plate (medial, thinnest); anterior ethmoid artery (in a canal or in a mesentery below the roof); frontal recess (anterior limit). Remove partitions along the roof working posterior to anterior with through-cutting instruments, never by pulling cells down. Do not dissect medially along the middle turbinate attachment where the lateral lamella is thinnest.
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.layer7: Final inspection — lamina papyracea; skull base; middle turbinate; AEA and PEA. Inspect for exposed orbital fat, CSF and bleeding from ethmoid arteries. Confirm the middle turbinate is stable and not lateralized.
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.landmark1: Lamina papyracea — Follow the medial orbital wall up from the orbital floor seen through the maxillary antrostomy; it lies in the plane of the medial maxillary wall. Ballotting the globe shows movement if the lamina is dehiscent. (The lateral boundary that must stay in view throughout the case)
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.landmark2: Basal lamella entry point — Directly behind the bulla, inferomedially, just above the horizontal portion and lateral to the turbinate vertical attachment (Safe entry into the posterior ethmoid)
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.landmark3: Skull base — Identify it first in the posterior ethmoid or sphenoid (thicker bone, lower height), or at the planum through a sphenoidotomy, then follow it anteriorly (A known superior limit so you do not chase disease upward blindly)
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.landmark4: Anterior ethmoid artery — Coronal CT shows the anterior ethmoid foramen as a notch (beak) in the medial orbital wall between the superior oblique and medial rectus. Endoscopically it usually crosses the roof at the posterior limit of the frontal recess, just behind the skull-base attachment of the bulla lamella or in the roof of the suprabullar recess (position varies; check CT). It may hang in a mesentery below the roof. (Transection with retraction into the orbit causes retrobulbar hematoma)
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.landmark5: Sphenoid ostium (transnasal check) — Medial to the superior turbinate in the sphenoethmoidal recess, classically about 1-1.5 cm above the choanal arch (cadaveric series report up to about 2-3 cm, so use the superior turbinate and the midline as the main guides) (Confirms the sphenoid before the transethmoid opening, so the posterior ethmoid is not mistaken for the sphenoid)
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.landmark6: Middle turbinate vertical attachment — Medial boundary of dissection, attached to the lateral lamella and cribriform plate (Twisting or medial dissection here risks CSF leak)
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.danger1: Lamina papyracea, orbital fat and medial rectus — where: Lateral wall of the ethmoid; the medial rectus sits close behind the periorbita at mid-height; protect: Keep the lamina identified, angle instruments medially, turn off the microdebrider near the lamina, and stop when fat appears; injury: Orbital hematoma, diplopia from medial rectus injury, enophthalmos
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.danger2: Lateral lamella of cribriform plate / skull base — where: Superomedial, at the middle turbinate attachment; the thinnest bone, longest in Keros type III; protect: Measure Keros depth and asymmetry on coronal CT, dissect posterior to anterior along the roof, and use no medial-pulling strokes; injury: CSF leak, meningitis, intracranial injury
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.danger3: Anterior and posterior ethmoid arteries — where: Crossing the ethmoid roof from orbit to olfactory fossa; the AEA is at the back of the frontal recess; protect: Locate on CT, avoid avulsion, and use bipolar if cut. Avoid monopolar near the orbit.; injury: Retrobulbar hematoma (urgent lateral canthotomy and cantholysis), intracranial bleeding. Do not embolize: these are ICA-system (ophthalmic) branches.
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.danger4: Optic nerve — where: Lateral wall of the posterior ethmoid / Onodi cell and the superolateral sphenoid; protect: Identify the Onodi cell on CT and use no powered or blind lateral work at the apex; injury: Blindness
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.danger5: Internal carotid artery — where: Lateral sphenoid wall, and occasionally the lateral wall of an Onodi cell; protect: Enter the sphenoid medially and inferiorly, and do not twist septations that insert on the carotid prominence; injury: Catastrophic hemorrhage, stroke, pseudoaneurysm
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.danger6: Middle turbinate — where: Medial; the basal lamella horizontal portion anchors it; protect: Preserve the horizontal basal lamella and the axilla and avoid mucosal stripping; injury: Lateralization, synechiae, frontal recess obstruction
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.variant1: Onodi (sphenoethmoidal) cell with the optic nerve and sometimes the ICA in its wall
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.variant2: Keros type III or asymmetric skull-base heights between sides
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.variant3: Haller (infraorbital ethmoid) cell narrowing the infundibulum
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.variant4: Concha bullosa or paradoxical middle turbinate
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.variant5: Dehiscent lamina papyracea, or AEA running in a mesentery below the roof
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.supply1: Arterial — AEA and PEA (ophthalmic, from the ICA) supply the ethmoid roof and upper septum; SPA (maxillary, from the ECA) supplies the lateral wall and turbinates
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.supply2: Venous — Ethmoid veins to the ophthalmic veins to the cavernous sinus (route of intracranial and orbital spread)
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.supply3: Sensory — Anterior ethmoidal nerve (V1, nasociliary); posterior lateral nasal nerves (V2 via the pterygopalatine ganglion)
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.supply4: Special sense — Olfactory epithelium at the cribriform plate, superior septum and superior turbinate
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.pimp1: Q: Give the Keros types. A: Olfactory fossa depth: I is 1-3 mm, II is 4-7 mm, III is 8-16 mm
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.pimp2: Q: Name the ethmoid lamellae in order. A: 1 uncinate, 2 bulla, 3 basal lamella of the middle turbinate, 4 superior turbinate (5 supreme turbinate if present)
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.pimp3: Q: Where do the anterior and posterior ethmoid cells drain? A: Anterior into the middle meatus; posterior into the superior meatus (sphenoethmoidal recess region)
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.pimp4: Q: What is an Onodi cell? A: A posterior ethmoid cell pneumatizing superolateral to the sphenoid sinus. The optic nerve, and sometimes the ICA, are at risk.
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.pimp5: Q: What is the classic orbital-wall distance rule for the ethmoid arteries? A: About 24-12-6 mm along the medial orbital wall: anterior lacrimal crest to AEA foramen, AEA to PEA, PEA to optic canal. These are averages; individual distances vary by several mm (PEA to optic canal is often reported as about 4-7 mm), so never rely on them in place of CT.
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.pimp6: Q: Thinnest part of the anterior skull base? A: Lateral lamella of the cribriform plate
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.pimp7: Q: AEA origin, and why not embolize it? A: Ophthalmic artery from the ICA; embolization risks blindness and stroke
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.pimp8: Q: What is the sinus lateralis? A: The suprabullar and retrobullar recess space, bounded by the bulla, basal lamella, lamina and skull base
A-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.pimp9: Q: What is the first step for retrobulbar hematoma? A: Lateral canthotomy with inferior cantholysis, plus medical decompression and ophthalmology
OR-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.step1: After uncinectomy/maxillary identification as appropriate, identify the ethmoid bulla, lamina papyracea, middle turbinate, and skull-base orientation.
OR-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.step2: Open the ethmoid bulla in its safe medial/inferior portion and remove partitions while keeping the orbit continuously defined laterally.
OR-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.step3: Progress through anterior ethmoid cells toward the basal lamella, maintaining a known superior limit rather than blindly following disease toward the skull base.
OR-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.step4: Enter the posterior ethmoid through the basal lamella at a controlled inferomedial location when posterior dissection is indicated.
OR-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.step5: Identify posterior ethmoid landmarks and the sphenoid face; recognize that the skull base slopes and may be asymmetric.
OR-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.step6: Skeletonize rather than violate the lamina/skull base, and treat the anterior ethmoid artery region only with deliberate awareness of its orbital and skull-base attachments.
OR-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.step7: Remove residual obstructing partitions while preserving middle-turbinate stability and mucosa where possible.
OR-endoscopic-sinus-surgery.step8: Perform a final orbit, skull-base, and hemostasis inspection before leaving the ethmoid cavity.

## Endoscopic Maxillary Antrostomy  (slug: maxillary-antrostomy, region: Rhinology / Skull Base)

A-maxillary-antrostomy.summary: A maxillary antrostomy widens the natural ostium. The ostium sits in the floor of the ethmoid infundibulum, hidden behind the uncinate process, and mucociliary clearance runs toward it. The operation fails if an accessory ostium is enlarged instead, and is unsafe if dissection strays anteriorly into the nasolacrimal duct or laterally and superiorly into the orbit.
A-maxillary-antrostomy.layer1: Middle meatus survey — middle turbinate; uncinate process; maxillary line; ethmoid bulla; hiatus semilunaris. The maxillary line is the curved ridge on the lateral wall from the middle turbinate axilla toward the inferior turbinate. It marks the frontal process of the maxilla and the nasolacrimal duct; the uncinate attaches just behind it.
A-maxillary-antrostomy.layer2: Uncinectomy — uncinate process (free posterior edge); ethmoid infundibulum; lamina papyracea (lateral, behind the uncinate); lacrimal bone (anterior). Incise or back-bite the uncinate and remove its horizontal (inferior) part. Keep instruments parallel to the lamina rather than pointing laterally, particularly when the uncinate is lateralized against it.
A-maxillary-antrostomy.layer3: Find the natural ostium — natural maxillary ostium; floor of the infundibulum; posterior fontanelle (common site of accessory ostia). Use a 30-45 degree scope. The ostium is an oblique opening in the floor of the infundibulum at about the level of the lower edge of the middle turbinate. Slide a ball probe inferolaterally behind the uncinate to find it.
A-maxillary-antrostomy.layer4: Enlarge posteriorly and inferiorly — posterior fontanelle; inferior turbinate attachment (inferior limit); palatine bone / posterior wall of maxillary sinus; posterior lateral nasal artery (SPA branch). Widen through the soft fontanelle tissue posteriorly and inferiorly. Join any accessory ostium to the natural ostium. Excessive posterior extension reaches SPA branches.
A-maxillary-antrostomy.layer5: Respect anterior and superior limits — nasolacrimal duct (anterior, hard bone); orbital floor (superior); infraorbital nerve canal (sinus roof). Stop anteriorly when the bone becomes hard (the frontal process and lacrimal canal). The orbital floor forms the roof of the antrostomy.
A-maxillary-antrostomy.layer6: Inspect the sinus — maxillary sinus walls; infraorbital nerve; alveolar recess; zygomatic recess. Use angled endoscopes. Treat only disease that needs removal and preserve healthy mucosa.
A-maxillary-antrostomy.landmark1: Natural maxillary ostium — Remove the inferior uncinate, then look into the infundibulum floor with an angled scope at about the level of the lower middle-turbinate edge. A curved ball probe slid inferolaterally behind the uncinate drops into it. It lies anterior to any posterior fontanelle accessory ostium. (The antrostomy must include it or recirculation persists)
A-maxillary-antrostomy.landmark2: Nasolacrimal duct — Under the maxillary line, anterior to the uncinate attachment, in hard bone a few mm in front of the natural ostium. It opens under the inferior turbinate (Hasner valve). (Anterior limit of the antrostomy)
A-maxillary-antrostomy.landmark3: Orbital floor and lamina papyracea — Superior edge of the antrostomy; with an angled scope the orbital floor is seen as the sinus roof, and it continues into the lamina above (Superior and lateral limits; it also guides the ethmoid dissection that follows)
A-maxillary-antrostomy.landmark4: Posterior fontanelle — Membranous area behind the natural ostium, above the inferior turbinate attachment. Accessory ostia here look round and posteriorly placed. (Must be connected to the natural ostium, not mistaken for it)
A-maxillary-antrostomy.landmark5: Infraorbital nerve — Ridge in the sinus roof running anteriorly; it may hang on a septation or be dehiscent (Angled instruments in the sinus can injure it)
A-maxillary-antrostomy.danger1: Orbit (lamina papyracea and orbital floor) — where: Lateral to the uncinate and superior to the antrostomy. Most at risk in a hypoplastic sinus or atelectatic uncinate apposed to the lamina.; protect: Swing the back-biter medially, do not push laterally through the infundibulum, and review CT for a lateralized uncinate; injury: Orbital fat exposure, hematoma, medial rectus injury, diplopia
A-maxillary-antrostomy.danger2: Nasolacrimal duct — where: Anterior to the natural ostium under the maxillary line; protect: Stop at hard bone anteriorly and use a back-biter only on thin bone; injury: Epiphora, dacryocystitis
A-maxillary-antrostomy.danger3: Accessory ostium / recirculation — where: Posterior fontanelle accessory ostium separate from the natural ostium; protect: Identify the natural ostium and join both openings into one; injury: Persistent sinusitis from mucus circulating out of one opening and back into the other
A-maxillary-antrostomy.danger4: Posterior lateral nasal artery (SPA branch) — where: Posterior and inferior limits of the antrostomy near the palatine bone; protect: Limit the posterior extent and use bipolar if a branch bleeds; injury: Intraoperative or delayed epistaxis
A-maxillary-antrostomy.danger5: Infraorbital nerve — where: Sinus roof, possibly dehiscent; protect: Gentle work with angled instruments in the roof; injury: Cheek and upper lip numbness
A-maxillary-antrostomy.variant1: Haller (infraorbital ethmoid) cell narrowing the infundibulum from above
A-maxillary-antrostomy.variant2: Hypoplastic maxillary sinus / silent sinus with a lateralized uncinate against the lamina
A-maxillary-antrostomy.variant3: Accessory ostia in the fontanelles (common)
A-maxillary-antrostomy.variant4: Pneumatized uncinate, or uncinate attaching to the lamina, skull base or middle turbinate
A-maxillary-antrostomy.variant5: Dehiscent infraorbital canal or nerve suspended on a septation
A-maxillary-antrostomy.supply1: Arterial — Posterior lateral nasal branches of the SPA; infraorbital and alveolar branches of the maxillary artery to the sinus
A-maxillary-antrostomy.supply2: Sensory — V2: infraorbital nerve and superior alveolar nerves (posterior, middle, anterior) in the sinus walls
A-maxillary-antrostomy.supply3: Drainage — Mucociliary flow is directed to the natural ostium, then through the infundibulum and hiatus semilunaris to the middle meatus
A-maxillary-antrostomy.pimp1: Q: Boundaries of the ethmoid infundibulum? A: Medially the uncinate, laterally the lamina papyracea / frontal process of the maxilla, posteriorly the bulla. It opens medially through the hiatus semilunaris.
A-maxillary-antrostomy.pimp2: Q: Where does the uncinate attach superiorly, and why does it matter? A: Most often to the lamina (forming the recessus terminalis), so the frontal sinus drains medial to the uncinate. Otherwise to the skull base or middle turbinate, and then the frontal drains into the infundibulum.
A-maxillary-antrostomy.pimp3: Q: Where does the nasolacrimal duct open? A: Inferior meatus under the inferior turbinate, at the valve of Hasner
A-maxillary-antrostomy.pimp4: Q: Why must accessory and natural ostia be joined? A: Mucociliary flow goes to the natural ostium regardless, so mucus exits and re-enters through the accessory opening (recirculation)
A-maxillary-antrostomy.pimp5: Q: What is a Haller cell? A: An infraorbital ethmoid cell along the orbital floor / medial maxillary roof that can narrow the infundibulum
A-maxillary-antrostomy.pimp6: Q: What are the fontanelles? A: Membranous areas of the medial maxillary wall above the inferior turbinate, anterior and posterior to the uncinate's inferior attachment
A-maxillary-antrostomy.pimp7: Q: Silent sinus syndrome: anatomy? A: Chronic ostial obstruction with negative pressure collapses the sinus walls. The orbital floor sinks (enophthalmos, hypoglobus) and the uncinate lateralizes.
OR-maxillary-antrostomy.step1: Decongest the nasal cavity and identify the middle turbinate, uncinate process, ethmoid bulla, and expected maxillary natural ostium region before cutting.
OR-maxillary-antrostomy.step2: Incise and remove the uncinate in a controlled anterior-to-posterior/superior-to-inferior fashion while staying aware of the lacrimal system anteriorly and orbit laterally.
OR-maxillary-antrostomy.step3: Identify the natural maxillary ostium with a probe or angled endoscope; do not create a separate accessory opening and assume it is the ostium.
OR-maxillary-antrostomy.step4: Enlarge the natural ostium posteriorly and inferiorly as indicated, joining any accessory ostium to the natural ostium to prevent mucus recirculation.
OR-maxillary-antrostomy.step5: Keep the anterior limit behind the nasolacrimal duct and the superior dissection oriented to the orbital floor/lamina.
OR-maxillary-antrostomy.step6: Use angled visualization to inspect the sinus and remove only disease that requires direct treatment rather than stripping healthy sinus mucosa.
OR-maxillary-antrostomy.step7: Confirm that the antrostomy is circumferentially patent and clearly incorporates the natural ostium.
OR-maxillary-antrostomy.step8: Obtain hemostasis while preserving middle-meatal landmarks and minimizing opposing raw mucosal surfaces.
