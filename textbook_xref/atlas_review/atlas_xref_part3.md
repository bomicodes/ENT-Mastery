
## Total Parotidectomy / Facial Nerve Reconstruction  (slug: parotid-total, region: Thyroid / Parathyroid / Salivary)

A-parotid-total.summary: Total parotidectomy removes gland both lateral and medial to the facial nerve, which runs through the parotid in the plane between the superficial and deep lobes. The deep lobe lies in the retromandibular and prestyloid parapharyngeal region beside the external carotid, retromandibular vein and styloid process. When tumor invades the nerve, oncologic resection is followed by tension-free neurorrhaphy or cable grafting between healthy proximal and distal stumps.
A-parotid-total.layer1: Incision and skin flap — modified Blair (preauricular, around the lobule, into a cervical crease) incision; SMAS / parotidomasseteric fascia; great auricular nerve on the SCM. Raise the anterior flap superficial to the parotid fascia to the anterior gland border. For malignancy, the fascia stays on the specimen and the great auricular nerve may be sacrificed; it is also a convenient cable graft donor.
A-parotid-total.layer2: Posterior gland mobilization — anterior border of SCM; external jugular vein; posterior belly of digastric; cartilaginous tragus. Separate the gland tail from the SCM and expose the posterior belly of digastric. Elevate the gland off the tragal cartilage down to the pointer.
A-parotid-total.layer3: Facial nerve main trunk — tragal pointer; tympanomastoid suture; posterior belly of digastric; stylomastoid foramen region. The trunk exits the stylomastoid foramen and runs anterolaterally into the gland. It lies deep to the pointer and at the depth of the digastric's attachment. If the trunk is involved, expose it retrograde from a distal branch or by mastoidectomy into the vertical segment.
A-parotid-total.layer4: Pes anserinus and branches (superficial lobe removal) — temporofacial division; cervicofacial division; temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular, cervical branches. Dissect along the nerve superficial surface, dividing gland over each branch. Branches are interconnected (especially zygomatic-buccal), but the temporal and marginal branches have few connections and are most vulnerable.
A-parotid-total.layer5: Deep lobe exposure — retromandibular vein (usually deep to the nerve, near the division); external carotid artery; maxillary and superficial temporal arteries; transverse facial artery. Gently elevate and retract the nerve branches. Ligate the ECA and retromandibular vein where they pass through the deep lobe. The maxillary artery runs medial to the mandibular neck.
A-parotid-total.layer6: Medial limits of the deep lobe — styloid process and stylomandibular ligament; parapharyngeal (prestyloid) space; internal carotid and IJV medial to the styloid; CN IX-XII in the poststyloid space. Deep-lobe tumors may pass through the stylomandibular tunnel into the parapharyngeal space. Dividing the stylomandibular ligament and, if needed, mandibulotomy widen access. Stay lateral to the styloid diaphragm to avoid the poststyloid neurovasculature.
A-parotid-total.layer7: Stensen duct and anterior border — Stensen duct crossing masseter; buccal branches alongside the duct; accessory parotid tissue. The duct runs about a fingerbreadth below the zygomatic arch across masseter, then pierces buccinator opposite the upper second molar. Buccal branches travel with it.
A-parotid-total.layer8: Nerve reconstruction — proximal stump (trunk or intratemporal segment); distal branch stumps; great auricular or sural nerve graft. Trim to healthy fascicles on frozen section; coapt with epineurial sutures without tension. A sural graft gives length and branches for multiple distal targets; consider masseteric nerve transfer or static procedures when no proximal stump is available.
A-parotid-total.landmark1: Facial nerve main trunk — About 1 cm deep and slightly anterior-inferior to the tragal pointer; 6-8 mm deep to the outer end of the tympanomastoid suture; at the level of the posterior belly of digastric's insertion, superior to it (Antegrade dissection starts at the trunk)
A-parotid-total.landmark2: Marginal mandibular branch (retrograde start) — Deep to platysma, superficial to the facial vessels as they cross the mandible near the antegonial notch (Alternate start for retrograde dissection when the trunk is obscured by tumor)
A-parotid-total.landmark3: Buccal branch (retrograde start) — Running with Stensen duct across the masseter, roughly a fingerbreadth below the zygomatic arch (Retrograde dissection for anterior or trunk-involving tumors)
A-parotid-total.landmark4: Retromandibular vein — Formed by superficial temporal and maxillary veins behind the mandibular ramus; usually deep to the nerve at the pes anserinus (Useful landmark for the nerve division; bleeding source in the deep lobe)
A-parotid-total.landmark5: Styloid process — Palpate deep and anterior to the trunk at the stylomastoid foramen, pointing anteroinferomedially (Separates prestyloid (deep lobe) from poststyloid (carotid, IJV, CN IX-XII) space)
A-parotid-total.landmark6: Intratemporal facial nerve (proximal stump) — Cortical mastoidectomy, identify the digastric ridge and follow it anteriorly to the stylomastoid foramen; the vertical segment lies medial and anterior (Provides a healthy proximal stump when the trunk is invaded)
A-parotid-total.danger1: Facial nerve and branches — where: Between superficial and deep lobes; at risk at the trunk, pes and on retraction during deep-lobe removal; protect: Antegrade identification, sharp dissection, gentle retraction, monitoring as adjunct; sacrifice only when grossly invaded; injury: Facial paralysis, eye exposure, oral incompetence
A-parotid-total.danger2: External carotid and maxillary artery — where: ECA enters the deep lobe and divides behind the mandibular neck into the superficial temporal and maxillary arteries; protect: Ligate under vision; avoid blind clamping behind the ramus; injury: Hemorrhage from a retracting maxillary artery
A-parotid-total.danger3: Internal carotid artery / IJV — where: Medial to the styloid in the poststyloid parapharyngeal space; protect: Stay on the tumor capsule lateral to the styloid diaphragm; define the ICA in the neck first for extensive deep-lobe tumors; injury: Life-threatening hemorrhage, stroke
A-parotid-total.danger4: Lower cranial nerves (IX-XII) and sympathetic chain — where: Poststyloid space, medial and posterior to the deep lobe; protect: Avoid dissection medial to the styloid unless tumor requires; identify XII in the neck; injury: Dysphagia, aspiration, Horner syndrome, tongue weakness
A-parotid-total.danger5: Great auricular nerve — where: On the SCM, running toward the lobule; protect: Preserve the posterior branch when oncologically safe; injury: Earlobe and preauricular numbness
A-parotid-total.danger6: Auriculotemporal nerve fibers — where: Parasympathetic secretomotor fibers (from IX via otic ganglion) within the gland; protect: Thick skin flap, SMAS or interposed tissue (e.g., SCM flap or acellular dermis); injury: Frey syndrome (gustatory sweating)
A-parotid-total.variant1: Retromandibular vein lateral (superficial) to the facial nerve in a minority of patients.
A-parotid-total.variant2: Branching patterns vary widely (Davis classes); bifurcation into upper and lower divisions is the most common.
A-parotid-total.variant3: Accessory parotid tissue along Stensen duct overlying masseter.
A-parotid-total.variant4: High-riding facial trunk division or early branching before the pes.
A-parotid-total.variant5: Deep-lobe 'dumbbell' tumor through the stylomandibular tunnel versus a round deep-lobe tumor.
A-parotid-total.supply1: Arterial supply — External carotid artery within the gland dividing into superficial temporal and maxillary; transverse facial artery from the superficial temporal
A-parotid-total.supply2: Venous drainage — Retromandibular vein (superficial temporal + maxillary veins), splitting into anterior (to facial) and posterior (to EJV) divisions
A-parotid-total.supply3: Secretomotor — Inferior salivatory nucleus → CN IX → tympanic (Jacobson) nerve → lesser petrosal → otic ganglion → auriculotemporal nerve
A-parotid-total.supply4: Sensory — Great auricular nerve (C2-C3) to the gland skin and lobule; auriculotemporal nerve (V3) preauricular
A-parotid-total.supply5: Motor — Facial nerve: trunk → temporofacial and cervicofacial divisions → temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular, cervical
A-parotid-total.pimp1: Q: What three branches does the facial nerve give before entering the parotid? A: Posterior auricular nerve, nerve to the posterior belly of digastric, nerve to stylohyoid
A-parotid-total.pimp2: Q: Which parotid landmarks locate the facial trunk? A: Tragal pointer, tympanomastoid suture, posterior belly of digastric, styloid process (and mastoid tip)
A-parotid-total.pimp3: Q: What forms the retromandibular vein? A: Superficial temporal vein and maxillary vein
A-parotid-total.pimp4: Q: Describe the parasympathetic pathway to the parotid. A: Inferior salivatory nucleus → CN IX → Jacobson nerve → tympanic plexus → lesser petrosal nerve → otic ganglion → auriculotemporal nerve
A-parotid-total.pimp5: Q: What is the stylomandibular tunnel? A: The gap between the posterior mandibular ramus, the stylomandibular ligament and the skull base through which deep-lobe tumors extend into the parapharyngeal space (dumbbell tumors)
A-parotid-total.pimp6: Q: Where does Stensen duct open? A: Buccal mucosa opposite the maxillary second molar after piercing buccinator
A-parotid-total.pimp7: Q: Preferred nerve graft for long or branched facial nerve defects? A: Sural nerve (great auricular for short defects)
A-parotid-total.pimp8: Q: Which facial nerve branches have the fewest interconnections? A: Temporal (frontal) and marginal mandibular
A-parotid-total.pimp9: Q: Which parotid malignancy is classic for perineural spread? A: Adenoid cystic carcinoma
OR-parotid-total.step1: When tumor approaches the facial nerve, slow the dissection and establish whether a true separable plane exists before converting adherence into intentional nerve sacrifice. Preserve an uninvolved branch without unnecessary circumferential skeletonization, traction, crush, or thermal injury. Facial-nerve monitoring can assist identification and functional feedback, especially in distorted anatomy, but it does not replace direct anatomic dissection or justify dividing a visually uncertain structure.
OR-parotid-total.step2: If the nerve is unexpectedly transected, STOP further traction and energy injury, identify and protect healthy proximal and distal ends, and define the actual defect before continuing the specimen dissection. Favor tension-free primary neurorrhaphy when healthy ends approximate without stretch; use an interposition cable graft when a gap prevents tension-free coaptation. If the proximal facial-nerve stump is unavailable or the distal targets/resection pattern make grafting inadequate, transition deliberately to an appropriate nerve-transfer and/or static/dynamic facial-reanimation plan rather than leaving the deficit unaddressed by default.
OR-parotid-total.step3: Use a preauricular/cervical parotid incision, elevate the skin flap in the appropriate plane, and expose the parotid fascia, tragal cartilage, SCM, and posterior digastric landmarks.
OR-parotid-total.step4: Develop the facial-nerve trunk using a reliable combination of tragal pointer, tympanomastoid suture, and posterior digastric landmarks rather than a single landmark alone.
OR-parotid-total.step5: Trace the facial nerve into upper and lower divisions, releasing the superficial lobe from the branches while controlling retromandibular/external carotid tributaries as encountered.
OR-parotid-total.step6: After the superficial lobe is mobilized, dissect the deep-lobe component from between/around the facial-nerve branches with the nerve continuously visualized and protected.
OR-parotid-total.step7: Control the parotid duct and deep vascular attachments as required by tumor location, maintaining oncologic margins without unnecessary branch sacrifice.
OR-parotid-total.step8: If a facial-nerve branch is involved by tumor, define proximal/distal control and the reconstructive plan before intentional sacrifice.
OR-parotid-total.step9: Remove and orient the specimen, then stimulate/inspect the main trunk and divisions when monitoring is used.
OR-parotid-total.step10: Obtain hemostasis, consider contour reconstruction, place a drain when indicated, and close without compressing the facial nerve.

## Parotidectomy  (slug: parotidectomy, region: Thyroid / Parathyroid / Salivary)

A-parotidectomy.summary: The facial nerve divides the parotid into a superficial and a deep lobe; this is a surgical plane, not a true anatomic lobe. Parotidectomy removes the tumor and a cuff of gland by finding the main nerve trunk at the stylomastoid foramen and lifting gland off the branches, so the safety of the whole case depends on finding the trunk. The retromandibular vein and external carotid artery lie deep to the nerve plane. The great auricular and auriculotemporal nerves account for most sensory and gustatory-sweating (Frey) problems after surgery.
A-parotidectomy.layer1: Incision and flap — skin; subcutaneous fat; SMAS / parotidomasseteric fascia; great auricular nerve on the SCM (posterior branch). Use a modified Blair (preauricular, around the lobule, into a neck crease) or facelift incision. Raise the flap superficial to the parotid fascia, anteriorly to the anterior gland border. The great auricular nerve ascends on the SCM about halfway between the mastoid and the angle of the mandible, and its posterior (lobular) branch can often be kept.
A-parotidectomy.layer2: Posterior gland release — anterior border of SCM; tragal cartilage; posterior belly of digastric; mastoid tip. Separate the gland from the cartilaginous EAC and the SCM. Expose the posterior belly of digastric toward its mastoid attachment. This opens a triangle in which the trunk is found.
A-parotidectomy.layer3: Facial nerve trunk — tympanomastoid suture; tragal pointer; stylomastoid foramen; styloid process (deep); postauricular artery / stylomastoid artery branch (bleeds just superficial to the trunk). Spread parallel to the expected nerve direction. The trunk runs anteriorly, inferiorly and laterally from the stylomastoid foramen, crossing lateral to the styloid. A small artery bleeding just above it is a useful warning sign.
A-parotidectomy.layer4: Pes anserinus and divisions — temporofacial (upper) division; cervicofacial (lower) division; pes anserinus. The trunk divides about 1-2 cm after leaving the foramen, usually within the gland behind the posterior mandibular ramus. Tunnel and divide gland tissue superficial to the branch being followed.
A-parotidectomy.layer5: Peripheral branches — frontal (temporal) branch; zygomatic branch; buccal branch (parallel to Stensen duct); marginal mandibular branch; cervical branch. Dissect the branches nearest the tumor first, following them anteriorly and lifting the superficial lobe off the nerve, rather than pulling the nerve out of the gland.
A-parotidectomy.layer6: Deep structures — retromandibular vein (usually deep to the nerve); external carotid artery and its terminal branches (superficial temporal, maxillary); transverse facial artery; Stensen duct (anterior border, over the masseter); deep lobe and stylomandibular tunnel. Ligate the RMV branches or Stensen duct only as the resection needs. Deep-lobe tumors pass through the stylomandibular tunnel into the prestyloid parapharyngeal space, giving a dumbbell shape.
A-parotidectomy.layer7: Closure — SMAS / fascia flap; drain; sternocleidomastoid flap or graft (optional, for contour or Frey prevention). Stimulate the trunk before closing. An SMAS or SCM flap or an acellular dermal barrier reduces Frey syndrome and contour defect.
A-parotidectomy.landmark1: Facial nerve main trunk — Tragal pointer: about 1 cm deep, anterior and inferior to its tip. Tympanomastoid suture: the trunk is about 6-8 mm deep to its outer end. Posterior belly of digastric: the trunk lies at the same depth, just superior to the digastric attachment at the mastoid. Use these together; none is enough alone. (Antegrade dissection begins at the trunk)
A-parotidectomy.landmark2: Marginal mandibular branch (retrograde) — Crosses superficial to the facial artery and vein at the anterior masseter border, where the vessels cross the inferior mandibular border at the antegonial (facial) notch, deep to the platysma (Retrograde identification when the trunk cannot be reached safely)
A-parotidectomy.landmark3: Buccal branch (retrograde) — Runs parallel to Stensen duct, which lies about a fingerbreadth below the zygomatic arch over the masseter (A reliable anterior retrograde landmark)
A-parotidectomy.landmark4: Frontal branch — Crosses the zygomatic arch along Pitanguy line (about 0.5 cm below the tragus to about 1.5 cm above the lateral brow) in the temporoparietal fascia (Superior limit of flap elevation)
A-parotidectomy.landmark5: Retromandibular vein — Runs vertically behind the ramus; the nerve divisions usually cross superficial (lateral) to it (Guides finding the nerve and marks the superficial-deep lobe division on imaging)
A-parotidectomy.landmark6: Great auricular nerve — On the SCM surface about halfway between the mastoid tip and the mandibular angle, heading toward the lobule (Preserve the posterior branch; it is also a convenient cable graft donor)
A-parotidectomy.danger1: Facial nerve trunk and branches — where: Within the parotid at the superficial-deep lobe interface; the trunk lies deep in the posterior gland at the level of the tympanomastoid suture and posterior belly of digastric (its depth from skin varies with gland and soft-tissue thickness); protect: Identify the trunk by multiple landmarks, dissect superficial to the nerve, use a stimulator or monitor, avoid traction and heat, and repair any transection immediately; injury: Facial paralysis of the branch territory, eye exposure, oral incompetence
A-parotidectomy.danger2: Marginal mandibular branch — where: Crossing the facial vessels superficially, deep to the platysma. Anterior to the facial artery it is almost always above the mandibular border. Posterior to the artery it runs below the border in a minority to about half of patients, depending on the series (Dingman and Grabb: about 1 in 5, within about 1 cm); it lies lower with the neck extended and the head turned; protect: Hayes Martin maneuver (ligate the facial vein low and reflect it up) or dissect deep to the investing fascia; injury: Lower lip depressor weakness
A-parotidectomy.danger3: Great auricular nerve — where: On the SCM, entering the gland and lobule; protect: Keep the posterior branch when oncologically safe; injury: Numbness of the lobule and preauricular skin, neuroma
A-parotidectomy.danger4: Retromandibular vein and ECA branches — where: Deep to the nerve plane; protect: Ligate under vision after the nerve is identified; avoid blind clamping; injury: Hemorrhage, nerve injury from blind clamping
A-parotidectomy.danger5: Tumor capsule (pleomorphic adenoma) — where: Thin capsule with pseudopodia, often lying against nerve branches; protect: Remove with a cuff of normal parotid, use no enucleation, and avoid rupture; injury: Tumor spillage and multinodular recurrence
A-parotidectomy.danger6: Auriculotemporal nerve — where: Behind the TMJ, running up with the superficial temporal vessels; protect: A barrier flap between the gland bed and skin; injury: Frey syndrome (gustatory sweating)
A-parotidectomy.variant1: Retromandibular vein lateral (superficial) to the facial nerve in a minority
A-parotidectomy.variant2: High or early bifurcation of the trunk, or multiple trunks, and many branching patterns with buccal-zygomatic interconnections
A-parotidectomy.variant3: Accessory parotid tissue along Stensen duct over the masseter
A-parotidectomy.variant4: Deep-lobe tumor extending through the stylomandibular tunnel into the prestyloid parapharyngeal space
A-parotidectomy.variant5: Intraparotid lymph nodes (unique among the salivary glands)
A-parotidectomy.supply1: Arterial — ECA within the gland, dividing into the superficial temporal and maxillary arteries; transverse facial and posterior auricular branches
A-parotidectomy.supply2: Venous — Retromandibular vein (superficial temporal plus maxillary veins), dividing into an anterior division to the facial vein and a posterior division to the EJV
A-parotidectomy.supply3: Sensory — Great auricular nerve (C2-C3); auriculotemporal nerve (V3)
A-parotidectomy.supply4: Parasympathetic — Inferior salivatory nucleus, then IX, Jacobson's nerve, lesser petrosal nerve, otic ganglion, auriculotemporal nerve to the gland
A-parotidectomy.pimp1: Q: What is the first extracranial branch of the facial nerve? A: Posterior auricular nerve, followed by branches to the posterior belly of digastric and the stylohyoid
A-parotidectomy.pimp2: Q: Trace the parotid secretomotor pathway. A: Inferior salivatory nucleus, then IX, Jacobson's (tympanic) nerve, lesser petrosal nerve, otic ganglion, then postganglionic fibres via the auriculotemporal nerve
A-parotidectomy.pimp3: Q: What causes Frey syndrome, and how is it tested? A: Severed parasympathetic fibres regrow into sweat glands of the skin; Minor starch-iodine test
A-parotidectomy.pimp4: Q: Where does Stensen duct open? A: Buccal mucosa opposite the upper second molar, after crossing the masseter and piercing the buccinator
A-parotidectomy.pimp5: Q: Why does the parotid contain lymph nodes? A: It encapsulates late in development, after the lymphatics form, so nodes become trapped within it
A-parotidectomy.pimp6: Q: What is the stylomandibular tunnel? A: The gap between the posterior ramus, the styloid / stylomandibular ligament and the skull base. Deep-lobe tumors pass through it to the parapharyngeal space, forming a dumbbell.
A-parotidectomy.pimp7: Q: Name the five main facial nerve branches. A: Temporal (frontal), zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular, cervical
A-parotidectomy.pimp8: Q: Which branches have the least redundancy and are most often weak after surgery? A: Frontal and marginal mandibular (few interconnections)
OR-parotidectomy.step1: When tumor approaches the facial nerve, slow the dissection and establish whether a true separable plane exists before converting adherence into intentional nerve sacrifice. Preserve an uninvolved branch without unnecessary circumferential skeletonization, traction, crush, or thermal injury. Facial-nerve monitoring can assist identification and functional feedback, especially in distorted anatomy, but it does not replace direct anatomic dissection or justify dividing a visually uncertain structure.
OR-parotidectomy.step2: If the nerve is unexpectedly transected, STOP further traction and energy injury, identify and protect healthy proximal and distal ends, and define the actual defect before continuing the specimen dissection. Favor tension-free primary neurorrhaphy when healthy ends approximate without stretch; use an interposition cable graft when a gap prevents tension-free coaptation. If the proximal facial-nerve stump is unavailable or the distal targets/resection pattern make grafting inadequate, transition deliberately to an appropriate nerve-transfer and/or static/dynamic facial-reanimation plan rather than leaving the deficit unaddressed by default.
OR-parotidectomy.step3: Design the preauricular/cervical incision and elevate a thick skin flap superficial to the parotid fascia while protecting the greater auricular nerve when oncologically appropriate.
OR-parotidectomy.step4: Expose the tragal cartilage, mastoid tip/tympanomastoid suture, SCM, and posterior belly of digastric to triangulate the facial-nerve trunk.
OR-parotidectomy.step5: Identify the facial-nerve trunk and confirm it before dividing parotid tissue in the expected nerve plane.
OR-parotidectomy.step6: Trace upper and lower divisions/peripheral branches in the direction required by the lesion, lifting the superficial lobe away from the nerve rather than pulling the nerve out of the gland.
OR-parotidectomy.step7: Control the retromandibular vein/external carotid branches and Stensen duct only as required by the resection.
OR-parotidectomy.step8: Remove the superficial-lobe specimen with an intact tumor capsule and appropriate margin, avoiding tumor spillage.
OR-parotidectomy.step9: Reinspect and stimulate the facial-nerve trunk/branches when used, then secure meticulous hemostasis.
OR-parotidectomy.step10: Place drain/contour reconstruction selectively and close the flap without excessive tension or pressure on the nerve.

## Reoperative Parathyroidectomy  (slug: reop-parathyroid, region: Thyroid / Parathyroid / Salivary)

A-reop-parathyroid.summary: Persistent or recurrent hyperparathyroidism usually reflects a gland that was missed in a typical position, an ectopic gland along the embryologic descent paths, or regrowth of hyperplastic tissue. Reoperation is a targeted, image-guided dissection through scar; safety depends on reaching the target through unscarred planes and identifying the RLN in a virgin segment before mobilizing any tissue in the groove.
A-reop-parathyroid.layer1: Entry and flaps — prior incision; platysma; scarred strap muscles. Reopen the prior incision and raise limited flaps. Plan the corridor from localization: midline for anterior/thymic targets, lateral for posterior/groove targets.
A-reop-parathyroid.layer2: Lateral approach — plane between strap muscles (medial) and SCM / carotid sheath (lateral); posterior thyroid bed. Enter lateral to the straps and medial to the carotid, which is usually unscarred and leads directly to the tracheoesophageal groove and retroesophageal space.
A-reop-parathyroid.layer3: Carotid sheath — common carotid; IJV; vagus. Defines the lateral limit and is itself a search site for undescended inferior glands up to the bifurcation.
A-reop-parathyroid.layer4: RLN in virgin tissue — RLN low near the thoracic inlet; RLN at laryngeal entry behind the cricothyroid joint. Identify and follow the nerve into the scarred segment before dissecting any target near it. Vagal stimulation confirms pathway integrity.
A-reop-parathyroid.layer5: Target region per localization — tracheoesophageal groove / retroesophageal space (missed superior); thyrothymic tract and thymus (missed inferior); intrathyroidal; carotid sheath / angle of mandible; anterior or posterior mediastinum. Dissect directly on the localized gland's capsule. Prior clips and scar are orientation aids only.
A-reop-parathyroid.layer6: Deep limits — esophagus (posteromedial); trachea (medial); prevertebral fascia (deep). Retroesophageal glands lie on the prevertebral fascia behind the esophagus; a bougie aids palpation.
A-reop-parathyroid.layer7: Remaining normal tissue — any viable parathyroid (in situ or prior autograft). Preserve; consider cryopreservation of resected hyperplastic tissue where available.
A-reop-parathyroid.landmark1: Lateral corridor — Retract SCM laterally and strap muscles medially; open the areolar plane along the medial carotid sheath toward the prevertebral fascia (Bypasses midline scar to the groove and retroesophageal space)
A-reop-parathyroid.landmark2: RLN virgin segment — Low in the tracheoesophageal groove just above the inlet, or at the laryngeal entry deep to the inferior constrictor posterior to the cricothyroid joint (Allows tracing the nerve through scar safely)
A-reop-parathyroid.landmark3: Missed superior gland — Posterior to the RLN plane in the tracheoesophageal groove, retroesophageal space or posterior superior mediastinum; follow the groove down from the cricothyroid level (Enlarged superior glands descend dorsally along the esophagus)
A-reop-parathyroid.landmark4: Missed inferior gland — Anterior to the RLN plane: thyrothymic ligament, cervical thymus (deliver by gentle traction), or high in the carotid sheath if undescended (Follows the third pouch/thymic descent path)
A-reop-parathyroid.landmark5: Intrathyroidal gland — Intraoperative ultrasound of the remaining lobe correlating with preoperative imaging (Explains a missing gland with no extrathyroidal finding)
A-reop-parathyroid.landmark6: Esophagus — Palpate bougie posterior and left of the trachea; identify longitudinal muscle (Deep limit and neighbor of retroesophageal targets)
A-reop-parathyroid.danger1: Recurrent laryngeal nerve — where: Scar-embedded in the groove, close to missed superior glands which lie dorsal to it; protect: Virgin-segment identification, neuromonitoring with vagal stimulation, dissect on the gland capsule; injury: Vocal fold paralysis; bilateral risk if prior injury
A-reop-parathyroid.danger2: Remaining parathyroid tissue — where: Unknown number of residual glands; may be only one; protect: Avoid unnecessary exploration of normal sites; preserve pedicles; cryopreserve resected tissue if available; injury: Permanent hypoparathyroidism (higher after reoperation)
A-reop-parathyroid.danger3: Esophagus — where: Adjacent to retroesophageal and groove targets; protect: Bougie; blunt dissection on prevertebral fascia; injury: Perforation, mediastinitis
A-reop-parathyroid.danger4: Carotid / IJV / vagus — where: Lateral limit and search site for undescended glands; protect: Define sheath through the lateral approach before deep dissection; injury: Hemorrhage, vagal palsy
A-reop-parathyroid.danger5: Mediastinal great vessels — where: Behind the sternum when following a thymic or mediastinal target; protect: Limit cervical approach to what can be seen; plan sternotomy or thoracoscopy for deep mediastinal glands; injury: Major hemorrhage
A-reop-parathyroid.variant1: Supernumerary (fifth) gland, most often intrathymic, causing persistent disease after four-gland resection in hyperplasia.
A-reop-parathyroid.variant2: Undescended inferior gland near the carotid bifurcation or angle of the mandible.
A-reop-parathyroid.variant3: Intrathyroidal parathyroid.
A-reop-parathyroid.variant4: Parathyromatosis (seeded parathyroid tissue from prior capsular rupture) or regrowth of a hyperplastic remnant or forearm autograft.
A-reop-parathyroid.variant5: Aortopulmonary window or deep mediastinal gland requiring a thoracic approach.
A-reop-parathyroid.supply1: Arterial supply — Inferior thyroid artery branches; mediastinal glands may be supplied by thymic branches of the internal thoracic artery or aortic arch branches
A-reop-parathyroid.supply2: Embryology — Superior glands: 4th pouch (short descent). Inferior glands: 3rd pouch with thymus (long descent)
A-reop-parathyroid.supply3: Motor nerves — RLN; vagus in the carotid sheath
A-reop-parathyroid.supply4: Venous localization — Superior, middle and inferior thyroid veins and thymic veins are sampled in selective venous sampling
A-reop-parathyroid.pimp1: Q: What is the most common cause of persistent hyperparathyroidism after initial exploration? A: A missed gland in a normal or near-normal (eutopic) position, followed by ectopic glands and unrecognized multigland disease
A-reop-parathyroid.pimp2: Q: Where do missed enlarged superior glands usually lie? A: Tracheoesophageal groove, retroesophageal space or posterior superior mediastinum, dorsal to the RLN
A-reop-parathyroid.pimp3: Q: Where is the most common ectopic site for an inferior gland? A: Thymus / thyrothymic ligament (anterior superior mediastinum)
A-reop-parathyroid.pimp4: Q: What test excludes FHH before reoperation? A: 24-hour urine calcium (calcium/creatinine clearance ratio typically <0.01 in FHH)
A-reop-parathyroid.pimp5: Q: What is parathyromatosis? A: Multiple nodules of hyperfunctioning parathyroid tissue scattered in the neck/mediastinum, often from capsular rupture at prior surgery
A-reop-parathyroid.pimp6: Q: Which approach avoids midline scar in reoperative neck surgery? A: Lateral (back-door) approach between strap muscles and SCM/carotid sheath
A-reop-parathyroid.pimp7: Q: When does a mediastinal gland need a thoracic approach? A: When it lies below the reach of cervical thymectomy, e.g., aortopulmonary window or deep anterior or posterior mediastinum
OR-reop-parathyroid.step1: Re-review prior operative notes and localization, then enter through the previous central-neck incision while anticipating scarred planes and displaced RLN/parathyroid anatomy.
OR-reop-parathyroid.step2: Raise subplatysmal flaps only as needed and develop the least-scarred route toward the localized target rather than performing broad blind scar dissection.
OR-reop-parathyroid.step3: Identify a reliable RLN landmark away from the densest scar—often lower in the tracheoesophageal groove or at the laryngeal entry point—and define the nerve before mobilizing tissue attached to it.
OR-reop-parathyroid.step4: Correlate the suspected gland with the carotid sheath, tracheoesophageal groove, thyrothymic tract, retroesophageal space, mediastinal descent, and prior clips or scar.
OR-reop-parathyroid.step5: Dissect directly on the abnormal gland, preserving the RLN and avoiding unnecessary devascularization of any remaining normal parathyroid tissue.
OR-reop-parathyroid.step6: Control the gland's vascular pedicle and remove the target intact; send tissue confirmation when localization or scar makes identity uncertain.
OR-reop-parathyroid.step7: Interpret intraoperative PTH against the pre-excision baseline and the patient's disease pattern; if the fall is inadequate, reassess localization and multigland disease before extending the exploration.
OR-reop-parathyroid.step8: Perform meticulous hemostasis in the reoperative bed, confirm nerve integrity when monitoring is used, and close without compressing the trachea or nerve.

## Reoperative Thyroid / Central Neck Surgery  (slug: reop-thyroid, region: Thyroid / Parathyroid / Salivary)

A-reop-thyroid.summary: Reoperation clears residual thyroid or recurrent nodal disease in a field where scar has erased the normal planes between strap muscles, thyroid capsule, RLN and parathyroids. The safe strategy is to approach through unoperated tissue (often lateral, between the strap muscles and the SCM/carotid) and to find the RLN in a virgin segment, usually low in the tracheoesophageal groove or at its laryngeal entry, before entering dense scar.
A-reop-thyroid.layer1: Prior incision and flaps — prior scar (excised or reopened); platysma (often adherent to straps); anterior jugular veins. Re-raise subplatysmal flaps only as far as needed. Flaps are often tethered to the straps in the midline, so begin lateral where planes are preserved.
A-reop-thyroid.layer2: Choice of corridor — midline strap raphe (often scarred); lateral 'back-door' plane between the strap muscles (medial) and SCM / carotid sheath (lateral). The lateral approach enters virgin tissue lateral to the straps and medial to the carotid sheath, arriving at the posterolateral thyroid bed and tracheoesophageal groove from the side, behind the old scar.
A-reop-thyroid.layer3: Carotid sheath and inferior thyroid artery — common carotid artery; IJV; vagus; inferior thyroid artery passing medially deep to the carotid. Defining the carotid gives a fixed lateral border. The inferior thyroid artery trunk, if preserved, leads medially to the RLN crossing.
A-reop-thyroid.layer4: RLN in a virgin segment — RLN low in the tracheoesophageal groove near the thoracic inlet; RLN at the laryngeal entry deep to the inferior constrictor, behind the cricothyroid joint. Find the nerve below or above the scar and trace it into the scarred segment. Neuromonitoring (with vagal stimulation) helps confirm function, but the nerve must still be seen.
A-reop-thyroid.layer5: Superior pole remnant — superior thyroid artery and vein; external branch of SLN on the inferior constrictor; sternothyroid insertion on the oblique line; superior parathyroid. Ligate superior pole branches close to the remnant capsule. The EBSLN often runs within the sternothyroid-laryngeal (Jolles/Cernea) triangle on the inferior constrictor, so stay on the capsule and ligate branches individually low on the pole.
A-reop-thyroid.layer6: Thyroid bed and Berry ligament — remnant thyroid tissue; Berry (posterior suspensory) ligament on the cricoid/upper tracheal rings; RLN passing deep to or through the ligament region. Residual tissue often sits at Berry ligament or the pyramidal lobe. Release the ligament only after the nerve has been traced to its laryngeal entry.
A-reop-thyroid.layer7: Parathyroid assessment — remaining parathyroids (possibly displaced or scar-embedded); their pedicles. Assume some glands were lost at the first operation. Preserve any gland on its pedicle; autotransplant tissue that is devascularized after frozen-section confirmation.
A-reop-thyroid.layer8: Deep limits — trachea (medial); esophagus (posteromedial, left of trachea); prevertebral fascia. Scar may draw the esophagus forward into the bed. A bougie or nasogastric tube helps palpate it.
A-reop-thyroid.landmark1: Lateral (back-door) plane — Retract the SCM laterally and the strap muscles medially; open the areolar plane along the medial carotid sheath and follow it deep to the posterior thyroid bed (Usually unscarred after a midline-approach first operation)
A-reop-thyroid.landmark2: RLN low in the groove — At or just above the thoracic inlet, in the tracheoesophageal groove (left nearly vertical; right more oblique from lateral), below the level of prior dissection (Virgin nerve segment that can be traced upward into scar)
A-reop-thyroid.landmark3: RLN laryngeal entry — Palpate the inferior cornu of the thyroid cartilage / cricothyroid joint; the nerve enters deep to the lower border of the inferior constrictor just posterior to it (Fixed bony landmark unaffected by scar)
A-reop-thyroid.landmark4: Common carotid artery — Palpate pulse lateral to the scarred bed; open the sheath medially (Lateral boundary; prevents straying into the IJV or vagus)
A-reop-thyroid.landmark5: Esophagus — Palpate an indwelling bougie or nasogastric tube posterior and left of the trachea; look for longitudinal muscle fibers (Defines the deep limit when scar distorts the groove)
A-reop-thyroid.landmark6: Vagus nerve (for monitoring) — Posterior in the carotid sheath between the carotid and IJV (Vagal stimulation before and after dissection tests the whole RLN pathway)
A-reop-thyroid.danger1: Recurrent laryngeal nerve — where: Embedded in scar in the tracheoesophageal groove or adherent to the remnant at Berry ligament; may be displaced anteriorly; protect: Identify in a virgin segment and trace it; neuromonitoring with vagal stimulation; do not divide any band until confirmed not to be nerve; injury: Vocal fold paralysis; bilateral injury (especially if contralateral nerve already weak) can require tracheostomy
A-reop-thyroid.danger2: Remaining parathyroids — where: Displaced, scar-embedded or already reduced in number after the first operation; protect: Assume they are few; preserve pedicles; autotransplant devascularized tissue; injury: Permanent hypoparathyroidism (higher risk than primary surgery)
A-reop-thyroid.danger3: External branch of SLN — where: On the inferior constrictor near the superior pole remnant; protect: Ligate branches on the capsule; avoid mass ligation of the pedicle; injury: Reduced pitch range and voice projection
A-reop-thyroid.danger4: Carotid artery / IJV — where: Lateral border of the bed, scar may pull them medially; protect: Define the sheath early through the lateral approach; injury: Major hemorrhage
A-reop-thyroid.danger5: Esophagus and trachea — where: Posterior and medial walls of the scarred bed; protect: Bougie for palpation; sharp dissection on the remnant rather than the viscus; injury: Perforation, mediastinitis, fistula
A-reop-thyroid.variant1: Nonrecurrent right laryngeal nerve with aberrant right subclavian artery; check prior op note and CT.
A-reop-thyroid.variant2: Pyramidal lobe or thyroglossal tract remnant as a site of residual tissue cephalad to the isthmus.
A-reop-thyroid.variant3: Extralaryngeal RLN branching; a single branch may have been mistaken for the whole nerve at the first operation.
A-reop-thyroid.variant4: Displaced or previously autotransplanted parathyroid tissue (forearm or SCM site) documented in the prior record.
A-reop-thyroid.supply1: Arterial supply — Superior thyroid artery (ECA) and inferior thyroid artery (thyrocervical trunk); both may have been ligated at the first operation
A-reop-thyroid.supply2: Parathyroid supply — Mainly inferior thyroid artery branches; collateral supply can come from tracheal and esophageal vessels
A-reop-thyroid.supply3: Motor nerves — RLN (intrinsic laryngeal muscles except cricothyroid); EBSLN (cricothyroid)
A-reop-thyroid.supply4: Nerve monitoring targets — Vagus in the carotid sheath; RLN at its laryngeal entry
A-reop-thyroid.pimp1: Q: What is the 'back-door' or lateral approach in reoperative thyroid surgery? A: Entering between the strap muscles and the SCM/carotid sheath to reach the posterior thyroid bed through unscarred tissue
A-reop-thyroid.pimp2: Q: Where should you look for the RLN in a reoperative neck? A: In a virgin segment: low in the tracheoesophageal groove near the inlet, or at the laryngeal entry behind the cricothyroid joint
A-reop-thyroid.pimp3: Q: Why stimulate the vagus rather than only the RLN? A: It tests the entire nerve pathway and detects nonrecurrent anatomy (no RLN signal from a low vagal point)
A-reop-thyroid.pimp4: Q: What is Berry ligament and why does it matter? A: The posterior suspensory ligament fixing the thyroid to the cricoid and upper tracheal rings; the RLN passes immediately deep to or through it and residual tissue often remains there
A-reop-thyroid.pimp5: Q: Where does the RLN enter the larynx? A: Deep to the inferior constrictor (cricopharyngeus) just posterior to the cricothyroid joint
A-reop-thyroid.pimp6: Q: Name two common sites of residual thyroid tissue after thyroidectomy. A: Berry ligament region and the pyramidal lobe; also the superior pole
A-reop-thyroid.pimp7: Q: Why is preoperative laryngoscopy mandatory before reoperation? A: To detect an existing unilateral paralysis, which changes risk (bilateral paralysis) and may change the extent or side of surgery
OR-reop-thyroid.step1: Enter through the prior low transverse incision and raise only the flaps necessary to obtain safe exposure of the remaining thyroid bed.
OR-reop-thyroid.step2: Separate the strap muscles through the least-scarred plane and mobilize the residual lobe while preserving the option to identify the RLN away from dense scar.
OR-reop-thyroid.step3: Control the superior pole close to the thyroid capsule, protecting the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve.
OR-reop-thyroid.step4: Identify the RLN at a reliable point—often inferiorly in the tracheoesophageal groove or at its laryngeal entry—and trace it through scar before dividing Berry-region attachments.
OR-reop-thyroid.step5: Identify and preserve viable parathyroid tissue and its blood supply; autotransplant clearly devascularized parathyroid tissue when appropriate.
OR-reop-thyroid.step6: Divide terminal thyroid vessels on the capsule and release Berry ligament only under direct knowledge of the RLN course.
OR-reop-thyroid.step7: Remove the remaining thyroid tissue/specimen, orient as needed, and address indicated central nodes only when part of the oncologic plan.
OR-reop-thyroid.step8: Reassess nerve signal/function, parathyroid viability, and hemostasis with Valsalva before closure.

## Sialendoscopy  (slug: sialendoscopy, region: Thyroid / Parathyroid / Salivary)

A-sialendoscopy.summary: Sialendoscopy treats obstruction (stones, stenosis, mucus plugs) from inside the duct. The papilla is the narrowest point. The Wharton duct runs upward and back around the mylohyoid to the hilum, with the lingual nerve crossing beneath it in the floor of mouth. The Stensen duct turns sharply at the anterior masseter border where it pierces the buccinator. Knowing these bends and the lingual nerve relationship prevents false passage, perforation, and nerve injury during combined transoral removal.
A-sialendoscopy.layer1: Papilla — Wharton papilla on the sublingual caruncle beside the frenulum; Stensen papilla on the buccal mucosa opposite the upper second molar. The papilla is the tightest point. Dilate gradually with lacrimal probes and dilators, or do a limited papillotomy. Massaging the gland to express saliva helps find the opening.
A-sialendoscopy.layer2: Distal duct — distal Wharton duct beneath the sublingual fold; sublingual gland and its ducts (superficial / lateral); buccinator and masseter for Stensen. Advance with continuous irrigation, keeping the lumen centered. Stensen has a sharp bend at the anterior masseter border; pulling the cheek forward and outward straightens it.
A-sialendoscopy.layer3: Mid duct and nerve crossing — Wharton duct over the hyoglossus; lingual nerve looping beneath the duct (lateral to medial), around the second and third molar region. This is the zone where a transoral duct incision for stone removal is made. Open the duct directly over the stone, which is easiest anterior to where the lingual nerve crosses.
A-sialendoscopy.layer4: Hilum / genu — Wharton duct bend around the posterior free border of the mylohyoid; hilum of the submandibular gland; parotid hilum and intraglandular branches. The genu at the hilum is a common place for stones to lodge and is hard to pass. Hilar stones may need a combined transoral approach with lingual nerve identification.
A-sialendoscopy.layer5: Intraglandular branches — primary and secondary ductal branches. Survey the branch points for stones, plugs and stenosis. Current scopes usually reach first-order and second-order branches, and sometimes third-order branches, depending on scope size and duct caliber.
A-sialendoscopy.layer6: Treatment and stenting — stone basket; laser or pneumatic fragmentation; duct stent at the papilla. Pass the basket beyond the stone, close it, and withdraw under vision. Fragment stones too large to pass the papilla or duct. Place a stent after ductotomy or for stenosis.
A-sialendoscopy.landmark1: Wharton papilla — On the sublingual caruncle just lateral to the lingual frenulum; dry the area and massage the gland to see saliva (Entry point; easily torn by forced entry)
A-sialendoscopy.landmark2: Stensen papilla — Buccal mucosa opposite the upper second molar, sometimes on a small fold; express by pressing the parotid (Entry point)
A-sialendoscopy.landmark3: Stensen bend — At the anterior masseter border where the duct turns medially through the buccinator; pull the cheek forward to straighten the path (Common site of false passage)
A-sialendoscopy.landmark4: Lingual nerve in the floor of mouth — Runs under the Wharton duct from lateral to medial around the second and third molar region, just above the mylohyoid. In a combined approach, see it posterior in the incision before deep dissection. (Protect during transoral stone removal)
A-sialendoscopy.landmark5: Submandibular hilum — Where the duct bends around the posterior free border of the mylohyoid; palpate bimanually (Common stone site and where the duct is hardest to pass)
A-sialendoscopy.danger1: Duct wall — where: Papilla, Stensen bend, Wharton genu; protect: Gradual dilation, irrigation, keeping the lumen centered, and no forcing; injury: Perforation, false passage, extravasation, later stenosis
A-sialendoscopy.danger2: Lingual nerve — where: Under the Wharton duct in the floor of mouth during a transoral incision; protect: Incise the mucosa directly over the duct and stone, and identify the nerve posteriorly before deep dissection; injury: Tongue numbness and loss of taste
A-sialendoscopy.danger3: Basket entrapment — where: Stone captured in a narrow segment, often at the papilla or bend; protect: Capture only stones that can pass the narrowest point; fragment first; injury: Impaction needing open removal
A-sialendoscopy.danger4: Sublingual gland — where: Lateral to the distal Wharton duct; protect: Gentle dissection in transoral approaches; injury: Ranula
A-sialendoscopy.danger5: Facial nerve buccal branch / Stensen duct (combined parotid approach) — where: Running together over the masseter; protect: Identify the buccal branch when external access is used; injury: Buccal branch weakness, salivary fistula
A-sialendoscopy.variant1: Papillary or ductal stenosis (in parotid disease, often radioiodine-related or inflammatory)
A-sialendoscopy.variant2: Wharton duct opening in common with the sublingual (Bartholin) duct
A-sialendoscopy.variant3: Accessory parotid gland duct joining Stensen over the masseter
A-sialendoscopy.variant4: Megaduct or dilated duct proximal to a stricture
A-sialendoscopy.variant5: Juvenile recurrent parotitis with a sialectatic duct system
A-sialendoscopy.supply1: Sensory — Lingual nerve (V3) to the floor of mouth; buccal nerve (V3) to the buccal mucosa
A-sialendoscopy.supply2: Parasympathetic — Submandibular: chorda tympani through the lingual nerve to the submandibular ganglion. Parotid: lesser petrosal nerve through the otic ganglion and auriculotemporal nerve.
A-sialendoscopy.supply3: Arterial — Sublingual (lingual artery) and submental (facial artery) in the floor of mouth; transverse facial artery along Stensen
A-sialendoscopy.pimp1: Q: Where does Stensen duct open, and what does it pierce? A: Opposite the upper second molar, after crossing the masseter and piercing the buccinator
A-sialendoscopy.pimp2: Q: Which gland forms most salivary stones? A: Submandibular, in the large majority (roughly 80-90%)
A-sialendoscopy.pimp3: Q: Relationship of the lingual nerve to Wharton duct? A: Starts lateral, passes under the duct, and ends medial to it
A-sialendoscopy.pimp4: Q: Where do submandibular stones most commonly lodge? A: At the hilum / genu where the duct bends around the posterior border of the mylohyoid, and in the distal duct
A-sialendoscopy.pimp5: Q: What is the narrowest point of the salivary ducts? A: The papilla
A-sialendoscopy.pimp6: Q: What is a plunging ranula? A: A sublingual gland mucus extravasation cyst extending into the neck through or around the mylohyoid
A-sialendoscopy.pimp7: Q: Which ducts drain the sublingual gland? A: Multiple small ducts of Rivinus on the plica sublingualis, and sometimes a larger Bartholin duct joining Wharton
OR-sialendoscopy.step1: Identify the papilla and gently dilate the ductal orifice in graduated fashion rather than forcing the endoscope through a tight opening.
OR-sialendoscopy.step2: Enter the duct with continuous irrigation and advance under direct visualization, keeping the lumen centered to avoid false passage/perforation.
OR-sialendoscopy.step3: Survey the main duct and accessible branch points while noting stenosis, mucus plugs, stones, and duct-wall injury.
OR-sialendoscopy.step4: For a mobile stone, pass the basket beyond it, capture it under vision, and withdraw without avulsing the duct.
OR-sialendoscopy.step5: For an impacted stone or stenosis, use the planned dilation, laser/pneumatic fragmentation, or combined transoral approach while protecting lingual nerve anatomy when working in the floor of mouth.
OR-sialendoscopy.step6: Reinspect the treated segment for residual stone fragments and confirm free irrigation/salivary flow.
OR-sialendoscopy.step7: Place a duct stent only when indicated by stenosis, ductotomy, or mucosal injury and secure it without obstructing the papilla.
OR-sialendoscopy.step8: Finish with oral hemostasis and document duct patency and any combined incision.

## Submandibular Gland Excision  (slug: submandibular-gland, region: Thyroid / Parathyroid / Salivary)

A-submandibular-gland.summary: The submandibular gland lies in the digastric (submandibular) triangle and wraps around the free posterior border of the mylohyoid. Its superficial lobe is below the muscle and its deep lobe and duct run above it into the floor of mouth. Three nerves define the operation. The marginal mandibular nerve lies superficial, in the flap. The lingual nerve lies deep and superior, attached to the gland by the submandibular ganglion. The hypoglossal nerve lies deep and inferior on the hyoglossus. The facial artery hooks over the gland and is controlled at two points.
A-submandibular-gland.layer1: Incision and flap — skin crease about 2 fingerbreadths (3-4 cm) below the mandibular border; platysma; marginal mandibular branch (deep to platysma, superficial to the investing fascia). Incise through the platysma. Raise the flap in the subplatysmal plane, or incise the investing fascia low on the gland and raise it with the flap (subcapsular approach), so the marginal nerve stays protected in the superior flap.
A-submandibular-gland.layer2: Superficial gland and facial vein — superficial layer of deep cervical fascia over the gland; anterior facial vein; facial artery at the mandibular border; level IB nodes (perifacial). Ligate the facial vein low on the gland and reflect it superiorly with the fascia (Hayes Martin maneuver). This lifts the marginal nerve out of the field. Ligate the distal facial artery as it crosses the mandible if it is not being preserved.
A-submandibular-gland.layer3: Inferior and posterior gland — anterior and posterior bellies of digastric; intermediate tendon; stylohyoid; proximal facial artery (posterior gland, deep to the posterior belly). Free the gland from the digastric tendon. The proximal facial artery comes from beneath the posterior belly and stylohyoid, grooving the deep gland. Ligate it here as the second pedicle.
A-submandibular-gland.layer4: Anterior gland and mylohyoid — mylohyoid (free posterior border); nerve to mylohyoid and submental vessels (superficial to the mylohyoid). Retract the mylohyoid anteriorly with a right-angle retractor while pulling the gland inferiorly. This exposes the deep lobe, lingual nerve and duct in the space above the mylohyoid.
A-submandibular-gland.layer5: Lingual nerve and ganglion — lingual nerve (superior); submandibular ganglion; ganglion branches to the gland. Inferior traction on the gland pulls the lingual nerve down into a V. Divide the ganglion branches close to the gland, and the nerve springs back up.
A-submandibular-gland.layer6: Wharton duct and hypoglossal nerve — Wharton duct; hypoglossal nerve (inferior, on the hyoglossus); ranine veins; hyoglossus. The hypoglossal nerve lies deep and inferior to the duct on the hyoglossus, with its accompanying veins, just above and deep to the intermediate digastric tendon (the tendon is the lower border of Pirogoff triangle and XII the upper). Divide the duct as far anteriorly as needed (fully for stone disease) only after the lingual and hypoglossal nerves are identified.
A-submandibular-gland.layer7: Removal and closure — gland bed; lingual nerve; XII; facial artery stumps. Check the nerves and ligatures, use a drain if needed, and close the platysma and skin.
A-submandibular-gland.landmark1: Marginal mandibular branch — Deep to the platysma over the gland's fascia, crossing the facial vessels superficially near the mandibular border. Incise low and use the Hayes Martin maneuver. (The nerve at greatest risk in this exposure)
A-submandibular-gland.landmark2: Facial artery — Distal: hooks over the mandible at the anterior masseter border. Proximal: emerges deep to the posterior belly of digastric and stylohyoid, grooving the posterior gland. (Two separate ligation points)
A-submandibular-gland.landmark3: Lingual nerve — Retract the mylohyoid anteriorly and pull the gland down. The nerve is the flat band dragged into a V above the gland by the submandibular ganglion. (Divide the ganglion, not the nerve)
A-submandibular-gland.landmark4: Hypoglossal nerve — On the hyoglossus below the duct, just above and deep to the intermediate digastric tendon, with the ranine veins; keep the plane superficial to the fascia over the hyoglossus (Must be seen before dividing the duct or deep tissue)
A-submandibular-gland.landmark5: Wharton duct — Leaves the deep lobe and runs anteriorly above the mylohyoid between the hyoglossus and mylohyoid; the lingual nerve crosses it (Ligate after identifying both nerves)
A-submandibular-gland.landmark6: Pirogoff (lingual) triangle — Bounded by the intermediate digastric tendon, the posterior border of the mylohyoid and the hypoglossal nerve; the lingual artery is deep to the hyoglossus here (Locates the lingual artery if there is bleeding)
A-submandibular-gland.danger1: Marginal mandibular branch — where: Deep to the platysma, superficial to the gland fascia, usually above the mandibular border but can lie below it, especially posterior to the facial artery; protect: Low incision, subfascial or Hayes Martin elevation, and no cautery in the superior flap; injury: Lower lip depressor weakness and asymmetric smile
A-submandibular-gland.danger2: Lingual nerve — where: Above the deep lobe, joined to the gland by the submandibular ganglion; crosses the duct; protect: Divide ganglion branches next to the gland and identify the nerve before cutting the duct; injury: Ipsilateral tongue numbness and loss of taste (anterior two-thirds), tongue biting
A-submandibular-gland.danger3: Hypoglossal nerve — where: Deep and inferior on the hyoglossus, below the duct and just above / deep to the intermediate digastric tendon; protect: Stay superficial to the hyoglossus fascia and use bipolar only near the ranine veins; injury: Ipsilateral tongue weakness, deviation toward the injured side
A-submandibular-gland.danger4: Facial artery and vein — where: Distal at the mandible; proximal deep to the posterior digastric; protect: Ligate securely at both points; injury: Hematoma, airway compromise
A-submandibular-gland.variant1: Marginal mandibular branch running below the mandible (more common posteriorly and in older patients), or present as multiple branches
A-submandibular-gland.variant2: Facial artery passing through the gland rather than in a groove
A-submandibular-gland.variant3: Large deep lobe extending far into the floor of mouth
A-submandibular-gland.variant4: Accessory or duplicated duct; communication with the sublingual gland (Bartholin duct)
A-submandibular-gland.variant5: Mylohyoid dehiscence (route for a plunging ranula)
A-submandibular-gland.supply1: Arterial — Facial artery (main) with its submental branch; sublingual branch of the lingual artery
A-submandibular-gland.supply2: Venous — Anterior facial vein to the common facial vein to the IJV
A-submandibular-gland.supply3: Parasympathetic — Superior salivatory nucleus, then nervus intermedius, chorda tympani, lingual nerve, submandibular ganglion to the gland
A-submandibular-gland.supply4: Neighbouring nerves — Marginal mandibular (VII) superficial; lingual (V3) deep and superior; hypoglossal (XII) deep and inferior; nerve to mylohyoid superficial to the mylohyoid
A-submandibular-gland.pimp1: Q: Boundaries of the submandibular triangle? A: Anterior and posterior bellies of digastric and the inferior border of the mandible
A-submandibular-gland.pimp2: Q: Describe the lingual nerve and duct relationship. A: The lingual nerve starts lateral to the duct, loops under it, and ends medial to it (the double crossing)
A-submandibular-gland.pimp3: Q: What lies superficial and deep to the hyoglossus? A: Superficial: XII, the duct and the lingual nerve. Deep: the lingual artery.
A-submandibular-gland.pimp4: Q: Boundaries of Pirogoff triangle? A: Intermediate digastric tendon, posterior border of the mylohyoid and the hypoglossal nerve; the floor is the hyoglossus, with the lingual artery deep
A-submandibular-gland.pimp5: Q: Why are stones more common in the submandibular gland? A: More alkaline, mucin-rich, calcium-rich saliva, and a long duct that runs upward against gravity
A-submandibular-gland.pimp6: Q: Trace the submandibular secretomotor pathway. A: Superior salivatory nucleus, nervus intermedius, chorda tympani, lingual nerve, submandibular ganglion
A-submandibular-gland.pimp7: Q: What does the marginal mandibular nerve innervate? A: Depressor anguli oris, depressor labii inferioris and mentalis
A-submandibular-gland.pimp8: Q: Where does Wharton duct open? A: Sublingual caruncle beside the lingual frenulum
OR-submandibular-gland.step1: Place the cervical incision below the mandibular border and elevate the flap in a plane that protects the marginal mandibular branch.
OR-submandibular-gland.step2: Identify the submandibular gland capsule and develop the inferior/lateral surface, controlling facial vein/artery branches as needed.
OR-submandibular-gland.step3: Retract the mylohyoid anteriorly to expose the deep lobe and identify the lingual nerve with the submandibular ganglion/duct relationship.
OR-submandibular-gland.step4: Separate the gland from the lingual nerve, dividing the ganglion branches close to the gland while preserving the nerve.
OR-submandibular-gland.step5: Identify Wharton duct and the hypoglossal nerve deep/inferior to it before dividing the duct.
OR-submandibular-gland.step6: Control the glandular facial-artery pedicle and release the remaining attachments without thermal injury to lingual or hypoglossal nerves.
OR-submandibular-gland.step7: Remove the gland/specimen and inspect the wound for nerve integrity and bleeding.
OR-submandibular-gland.step8: Irrigate, obtain hemostasis, place a drain selectively, and close in layers.

## Thyroid Lobectomy  (slug: thyroid-lobectomy, region: Thyroid / Parathyroid / Salivary)

A-thyroid-lobectomy.summary: Thyroid lobectomy is a dissection on the thyroid capsule that removes the lobe while keeping two nerves and two glands safe. The external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve (EBSLN) runs near the superior pole vessels. The RLN runs up the tracheoesophageal groove to enter the larynx at Berry ligament. The superior and inferior parathyroids keep their blood supply only if inferior thyroid artery branches are divided on the capsule. The highest-risk point is Berry ligament, where the RLN is closest to the gland.
A-thyroid-lobectomy.layer1: Incision and subplatysmal flaps — skin crease incision about 1-2 fingerbreadths above the sternal notch; platysma; anterior jugular veins (superficial to the strap fascia). Raise flaps superiorly to the thyroid notch and inferiorly to the sternal notch in the subplatysmal plane, leaving the anterior jugular veins down on the straps.
A-thyroid-lobectomy.layer2: Strap muscles — linea alba; sternohyoid; sternothyroid (inserts on the oblique line of the thyroid cartilage); ansa cervicalis (enters the straps from below and laterally). Separate the straps in the midline. If they must be divided for a large gland, divide them high so the ansa innervation from below is preserved.
A-thyroid-lobectomy.layer3: Lateral mobilization — false (pretracheal) fascia and true capsule; middle thyroid vein; carotid sheath. Peel the sternothyroid off the lobe. Divide the middle thyroid vein so the lobe can be rotated medially.
A-thyroid-lobectomy.layer4: Superior pole — superior thyroid artery and vein; sternothyroid-laryngeal (Joll) triangle; EBSLN on the inferior constrictor; cricothyroid muscle. Pull the pole down and laterally to open the avascular plane between the pole and the cricothyroid. Ligate branches individually on the capsule, below where the EBSLN may cross.
A-thyroid-lobectomy.layer5: Inferior pole — inferior thyroid veins; thyrothymic ligament; inferior parathyroid. Divide the inferior veins on the capsule. Sweep the inferior parathyroid and thymic tissue away with their blood supply intact.
A-thyroid-lobectomy.layer6: RLN identification — RLN; ITA; tubercle of Zuckerkandl; inferior cornu of thyroid cartilage. Find the nerve where the anatomy is undisturbed: inferiorly in the groove, laterally at the ITA, or superiorly at the laryngeal entry. Then follow it upward as the lobe is rotated.
A-thyroid-lobectomy.layer7: Superior parathyroid and terminal vessels — superior parathyroid; ITA terminal branches on the capsule. Mobilize the superior gland off the posterior capsule on its pedicle. Divide ITA branches on the capsule, not at the trunk.
A-thyroid-lobectomy.layer8: Berry ligament and isthmus — posterior suspensory (Berry) ligament; RLN at laryngeal entry; cricoid and first tracheal rings; isthmus / pyramidal lobe; Delphian (prelaryngeal) node. Divide Berry ligament sharply under direct vision of the nerve, with no energy device close to it. Divide the isthmus and remove any pyramidal lobe up toward the hyoid.
A-thyroid-lobectomy.landmark1: EBSLN — Retract the superior pole inferolaterally and open Joll triangle (medially the cricothyroid / inferior constrictor, laterally the pole vessels, superiorly the sternothyroid insertion). Look for the nerve on the inferior constrictor running down to the cricothyroid, or confirm by monitor (cricothyroid twitch). (Avoid the ligature and energy zone when it runs low (Cernea 2b))
A-thyroid-lobectomy.landmark2: RLN, inferior approach — Low in the tracheoesophageal groove near the thoracic inlet. The right nerve runs more obliquely from lateral to medial; the left is more vertical and closer to the groove. (Virgin territory, useful for large or revision cases)
A-thyroid-lobectomy.landmark3: RLN, lateral approach — At the ITA crossing at mid-lobe; the nerve may pass anterior, posterior or between ITA branches (Most commonly used point of identification)
A-thyroid-lobectomy.landmark4: RLN, superior approach — At its entry under the inferior constrictor just posterior and inferior to the inferior cornu of the thyroid cartilage / cricothyroid joint (Constant position; useful for large substernal goiters)
A-thyroid-lobectomy.landmark5: Tubercle of Zuckerkandl — Posterolateral projection at the cricoid level; lift it medially (The RLN usually lies medial and deep to it; the superior parathyroid sits nearby)
A-thyroid-lobectomy.landmark6: Berry ligament — Dense attachment of the posteromedial lobe to the cricoid and first tracheal rings, just medial to the nerve's entry point (Point of closest nerve-to-gland contact)
A-thyroid-lobectomy.danger1: Recurrent laryngeal nerve — where: Tracheoesophageal groove to Berry ligament; may branch extralaryngeally; protect: Visual identification along its course, no energy device within a few mm, minimal traction on the lobe at Berry ligament, and neuromonitoring as an adjunct; injury: Vocal fold paralysis, dysphonia, aspiration
A-thyroid-lobectomy.danger2: EBSLN — where: Close to the superior thyroid artery near the upper pole, especially when it crosses below the top of the pole; protect: Individual capsular ligation of branches and no mass ligation high on the pedicle; injury: Cricothyroid paralysis, causing loss of pitch range and projection and voice fatigue
A-thyroid-lobectomy.danger3: Parathyroid glands and pedicles — where: Superior gland on the posterior upper lobe; inferior gland at the lower pole; protect: Capsular dissection, divide ITA branches distal to the parathyroid branches, and autotransplant if devascularized; injury: Hypocalcemia (relevant if the contralateral glands are later removed)
A-thyroid-lobectomy.danger4: Trachea and esophagus — where: Medial and posterior to the lobe at Berry ligament; protect: Sharp dissection and care with energy devices; injury: Tracheal or esophageal injury
A-thyroid-lobectomy.danger5: Hematoma — where: Under the straps in the central neck; protect: Valsalva check and ligation of the superior pole vessels individually; injury: Airway compromise; open the wound urgently
A-thyroid-lobectomy.variant1: Nonrecurrent right inferior laryngeal nerve (aberrant right subclavian)
A-thyroid-lobectomy.variant2: Extralaryngeal RLN branching before laryngeal entry
A-thyroid-lobectomy.variant3: Cernea type 2b EBSLN, crossing the pedicle below the upper border of the pole
A-thyroid-lobectomy.variant4: Pyramidal lobe (thyroglossal remnant) extending toward the hyoid
A-thyroid-lobectomy.variant5: Thyroid ima artery from the brachiocephalic trunk or aortic arch (small minority)
A-thyroid-lobectomy.supply1: Arterial — Superior thyroid artery (first anterior ECA branch); ITA (thyrocervical trunk); thyroid ima (variable)
A-thyroid-lobectomy.supply2: Venous — Superior and middle thyroid veins to the IJV; inferior thyroid veins to the brachiocephalic veins
A-thyroid-lobectomy.supply3: Motor — RLN to all intrinsic laryngeal muscles except the cricothyroid; EBSLN to the cricothyroid; ansa cervicalis to the straps
A-thyroid-lobectomy.supply4: Embryology — Median anlage from the foramen cecum, descending as the thyroglossal duct; lateral anlage (ultimobranchial body, fourth pouch) gives parafollicular C cells
A-thyroid-lobectomy.pimp1: Q: Around which vessels does each RLN loop? A: Right: the subclavian artery. Left: the aortic arch at the ligamentum arteriosum.
A-thyroid-lobectomy.pimp2: Q: Boundaries of Joll (sternothyroid-laryngeal) triangle? A: Medially the cricothyroid / inferior constrictor, laterally the superior pole vessels, superiorly the sternothyroid insertion on the oblique line
A-thyroid-lobectomy.pimp3: Q: Describe the Cernea classification. A: EBSLN crosses the STA: type 1 more than 1 cm above the upper pole, type 2a within 1 cm above it, type 2b below the upper border of the pole
A-thyroid-lobectomy.pimp4: Q: What does the EBSLN innervate, and what is the deficit? A: Cricothyroid; loss of pitch elevation and projection, voice fatigue
A-thyroid-lobectomy.pimp5: Q: Where does the RLN enter the larynx? A: Under the inferior constrictor, posterior to the cricothyroid joint (inferior cornu of the thyroid cartilage)
A-thyroid-lobectomy.pimp6: Q: Where does the thyroid arise embryologically? A: Foramen cecum of the tongue; descends anterior to the hyoid as the thyroglossal duct
A-thyroid-lobectomy.pimp7: Q: Why divide the straps high? A: The ansa cervicalis enters them inferiorly, so high division preserves innervation
A-thyroid-lobectomy.pimp8: Q: What is the Delphian node? A: The prelaryngeal node on the cricothyroid membrane
OR-thyroid-lobectomy.step1: Make a low transverse incision, elevate subplatysmal flaps, and open the strap-muscle midline to expose the involved thyroid lobe.
OR-thyroid-lobectomy.step2: Separate the strap muscles from the capsule, mobilize the lateral lobe, and divide the middle thyroid vein when present to allow medial rotation.
OR-thyroid-lobectomy.step3: Control the superior pole vessels individually and close to the thyroid capsule while protecting the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve.
OR-thyroid-lobectomy.step4: Mobilize the inferior pole while preserving the inferior parathyroid and its vascular pedicle.
OR-thyroid-lobectomy.step5: Identify the recurrent laryngeal nerve in a safe segment and follow it toward the laryngeal entry point as the lobe is rotated medially.
OR-thyroid-lobectomy.step6: Identify and preserve the superior parathyroid; divide terminal inferior-thyroid branches on the capsule rather than away from the gland.
OR-thyroid-lobectomy.step7: Divide Berry ligament and final tracheal attachments under direct RLN visualization, then divide the isthmus and remove/orient the lobe.
OR-thyroid-lobectomy.step8: Confirm hemostasis with Valsalva and reassess nerve integrity before layered closure.

## Total Thyroidectomy  (slug: total-thyroidectomy, region: Thyroid / Parathyroid / Salivary)

A-total-thyroidectomy.summary: Total thyroidectomy repeats the capsular lobectomy on both sides. What changes is the stakes: both RLNs and all four parathyroids are at risk at once. Bilateral RLN injury leaves both vocal folds paramedian and threatens the airway. Losing the blood supply of all parathyroids causes permanent hypocalcemia. The anatomy differs by side (left RLN vertical in the groove, right RLN oblique, and a possible right nonrecurrent nerve), and confirming nerve function after the first side decides whether to proceed.
A-total-thyroidectomy.layer1: Exposure — skin; platysma; anterior jugular veins; linea alba; sternohyoid and sternothyroid; pyramidal lobe / Delphian node. Use a low transverse incision and subplatysmal flaps to the thyroid notch and sternal notch. Open the midline raphe fully. Look for a pyramidal lobe along the midline toward the hyoid; it must be removed in a total thyroidectomy.
A-total-thyroidectomy.layer2: First lobe: lateral and superior pole — middle thyroid vein; superior thyroid vessels; EBSLN; Joll triangle. As for lobectomy: divide the middle vein, open the avascular space medial to the superior pole, and ligate branches individually on the capsule.
A-total-thyroidectomy.layer3: First lobe: parathyroids and RLN — inferior parathyroid / thyrothymic ligament; RLN; ITA; superior parathyroid; tubercle of Zuckerkandl. Preserve each gland on its pedicle as found. Identify the RLN and follow it up to Berry ligament, dividing terminal ITA branches on the capsule.
A-total-thyroidectomy.layer4: Berry ligament and isthmus — Berry ligament; RLN entry; isthmus; trachea. Release sharply under vision and carry the specimen across the trachea.
A-total-thyroidectomy.layer5: Decision point — ipsilateral vagus / RLN signal (if monitored); visual nerve integrity. If there is unexplained loss of signal on the first side, consider staging the contralateral lobectomy to avoid bilateral paralysis.
A-total-thyroidectomy.layer6: Contralateral lobe — contralateral superior pole and EBSLN; contralateral parathyroids; contralateral RLN (opposite course); Berry ligament. Repeat every step in full. Expect a different nerve course: the left RLN is more vertical, deeper and in the groove; the right is more oblique and lateral, and a right nonrecurrent nerve runs directly from the vagus at about the laryngeal level.
A-total-thyroidectomy.layer7: Final survey — both parathyroid beds; both RLNs; hemostasis (Valsalva). Inspect parathyroid color. Autotransplant any devascularized gland into the SCM after a frozen-section confirmation sliver if there is doubt.
A-total-thyroidectomy.landmark1: Left RLN — Rises more vertically and medially in the tracheoesophageal groove from the aortic arch; find it low in the groove or at the ITA (More predictable course but closely applied to the trachea)
A-total-thyroidectomy.landmark2: Right RLN — Runs obliquely from lateral (from the subclavian) to medial. It is more exposed and lateral in the lower neck and enters the groove higher up. If none is found low, look for a nonrecurrent nerve running horizontally from the vagus near the cricoid / ITA level. (Higher risk of a variant course)
A-total-thyroidectomy.landmark3: EBSLN (both sides) — Joll triangle with the pole retracted inferolaterally; nerve on the inferior constrictor heading to the cricothyroid (Bilateral injury causes significant pitch and voice-fatigue loss)
A-total-thyroidectomy.landmark4: Four parathyroids — Superior: posterior upper-mid lobe, dorsal to the RLN, about 1 cm above the ITA crossing. Inferior: near the lower pole, ventral to the RLN, or in the thyrothymic ligament. (Keeping each on its pedicle prevents permanent hypoparathyroidism)
A-total-thyroidectomy.landmark5: Berry ligament — Posteromedial attachment to the cricoid and upper trachea at the nerve entry point (Point of greatest nerve risk on each side)
A-total-thyroidectomy.landmark6: Pyramidal lobe — Superior extension from the isthmus, usually left of midline, toward the hyoid along the thyroglossal tract (Residual tissue affects radioiodine uptake and Tg surveillance)
A-total-thyroidectomy.danger1: Bilateral RLNs — where: Both tracheoesophageal grooves and Berry ligaments; protect: Identify both nerves, monitor and confirm the signal after the first side, and stage if loss of signal occurs; injury: Bilateral vocal fold paralysis with stridor and airway obstruction; possible tracheostomy
A-total-thyroidectomy.danger2: All parathyroids and ITA branches — where: Posterior capsule bilaterally; protect: Capsular dissection, branch ligation distal to the parathyroid branches, and autotransplant of devascularized glands; injury: Transient or permanent hypoparathyroidism and hypocalcemia
A-total-thyroidectomy.danger3: EBSLN (bilateral) — where: Superior poles; protect: Individual capsular ligation; injury: Loss of pitch range and projection
A-total-thyroidectomy.danger4: Trachea and esophagus — where: Under the isthmus and at Berry ligament; at risk in large goiters and with tracheomalacia; protect: Sharp dissection and limited energy use; injury: Tracheal perforation or injury, esophageal injury
A-total-thyroidectomy.danger5: Neck hematoma — where: Central compartment, often from the superior pole or strap vessels; protect: Valsalva, meticulous hemostasis and good closure; injury: Airway compromise needing bedside wound opening
A-total-thyroidectomy.danger6: Thermal spread — where: Energy devices near the nerve or parathyroid pedicles; protect: Keep active blades a few mm from the nerve and turn the blade away; injury: Neurapraxia or delayed paralysis
A-total-thyroidectomy.variant1: Right nonrecurrent laryngeal nerve (aberrant right subclavian artery); a left nonrecurrent nerve needs situs inversus and is exceptionally rare
A-total-thyroidectomy.variant2: Extralaryngeal RLN branching (motor fibres usually in the anterior branch)
A-total-thyroidectomy.variant3: Pyramidal lobe, thyroglossal tract remnants
A-total-thyroidectomy.variant4: Substernal extension, usually supplied from the neck by the ITA
A-total-thyroidectomy.variant5: Intrathyroidal or ectopic parathyroid
A-total-thyroidectomy.supply1: Arterial — Bilateral STA and ITA; occasional thyroid ima
A-total-thyroidectomy.supply2: Venous — Superior and middle thyroid veins to the IJV; inferior thyroid veins to the brachiocephalic veins
A-total-thyroidectomy.supply3: Nerves — Bilateral RLNs, bilateral EBSLNs, ansa cervicalis to the straps
A-total-thyroidectomy.supply4: Parathyroid supply — ITA branches primarily
A-total-thyroidectomy.pimp1: Q: Position of the vocal folds after bilateral RLN injury? A: Paramedian. The voice is often fairly good, but the glottic airway is narrow, causing stridor and airway compromise
A-total-thyroidectomy.pimp2: Q: Why is the right RLN at greater risk of variant anatomy? A: A nonrecurrent course occurs on the right with an aberrant retroesophageal subclavian; the right also runs more obliquely and laterally
A-total-thyroidectomy.pimp3: Q: What is loss of signal on neuromonitoring? A: Usually defined as an evoked EMG amplitude below 100 microvolts with a previously good signal (after excluding equipment problems)
A-total-thyroidectomy.pimp4: Q: How do you confirm devascularized tissue is parathyroid before autotransplantation? A: Frozen section of a small sliver; then mince it into SCM pockets
A-total-thyroidectomy.pimp5: Q: Clinical signs of hypocalcemia? A: Chvostek (facial nerve tap) and Trousseau (carpal spasm with cuff); perioral and digital paresthesia
A-total-thyroidectomy.pimp6: Q: Which branch of the RLN usually carries the motor fibres when it branches extralaryngeally? A: The anterior branch
A-total-thyroidectomy.pimp7: Q: What remnant can lie between the isthmus and the hyoid? A: Pyramidal lobe / thyroglossal duct remnant
A-total-thyroidectomy.pimp8: Q: Blood supply of a substernal goiter? A: Usually cervical (ITA); it can usually be delivered through the neck
OR-total-thyroidectomy.step1: Make a low transverse cervical incision, raise superior and inferior subplatysmal flaps, and divide the linea alba between the strap muscles to expose the thyroid.
OR-total-thyroidectomy.step2: Choose the first lobe, free the strap muscles from its capsule, mobilize the lateral surface, and divide the middle thyroid vein when present to permit medial rotation.
OR-total-thyroidectomy.step3: Develop the superior pole and control individual superior thyroid vessels close to the capsule while keeping the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve out of the ligature/energy field.
OR-total-thyroidectomy.step4: Mobilize the inferior pole and identify the inferior parathyroid/thyrothymic tissue, preserving its vascular supply rather than skeletonizing the gland.
OR-total-thyroidectomy.step5: Identify the recurrent laryngeal nerve in a safe segment and trace it toward the cricothyroid joint/laryngeal entry as the thyroid is rotated medially.
OR-total-thyroidectomy.step6: Identify the superior parathyroid on the posterior capsule, preserve its pedicle, and divide terminal inferior-thyroid branches directly on the thyroid capsule.
OR-total-thyroidectomy.step7: Release Berry ligament and the final tracheal attachments under direct visualization of the RLN, then remove the first lobe/isthmus as planned.
OR-total-thyroidectomy.step8: Before committing to the opposite side, reassess RLN function/monitoring if used; if there is unexplained loss of signal, consider staged completion rather than risking bilateral paralysis.
OR-total-thyroidectomy.step9: Repeat the complete capsular dissection on the contralateral side—superior pole, parathyroids, RLN, terminal vessels, then Berry ligament—rather than treating it as a shorthand repeat step.
OR-total-thyroidectomy.step10: Inspect both beds for hemostasis with Valsalva, confirm parathyroid viability and nerve integrity, then reapproximate straps/platysma and close the skin.

## Adult Laryngotracheal Reconstruction  (slug: adult-ltr, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-adult-ltr.summary: Adult LTR expands a stenotic cricoid and upper trachea by splitting the framework in the midline and inserting costal cartilage, instead of resecting the segment. Safety turns on staying in the midline: the recurrent laryngeal nerves enter the larynx just behind the cricothyroid joints laterally, the esophagus lies directly behind the posterior cricoid plate, and the anterior commissure sits just above any anterior split of the thyroid cartilage.
A-adult-ltr.layer1: Incision and flaps — skin; platysma; anterior jugular veins. Horizontal incision at about the cricoid level (or around an existing stoma); raise subplatysmal flaps from the hyoid to the sternal notch.
A-adult-ltr.layer2: Strap muscles — midline raphe; sternohyoid (superficial); sternothyroid (deep, inserts on thyroid cartilage oblique line); thyrohyoid. Separate the straps in the midline and retract laterally; avoid lateral dissection beyond the cricoid arch.
A-adult-ltr.layer3: Thyroid isthmus and airway exposure — thyroid isthmus (over rings 2-4); pyramidal lobe (variable); cricothyroid muscles (anterolateral); cricothyroid membrane; cricoid arch; upper tracheal rings. Divide and suture-ligate the isthmus; expose the thyroid cartilage, cricoid and upper trachea in the midline only.
A-adult-ltr.layer4: Anterior split — lower thyroid cartilage (below the anterior commissure); cricothyroid membrane; anterior cricoid arch; upper tracheal rings; stenotic scar. Vertical midline incision through scar and cartilage; open the thyroid cartilage only as high as needed and stay below the anterior commissure unless a laryngofissure is planned.
A-adult-ltr.layer5: Posterior split (when needed) — posterior cricoid lamina; interarytenoid muscle and scar; postcricoid tissue; anterior wall of hypopharynx/esophageal inlet. Divide the posterior cricoid plate exactly in the midline through full cartilage thickness until the postcricoid soft tissue bulges; do not enter hypopharyngeal or esophageal mucosa.
A-adult-ltr.layer6: Costal cartilage harvest — skin; pectoralis major / rectus abdominis fibers; costal cartilage (commonly 5th-7th); outer perichondrium; inner perichondrium; parietal pleura. Preserve the inner perichondrium over the pleura; check for air leak with a saline-filled wound and positive-pressure breath.
A-adult-ltr.layer7: Graft inset and stenting — graft flanges; cut cartilage edges; perichondrium facing the lumen; stent or endotracheal tube. Shape the graft with flanges so it cannot prolapse into the lumen; secure with absorbable sutures outside the mucosa; confirm lumen endoscopically.
A-adult-ltr.layer8: Closure — strap muscles over graft; drain; platysma and skin. Reapproximate straps over the graft for vascularized cover; drain to avoid fluid around the graft.
A-adult-ltr.landmark1: Cricoid arch — Firm ring palpable just below the thyroid cartilage lower border and cricothyroid membrane; first complete ring above the trachea (Central element of the stenosis and the reference for the split)
A-adult-ltr.landmark2: Midline — Thyroid notch above, anterior cricoid midpoint, and tracheal midline below; confirm with the endoscopic light or a needle seen from inside (Off-midline splits risk RLN injury and asymmetric expansion)
A-adult-ltr.landmark3: Anterior commissure — Roughly the midpoint of the vertical height of the thyroid cartilage in the midline (varies by sex/age); confirm with transillumination or a needle from inside (Splitting through it causes anterior web and dysphonia)
A-adult-ltr.landmark4: RLN entry point — Posterior to the cricothyroid joint, deep to the lower edge of the inferior constrictor; ascends in or near the tracheoesophageal groove (Stay medial to the cricothyroid joints and do not skeletonize the lateral cricoid)
A-adult-ltr.landmark5: Posterior cricoid midline — Viewed from inside after the anterior split: midline between the arytenoids, in line with the interarytenoid notch (Off-midline posterior split risks cricoarytenoid joint injury)
A-adult-ltr.danger1: Recurrent laryngeal nerves — where: Tracheoesophageal grooves, entering the larynx just posterior to the cricothyroid joints; protect: Midline dissection only; no lateral dissection around the cricoid; injury: Vocal fold paralysis; bilateral injury causes airway obstruction
A-adult-ltr.danger2: Esophagus / hypopharynx — where: Directly posterior to the posterior cricoid lamina; protect: Controlled full-thickness cartilage cut only, stop at the bulging soft tissue; feel for the nasogastric tube; injury: Pharyngoesophageal perforation, fistula, graft infection
A-adult-ltr.danger3: Anterior commissure — where: Midline thyroid cartilage at about mid-height; protect: Limit superior extent of the anterior split; precise midline closure if a laryngofissure is needed; injury: Anterior glottic web, voice change
A-adult-ltr.danger4: Cricoarytenoid joints — where: Posterior-superior cricoid lamina on each side of midline; protect: Exact midline posterior split; injury: Arytenoid fixation, posterior glottic dysfunction
A-adult-ltr.danger5: Pleura — where: Deep to the inner perichondrium of the costal cartilage; protect: Subperichondrial harvest leaving inner perichondrium; leak test; injury: Pneumothorax
A-adult-ltr.danger6: Innominate artery — where: Anterior to the lower cervical/upper thoracic trachea; protect: Avoid low dissection and stent/tube pressure against the anterior wall; injury: Hemorrhage, tracheoinnominate fistula
A-adult-ltr.variant1: High-riding innominate artery or low-lying thyroid isthmus
A-adult-ltr.variant2: Ossified laryngeal cartilage in older adults (needs saw or drill to split)
A-adult-ltr.variant3: Calcified costal cartilage in older patients limiting graft carving
A-adult-ltr.variant4: Prior tracheostomy with distorted anterior wall and suprastomal collapse
A-adult-ltr.supply1: Laryngeal arteries — Superior laryngeal artery (from superior thyroid) through the thyrohyoid membrane; cricothyroid artery across the membrane; inferior laryngeal artery (from inferior thyroid) with the RLN
A-adult-ltr.supply2: Tracheal supply — Lateral segmental branches of the inferior thyroid and bronchial arteries
A-adult-ltr.supply3: Motor — RLN to all intrinsic muscles except cricothyroid; external branch of SLN to cricothyroid
A-adult-ltr.supply4: Rib graft site — Intercostal neurovascular bundle runs along the inferior border of each rib
A-adult-ltr.pimp1: Q: What is the McCaffrey staging of adult laryngotracheal stenosis? A: I: subglottis or trachea under 1 cm; II: subglottic over 1 cm within cricoid, not glottis or trachea; III: subglottic extending into trachea, not glottis; IV: glottic involvement with vocal fold fixation
A-adult-ltr.pimp2: Q: What is the Cotton-Myer grading scale? A: Grade I 0-50%, II 51-70%, III 71-99% obstruction, IV no detectable lumen
A-adult-ltr.pimp3: Q: Where does the RLN enter the larynx? A: Posterior to the cricothyroid joint, deep to the inferior border of the inferior constrictor
A-adult-ltr.pimp4: Q: Which intrinsic laryngeal muscle is not innervated by the RLN? A: Cricothyroid (external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve)
A-adult-ltr.pimp5: Q: Which way does the perichondrium of a costal graft usually face? A: Toward the airway lumen
A-adult-ltr.pimp6: Q: What lies immediately posterior to the posterior cricoid plate? A: The postcricoid hypopharynx/esophageal inlet with the cricopharyngeus
A-adult-ltr.pimp7: Q: Which muscle is divided in a posterior split for posterior glottic stenosis? A: The interarytenoid (transverse/oblique arytenoid) muscle and scar along with the posterior cricoid lamina
A-adult-ltr.pimp8: Q: What lies along the inferior border of a rib? A: The intercostal vein, artery and nerve (VAN, superior to inferior)
OR-adult-ltr.step1: Expose the airway framework and define the full extent of the stenosis before committing to a graft plan.
OR-adult-ltr.step2: Incise the stenotic segment (anterior, or anterior and posterior for circumferential/posterior glottic disease) and harvest and shape a cartilage graft (commonly costal) to expand the lumen.
OR-adult-ltr.step3: Secure the graft, assess the resulting lumen endoscopically, and decide single-stage closure versus staged reconstruction with a stent based on the airway's stability and the extent of grafting performed.

## Endoscopic Airway Dilation  (slug: airway-dilation, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-airway-dilation.summary: Endoscopic dilation treats a narrowed subglottis or cervical trachea from scar (idiopathic, post-intubation, autoimmune) by stretching or radially cutting and then stretching the scar ring. Safety depends on knowing where the stenosis sits relative to the vocal folds and cricoid, sizing the balloon to the normal adjacent airway, and protecting the thin posterior membranous wall that separates the airway from the esophagus.
A-airway-dilation.layer1: Oral cavity and suspension — upper incisors and dentition; tongue base; vallecula; epiglottis. Protect the teeth with a guard and suspend a laryngoscope so the glottis and subglottis are in line. Avoid excessive tongue-base pressure (lingual nerve and tongue injury).
A-airway-dilation.layer2: Supraglottis and glottis — false folds; true vocal folds; anterior commissure; posterior commissure / interarytenoid area; arytenoids and cricoarytenoid joints. Inspect and palpate the arytenoids for mobility. Note any posterior glottic scar, which changes the diagnosis and treatment. The proximal end of the balloon must not sit on the vocal folds or anterior commissure.
A-airway-dilation.layer3: Subglottis — conus elasticus; inner surface of the cricoid ring; subglottic mucosa. The subglottis runs from just below the true folds to the lower edge of the cricoid. It is bounded by the only complete cartilaginous ring, so its caliber cannot expand outward. Idiopathic stenosis usually sits here and in the first tracheal rings.
A-airway-dilation.layer4: Stenotic segment — scar ring or web; distance from the vocal folds; craniocaudal length; residual lumen; cartilage support (firm vs collapsing). Measure with a telescope or a sized endotracheal tube or bougie: distance from the folds, length, grade (Myers-Cotton) and whether the wall is firm scar, soft inflamed tissue or cartilage collapse (malacia does not respond to dilation).
A-airway-dilation.layer5: Radial incisions (when used) — scar band; submucosa; underlying cricoid or tracheal cartilage. Radial cuts with CO2 laser or cold knife are usually placed at several clock positions through the scar only, not into cartilage. Many surgeons avoid deep posterior cuts because the membranous wall and esophagus lie directly behind.
A-airway-dilation.layer6: Balloon dilation — stenotic ring; normal trachea above and below; posterior membranous wall. Center the balloon across the stenosis with its distal end visible or measured so it does not migrate into a bronchus. Choose a diameter close to the normal adjacent airway for the patient's size, not larger. Watch the tracheal wall and ventilation during the inflation interval.
A-airway-dilation.layer7: Distal airway survey — mid and distal trachea; carina; main bronchi. Inspect after dilation for mucosal tears, bleeding and second stenoses. Look especially at the posterior wall for a tear through the trachealis.
A-airway-dilation.landmark1: True vocal folds — Pale white bands at the glottis, below the ventricles and false folds, meeting at the anterior commissure (The upper reference point for measuring the stenosis; the balloon must sit below them)
A-airway-dilation.landmark2: Lower border of the cricoid — Seen endoscopically as the end of the smooth, ring-shaped subglottic wall before the first corrugated tracheal ring appears (Separates a subglottic (cricoid) stenosis from a tracheal one; the cricoid cannot be expanded by dilation as much as scar can)
A-airway-dilation.landmark3: Tracheal rings — Regular horseshoe-shaped anterior and lateral ridges with a flat posterior membranous wall; count them below the stenosis (Documents stenosis length and confirms the cartilage is intact)
A-airway-dilation.landmark4: Posterior membranous wall — The flat or slightly bulging posterior surface without cartilage ridges, which moves with respiration and coughing (Thinnest part of the airway and the site of dangerous tears)
A-airway-dilation.landmark5: Carina — Sharp midline ridge at the bottom of the trachea where the right and left main bronchi diverge (Limits distal balloon placement and confirms the full airway survey)
A-airway-dilation.danger1: Posterior membranous trachea and esophagus — where: Directly behind the airway lumen; no cartilage support; protect: Avoid deep posterior radial cuts, do not oversize the balloon, and inspect the posterior wall after each dilation; injury: Tracheal tear, pneumomediastinum, tracheoesophageal fistula, mediastinitis
A-airway-dilation.danger2: Vocal folds and anterior commissure — where: Immediately above a high subglottic stenosis; protect: Place the balloon so its proximal shoulder is below the glottis; avoid laser near the anterior commissure; injury: Glottic scar, web, voice change
A-airway-dilation.danger3: Cricoid and tracheal cartilage — where: Deep to the scar ring; protect: Cut through scar only, not into cartilage; avoid excessive dilation pressures; injury: Cartilage exposure, chondritis and worse restenosis, or fracture with collapse
A-airway-dilation.danger4: Airway (oxygenation and fire) — where: Shared field between surgeon and anesthesia during apnea, jet ventilation or tubeless technique; protect: Agree on the apnea interval and target saturation; keep FiO2 as low as tolerated with laser and keep flammable material out of the beam path; injury: Hypoxia, airway fire
A-airway-dilation.danger5: Main bronchi and distal airway — where: Below the stenosis; protect: Keep the distal balloon tip in view or at a measured depth; injury: Bronchial injury or obstruction of ventilation by a migrated balloon
A-airway-dilation.variant1: Idiopathic subglottic stenosis: almost always adult women, centered at the cricoid and upper trachea, with a normal glottis
A-airway-dilation.variant2: Post-intubation stenosis may extend into the posterior glottis (interarytenoid scar), which dilation alone does not treat
A-airway-dilation.variant3: Granulomatosis with polyangiitis: subglottic stenosis with inflamed mucosa that may need medical control
A-airway-dilation.variant4: Pediatric congenital subglottic stenosis from a small or elliptical cricoid, and complete tracheal rings, which should not be dilated like scar
A-airway-dilation.variant5: Tracheomalacia or A-frame deformity at an old tracheostomy site: cartilage collapse, not scar
A-airway-dilation.supply1: Subglottic and tracheal mucosa — Inferior thyroid artery branches (cricothyroid branches of the superior thyroid artery anteriorly near the cricoid)
A-airway-dilation.supply2: Sensory — Subglottis and trachea: RLN sensory fibers (supraglottis is internal branch of SLN)
A-airway-dilation.supply3: Motor around the field — RLN to intrinsic laryngeal muscles; not usually at risk in endoscopic dilation

A-airway-dilation.pimp3: Q: What are the boundaries of the subglottis? A: From just below the true vocal folds to the lower border of the cricoid cartilage
A-airway-dilation.pimp4: Q: Which patient population typically has idiopathic subglottic stenosis? A: Adult women

A-airway-dilation.pimp6: Q: Why is the posterior tracheal wall at particular risk? A: It is membranous (trachealis) with no cartilage and the esophagus lies directly behind it
A-airway-dilation.pimp7: Q: What flow-volume loop pattern does a fixed subglottic stenosis produce? A: Flattening of both the inspiratory and expiratory limbs
A-airway-dilation.pimp8: Q: Which vasculitis classically causes subglottic stenosis? A: Granulomatosis with polyangiitis

OR-airway-dilation.step2: Protect the posterior glottis and identify any scar bands that should be radially divided before dilation.
OR-airway-dilation.step3: Place the balloon centered across the stenosis under direct vision, avoiding distal migration and vocal-fold injury.
OR-airway-dilation.step4: Inflate to the planned diameter/pressure for a controlled interval while maintaining the agreed ventilation/apnea strategy.
OR-airway-dilation.step5: Deflate completely and reassess mucosa, bleeding, and lumen before deciding on additional dilation.
OR-airway-dilation.step6: Repeat only as needed, avoiding progressive mucosal tearing or cartilage injury from oversized dilation.
OR-airway-dilation.step7: Apply adjunctive steroid/mitomycin only when part of the specific protocol and with careful dose/contact control.
OR-airway-dilation.step8: Reinspect the entire airway and determine postoperative steroid, observation, and rescue-airway plan.

## Arytenoid Adduction  (slug: arytenoid-adduction, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-arytenoid-adduction.summary: Arytenoid adduction rotates a paralyzed arytenoid so its vocal process moves medially and down to the level of the healthy fold, closing a posterior glottic gap that a medialization implant cannot reach. It works through the muscular process, found behind the posterior thyroid ala deep to the pyriform sinus; safety depends on keeping the pyriform mucosa intact and not overrotating or overtensioning the suture, which narrows the posterior airway.
A-arytenoid-adduction.layer1: Incision and straps — skin; platysma; sternohyoid; thyrohyoid; sternothyroid. A horizontal incision on the paralyzed side over the thyroid ala, often the same incision as a medialization. Retract or divide the straps to see the whole ala to its posterior border.
A-arytenoid-adduction.layer2: Posterior thyroid border — posterior border of the thyroid ala; inferior constrictor (thyropharyngeus) attached along it; superior and inferior cornua. Divide the inferior constrictor along the posterior border and lift the border with a hook, rotating the larynx toward the opposite side. Some surgeons separate the cricothyroid joint to improve exposure; this places the RLN, which lies just behind the joint, at greater risk.
A-arytenoid-adduction.layer3: Pyriform sinus — pyriform sinus mucosa; posterior thyroid lamina (inner surface); posterior cricoid lamina. Elevate the pyriform sinus mucosa off the inner surface of the posterior ala and the cricoid lamina, pushing it superiorly and posteriorly. It is thin; a tear risks a pharyngeal leak.
A-arytenoid-adduction.layer4: Posterior cricoarytenoid and muscular process — PCA muscle fibers on the posterior cricoid lamina; muscular process of the arytenoid; lateral cricoarytenoid (anterior to the process). Follow the PCA fibers upward and laterally from the posterior cricoid lamina; they converge onto the muscular process, which is felt as a firm knob on the upper lateral cricoid rim, roughly where a line extended back from the vocal-fold level meets the posterior border of the ala.
A-arytenoid-adduction.layer5: Cricoarytenoid joint — joint capsule; superolateral facet of the cricoid lamina. Some surgeons open the joint deliberately to free the arytenoid; others leave it intact. Either way, know that the arytenoid is now mobile and can be overrotated.
A-arytenoid-adduction.layer6: Suture placement and tensioning — muscular process (suture purchase); thyroid ala anteriorly (fixation point); vocal process position on endoscopy. Pass a permanent suture through the muscular process and bring it forward through the paraglottic space to the anterior thyroid ala. Depending on technique it is passed through drill holes in the anterior-inferior ala or tied at the anterior edge of a thyroplasty window, so the pull is anterior and somewhat inferior, mimicking the LCA. Tension it gradually while watching the vocal process move medially and inferiorly.
A-arytenoid-adduction.layer7: Closure — constrictor reattachment; straps; drain. Reapproximate the constrictor and straps. Airway edema can increase overnight, so plan observation.
A-arytenoid-adduction.landmark1: Posterior border of the thyroid ala — The smooth vertical edge of the ala between the superior and inferior cornua, palpated behind the oblique line (The working corridor lies just deep to it)
A-arytenoid-adduction.landmark2: Muscular process — Follow PCA fibers superolaterally from the posterior cricoid plate to their insertion; it is a firm knob at roughly the vocal-fold level projected back to the posterior border of the ala, deep to that border and above the cricothyroid joint (The suture must hold here)
A-arytenoid-adduction.landmark3: Cricothyroid joint — Inferior cornu of the thyroid cartilage meeting the lateral cricoid (RLN entry point is just behind it; stay above it when possible)
A-arytenoid-adduction.landmark4: Pyriform sinus mucosa — Thin, smooth, gray-pink membrane lining the inner posterior ala; it lifts away with gentle blunt dissection (Must be elevated, not entered)
A-arytenoid-adduction.danger1: Pyriform sinus mucosa — where: Deep to the posterior thyroid ala, directly over the dissection; protect: Elevate bluntly and inspect for tears; repair any tear; injury: Pharyngocutaneous fistula, deep neck infection
A-arytenoid-adduction.danger2: Airway — where: Posterior glottis narrows as the arytenoid is adducted; protect: Avoid overtensioning; monitor endoscopically; plan overnight observation; injury: Stridor, edema, need for tracheostomy
A-arytenoid-adduction.danger3: RLN — where: Just behind the cricothyroid joint and in the tracheoesophageal groove; protect: Avoid dissecting behind the joint if possible; injury: Loss of any remaining nerve function (relevant for reinnervation plans)
A-arytenoid-adduction.danger4: Superior laryngeal neurovascular bundle — where: Enters the thyrohyoid membrane near the superior cornu; protect: Do not extend dissection to the superior cornu; injury: Bleeding, supraglottic sensory loss
A-arytenoid-adduction.danger5: Arytenoid position — where: Vocal process; protect: Tension gradually; aim to match the healthy side, not overcorrect; injury: Overrotation with the vocal process too low or too medial; poor voice, airway narrowing
A-arytenoid-adduction.variant1: Ossified thyroid cartilage making the window and suture passage harder
A-arytenoid-adduction.variant2: Fixed cricoarytenoid joint (from trauma or intubation) rather than paralysis: suture will not rotate the arytenoid well
A-arytenoid-adduction.variant3: Previous medialization implant that crowds the posterior ala
A-arytenoid-adduction.variant4: Vertical height mismatch between the vocal processes, which medialization alone does not correct
A-arytenoid-adduction.supply1: Arterial supply — Inferior laryngeal artery (from the inferior thyroid artery) near the posterior cricoid; superior laryngeal artery above
A-arytenoid-adduction.supply2: Motor — RLN to PCA, LCA, IA and TA
A-arytenoid-adduction.supply3: Sensory — Internal branch of SLN above the folds
A-arytenoid-adduction.pimp1: Q: Which muscles attach to the muscular process? A: Posterior cricoarytenoid (posteriorly) and lateral cricoarytenoid (anteriorly)
A-arytenoid-adduction.pimp2: Q: What attaches to the vocal process? A: The vocal ligament
A-arytenoid-adduction.pimp3: Q: Which muscle does the arytenoid adduction suture imitate? A: Lateral cricoarytenoid
A-arytenoid-adduction.pimp4: Q: What is the main motion of the cricoarytenoid joint? A: Rocking (rotation) with some gliding
A-arytenoid-adduction.pimp5: Q: Why do arytenoid adduction instead of thyroplasty alone? A: Large posterior gap or height mismatch that the implant cannot correct
A-arytenoid-adduction.pimp6: Q: Where does the RLN enter the larynx? A: Just behind the cricothyroid joint
A-arytenoid-adduction.pimp7: Q: Which muscle is divided along the posterior thyroid border? A: Inferior constrictor (thyropharyngeus part)
OR-arytenoid-adduction.step1: Expose the ipsilateral thyroid ala through a horizontal neck incision and identify the posterior thyroid border while preserving superior laryngeal and strap-muscle anatomy.
OR-arytenoid-adduction.step2: Open the posterior laryngeal framework corridor and identify the muscular process of the arytenoid without entering the pyriform mucosa.
OR-arytenoid-adduction.step3: Place the adduction suture through the muscular process in a secure orientation that reproduces lateral cricoarytenoid/interarytenoid vector rather than simply pulling the cartilage anteriorly.
OR-arytenoid-adduction.step4: Bring the suture anteriorly through the thyroid cartilage at the planned fixation point while keeping it free of soft-tissue entrapment.
OR-arytenoid-adduction.step5: Tension the suture gradually while assessing vocal-fold position, posterior glottic closure, and voice with flexible laryngoscopy/phonation when an awake technique is used.
OR-arytenoid-adduction.step6: Combine with medialization thyroplasty when planned, adjusting implant and adduction tension together so posterior closure improves without airway compromise.
OR-arytenoid-adduction.step7: Secure the adduction suture only after the arytenoid position is stable and the voice/airway endpoint is acceptable.
OR-arytenoid-adduction.step8: Inspect for mucosal violation and bleeding, then close the neck with postoperative airway, voice, and swallowing surveillance.

## Posterior Cordotomy / Arytenoidectomy  (slug: cordotomy, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-cordotomy.summary: Posterior cordotomy and arytenoidectomy enlarge a glottis narrowed by bilateral vocal-fold immobility by removing tissue from the posterior glottis, the intercartilaginous part often called the respiratory glottis because it contributes much of the airway in quiet breathing. The key relationship is the vocal process: a cordotomy is cut just in front of it, an arytenoidectomy removes some of the arytenoid itself. Gaining airway trades against voice and aspiration.
A-cordotomy.layer1: Exposure — suspension laryngoscope; vocal folds; posterior commissure; interarytenoid area; arytenoids. Expose the entire posterior glottis. Palpate both arytenoids to tell paralysis (mobile joint) from posterior glottic stenosis or joint fixation.
A-cordotomy.layer2: Vocal process and membranous fold — vocal process (posterior end of membranous fold); membranous fold; ventricle; false fold. The vocal process divides the anterior membranous glottis from the posterior cartilaginous glottis. Identify it before cutting.
A-cordotomy.layer3: Cordotomy cut — vocal fold epithelium and lamina propria; vocal ligament; thyroarytenoid muscle; lateral paraglottic tissue toward the thyroid perichondrium. A transverse cut about 1-2 mm anterior to the vocal process, carried laterally through the vocal ligament and TA muscle toward the thyroid lamina, lets the fold retract forward and opens a C-shaped wedge posteriorly. The Kashima description removes a wedge of roughly 4 mm at the free edge and extends a few millimeters onto the ventricular band; series vary in how far they go. Do not expose the vocal process cartilage.
A-cordotomy.layer4: Arytenoidectomy (if planned) — arytenoid mucosa; arytenoid body (medial or total); vocal process; muscular process. Medial arytenoidectomy removes the medial part of the body; total removes most of the cartilage. Preserve posterior and interarytenoid mucosa to reduce scar.
A-cordotomy.layer5: Hemostasis — vessels in the TA and lateral paraglottic tissue. Bleeding comes from small laryngeal vessels within the muscle; use pinpoint coagulation.
A-cordotomy.layer6: Final inspection — airway lumen; posterior commissure; subglottis. Look for loose tissue and exposed cartilage, and confirm the opposite fold is untouched.
A-cordotomy.landmark1: Vocal process — The white pointed end of the arytenoid at the posterior edge of the membranous fold; it moves when the arytenoid is palpated (The cordotomy is cut just in front of it)
A-cordotomy.landmark2: Posterior commissure — Mucosa between the arytenoids at the back of the glottis, above the cricoid plate (Raw surfaces here scar and close the airway)
A-cordotomy.landmark3: Arytenoid body — Palpated as a firm mobile structure behind the vocal process; the corniculate cartilage sits on its apex (Target in arytenoidectomy; palpation tests joint mobility)
A-cordotomy.landmark4: Ventricle — The recess between true and false folds; lateral to it is the paraglottic space (Orients the lateral extent of the cut: the wedge is carried laterally through the TA, under the ventricle floor, and may extend a few millimeters onto the ventricular band)
A-cordotomy.danger1: Posterior commissure and interarytenoid mucosa — where: Behind the operated arytenoid; protect: Preserve mucosa and avoid raw surfaces facing each other; injury: Posterior glottic scar or web
A-cordotomy.danger2: Vocal process cartilage — where: Behind the cordotomy cut; protect: Cut anterior to it and keep it covered; injury: Granuloma, chondritis
A-cordotomy.danger3: Opposite fold — where: Across the glottis; protect: Protect with a moist pledget; injury: Bilateral injury, web formation
A-cordotomy.danger4: Airway fire — where: Endotracheal tube or oxygen near the laser beam; protect: Laser-safe tube, saline-filled cuff, low FiO2, wet cottonoids; injury: Airway burn
A-cordotomy.danger5: Supraglottic protection — where: Arytenoid and aryepiglottic fold; protect: Limit the size of resection, especially total arytenoidectomy; injury: Aspiration
A-cordotomy.variant1: Bilateral vocal-fold paralysis after thyroidectomy (most common cause) vs posterior glottic stenosis
A-cordotomy.variant2: Cricoarytenoid joint fixation from intubation or rheumatoid arthritis
A-cordotomy.variant3: Partial recovery possible after nerve injury; delay irreversible surgery if within the expected recovery window
A-cordotomy.supply1: Arterial supply — Superior and inferior laryngeal arteries
A-cordotomy.supply2: Motor — RLN (PCA is the only abductor)
A-cordotomy.supply3: Sensory — Internal branch of SLN supraglottic; RLN subglottic
A-cordotomy.pimp1: Q: What is the Bogdasarian classification? A: Posterior glottic stenosis: I interarytenoid adhesion; II posterior commissure scar; III with unilateral cricoarytenoid fixation; IV bilateral fixation
A-cordotomy.pimp2: Q: Who described transverse posterior cordotomy? A: Dennis and Kashima (1989)
A-cordotomy.pimp3: Q: What is the most common cause of bilateral vocal-fold paralysis? A: Thyroid surgery
A-cordotomy.pimp4: Q: What is the only vocal-fold abductor? A: Posterior cricoarytenoid
A-cordotomy.pimp5: Q: Why remove posterior tissue to enlarge the airway? A: The posterior (cartilaginous) glottis, the respiratory glottis, contributes a large share of the airway in breathing, while the membranous fold is more important for voice
A-cordotomy.pimp6: Q: How do you tell paralysis from joint fixation in the OR? A: Palpate the arytenoid; a fixed joint does not move
A-cordotomy.pimp7: Q: What cartilage sits on the arytenoid apex? A: Corniculate cartilage
OR-cordotomy.step1: Obtain full posterior-glottic exposure and confirm which side is planned for airway enlargement based on preoperative motion, voice, and aspiration considerations.
OR-cordotomy.step2: Identify the vocal process, membranous vocal fold, ventricle, and posterior commissure before laser or powered instrumentation.
OR-cordotomy.step3: For posterior cordotomy, create the transverse opening just anterior to the vocal process through the vocal fold toward the thyroid cartilage/perichondrium to establish a lateral airway channel.
OR-cordotomy.step4: For arytenoid reduction when planned, expose and remove only the intended arytenoid component while protecting posterior mucosa and avoiding bilateral scar surfaces.
OR-cordotomy.step5: Control bleeding and char with minimal collateral thermal injury, keeping the opposite fold untouched.
OR-cordotomy.step6: Assess the resulting posterior airway under direct visualization and remove only additional tissue necessary for an adequate lumen.
OR-cordotomy.step7: Reinspect the subglottis and posterior commissure for loose tissue or flap that could obstruct after extubation.
OR-cordotomy.step8: Plan postoperative airway observation and counsel that voice quality may trade off against airway gain.

## Cricopharyngeal Myotomy  (slug: cp-myotomy, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-cp-myotomy.summary: Cricopharyngeal myotomy cuts the cricopharyngeus, the main tonic muscle of the upper esophageal sphincter, when it does not relax or is fibrotic. The open approach is usually from the left, behind the larynx and in front of the prevertebral fascia; the myotomy is posterior or posterolateral so it stays away from the RLN in the tracheoesophageal groove, and it stops at intact mucosa.
A-cp-myotomy.layer1: Incision — skin; platysma; external jugular vein (lateral). A horizontal incision at about the cricoid level or along the anterior border of the left SCM.
A-cp-myotomy.layer2: Investing fascia and SCM — investing fascia; anterior border of the SCM; omohyoid (crosses the field). Retract the SCM laterally and divide or retract the omohyoid.
A-cp-myotomy.layer3: Carotid sheath and thyroid — carotid sheath (lateral); middle thyroid vein; thyroid lobe and larynx (medial); inferior thyroid artery (crossing transversely). Ligate the middle thyroid vein. Retract the carotid sheath laterally and the thyroid and larynx medially. The inferior thyroid artery and RLN may be seen deep to the lobe.
A-cp-myotomy.layer4: Retrovisceral space — prevertebral fascia (posterior); buccopharyngeal fascia over the pharynx; cervical spine. Bluntly open the space behind the pharynx and esophagus along the prevertebral fascia. Rotate the larynx toward the opposite side so the posterolateral pharyngoesophageal junction faces you.
A-cp-myotomy.layer5: Pharyngoesophageal segment — inferior constrictor (thyropharyngeus, oblique fibers); cricopharyngeus (horizontal fibers); upper esophageal circular and longitudinal muscle. A bougie or scope in the lumen tents the wall. The cricopharyngeus is the band of horizontal fibers at about the cricoid level between the oblique thyropharyngeus above and the esophageal muscle below.
A-cp-myotomy.layer6: Myotomy — muscle fibers; submucosa; intact mucosa. Divide all muscle layers vertically down to bulging mucosa, from the lowest inferior constrictor fibers through the full cricopharyngeus onto the upper cervical esophagus; the total length is commonly about 4-5 cm, though reported lengths vary. Cut posteriorly or posterolaterally, away from the RLN.
A-cp-myotomy.layer7: Mucosal check and closure — mucosa; drain. Insufflate air or fill with saline to test the mucosa; repair any tear. Close with a drain when appropriate.
A-cp-myotomy.landmark1: Cricoid cartilage — Palpable posterolaterally once the larynx is rotated; the cricopharyngeus attaches to the sides of the cricoid lamina (The muscle sits at this level)
A-cp-myotomy.landmark2: Cricopharyngeus — Horizontal muscle band, often thicker and firmer than the surrounding muscle, tented by the intraluminal bougie (Target of myotomy)
A-cp-myotomy.landmark3: Prevertebral fascia — Smooth fascia on the front of the vertebral bodies and longus colli muscles, behind the retrovisceral space (Defines the safe blunt plane behind the esophagus)
A-cp-myotomy.landmark4: Tracheoesophageal groove — Angle between the trachea and esophagus medially, below the inferior thyroid artery (The RLN runs here; keep the myotomy posterior to it)
A-cp-myotomy.landmark5: Inferior thyroid artery — Runs transversely from behind the carotid sheath toward the lower thyroid lobe (A landmark for the RLN, which crosses near it)
A-cp-myotomy.danger1: Esophageal mucosa — where: Directly under the divided muscle; protect: Cut in thin layers with a bougie in place; test for leaks; injury: Perforation, fistula, mediastinitis
A-cp-myotomy.danger2: RLN — where: In the tracheoesophageal groove, anteromedial to the myotomy; protect: Keep the myotomy posterior or posterolateral; avoid deep retraction against the groove; injury: Vocal-fold paralysis, worse dysphagia and aspiration
A-cp-myotomy.danger3: Carotid sheath — where: Lateral to the dissection; protect: Use gentle lateral retraction; injury: Vascular injury, vagal injury
A-cp-myotomy.danger4: Thoracic duct — where: Low left neck, lateral to the esophagus near the thoracic inlet; protect: Stay above the low neck when the incision is high; inspect for chyle; injury: Chyle leak
A-cp-myotomy.danger5: Retrovisceral space contamination — where: Behind the esophagus, continuous with the posterior mediastinum; protect: Repair any mucosal tear and drain; injury: Mediastinitis
A-cp-myotomy.variant1: Nonrecurrent right inferior laryngeal nerve with aberrant right subclavian artery (relevant in a right-sided approach)
A-cp-myotomy.variant2: Cervical osteophytes behind the pharyngoesophageal segment
A-cp-myotomy.variant3: Coexisting Zenker diverticulum, which can be excised, suspended or inverted with the myotomy
A-cp-myotomy.supply1: Arterial supply — Inferior thyroid artery branches
A-cp-myotomy.supply2: Motor — Pharyngeal plexus (vagus) and RLN fibers
A-cp-myotomy.supply3: Sensory — Vagal branches
A-cp-myotomy.pimp1: Q: What are the attachments of the cricopharyngeus? A: Both sides of the cricoid lamina, forming a sling around the back of the pharyngoesophageal junction
A-cp-myotomy.pimp2: Q: Why approach from the left? A: The cervical esophagus lies slightly to the left of midline, and the left RLN is consistently recurrent
A-cp-myotomy.pimp3: Q: What are the components of the UES? A: Cricopharyngeus, lower inferior constrictor and upper cervical esophageal muscle
A-cp-myotomy.pimp4: Q: What vertebral level is the cricopharyngeus at? A: About C5-C6, at the cricoid
A-cp-myotomy.pimp5: Q: Which muscle forms the upper border of Killian's triangle? A: Thyropharyngeus (oblique part of the inferior constrictor)
A-cp-myotomy.pimp6: Q: What relationship does the RLN have to the inferior thyroid artery? A: It crosses the artery, passing in front of, behind or between its branches (variable)

## Cricotracheal Resection  (slug: ctr, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-ctr.summary: Cricotracheal resection removes a subglottic stenosis by excising the anterior cricoid arch with the diseased upper trachea, while keeping the posterior cricoid plate, and joining the healthy trachea to the thyroid cartilage and cricoid plate. The key relationship is that each RLN enters the larynx just behind the cricothyroid joint, so the lateral cuts must pass anterior to those joints; the second is that the trachea's blood supply arrives laterally, so circumferential mobilization must be limited.
A-ctr.layer1: Incision and flaps — skin; platysma; anterior jugular veins; existing tracheostoma (if present). A low collar incision, often including an existing stoma, with subplatysmal flaps up to the hyoid and down to the sternal notch.
A-ctr.layer2: Strap muscles and thyroid isthmus — sternohyoid; sternothyroid; thyroid isthmus; pretracheal fascia. Separate the straps in the midline and divide the isthmus so each lobe can be retracted laterally off the trachea.
A-ctr.layer3: Anterior larynx and trachea — thyroid cartilage; cricothyroid membrane; cricothyroid muscles; anterior cricoid arch; upper tracheal rings. Expose the anterior surface of the airway from the thyroid notch to below the stenosis. Dissect directly on the anterior trachea, where the plane is avascular and can be opened bluntly into the upper mediastinum.
A-ctr.layer4: Lateral cricoid — cricothyroid muscle (detached); cricothyroid joint; inferior cornu of the thyroid cartilage; RLN entering behind the joint. Detach the cricothyroid muscles and stay on the cartilage (subperichondrial on the lateral cricoid). Do not dissect behind the cricothyroid joint, where the RLN enters the larynx.
A-ctr.layer5: Tracheal transection and distal airway — tracheal rings below the stenosis; posterior membranous wall; esophagus behind; cross-field ventilation tube. Divide the trachea at the lower edge of the stenosis and intubate the distal trachea across the field. Separate the membranous trachea from the esophagus only as far as needed.
A-ctr.layer6: Resection of the anterior cricoid and stenosis — anterior and lateral cricoid arch; posterior cricoid plate (preserved); subglottic scar over the plate. The cut runs obliquely from just below the thyroid cartilage anteriorly to the lower edge of the cricoid plate posteriorly, anterior to the cricothyroid joints. Remove scar from the inner surface of the plate and thin the plate if needed, keeping its posterior perichondrium.
A-ctr.layer7: Anastomosis and closure — distal tracheal ring (shaped with a posterior membranous flap); thyroid cartilage; cricoid plate; release maneuvers if tense. The membranous flap of the distal trachea resurfaces the bare cricoid plate. Place posterior sutures first, then lateral and anterior. Use a suprahyoid release if tension remains, then hold the neck flexed.
A-ctr.landmark1: Cricothyroid joint — Follow the inferior border of the thyroid ala posteriorly to the inferior cornu, which articulates with a small facet on the lateral cricoid (The RLN enters the larynx just posterior to this joint; resection lines stay anterior to it)
A-ctr.landmark2: Lower edge of the stenosis — Use endoscopy with a needle passed through the anterior trachea under vision to mark the first normal ring (Defines the distal transection without leaving scar or removing healthy trachea)
A-ctr.landmark3: Posterior cricoid plate — The tall, signet-shaped posterior part of the cricoid, felt behind the airway once the anterior arch is removed (It is preserved; it carries the cricoarytenoid joints and the posterior cricoarytenoid muscles)
A-ctr.landmark4: Pretracheal plane — Finger dissection on the anterior trachea from the neck down into the mediastinum, in front of the rings and behind the innominate artery (Mobilizes the trachea without disturbing the lateral blood supply)
A-ctr.landmark5: Tracheoesophageal groove — Angle between the lateral tracheal wall and the esophagus, seen by rotating the trachea (Contains the RLN; avoid dissection here beyond the area of resection)
A-ctr.danger1: Recurrent laryngeal nerves — where: In the tracheoesophageal grooves, entering the larynx behind the cricothyroid joints; protect: Stay subperichondrial on the lateral cricoid, keep resection lines anterior to the joints, and limit lateral tracheal dissection; injury: Unilateral or bilateral vocal-fold paralysis; bilateral injury causes airway obstruction
A-ctr.danger2: Esophagus — where: Directly behind the posterior membranous trachea and cricoid plate; protect: Separate the membranous wall carefully and keep a bougie or nasogastric tube in place if helpful; injury: Esophageal perforation, tracheoesophageal fistula
A-ctr.danger3: Lateral tracheal blood supply — where: Segmental vessels enter the trachea laterally; protect: Limit circumferential mobilization to about 1-2 cm beyond the cut edges; mobilize anteriorly and posteriorly instead; injury: Ischemic anastomosis, dehiscence, restenosis
A-ctr.danger4: Anastomosis — where: Thyrotracheal and cricotracheal suture line; protect: Use release maneuvers instead of tight sutures; keep the neck flexed (chin stitch or brace); injury: Dehiscence, mediastinitis, restenosis
A-ctr.danger5: Innominate artery — where: Anterior to the lower cervical trachea; protect: Stay on the trachea when dissecting into the mediastinum; injury: Hemorrhage, late fistula
A-ctr.variant1: Stenosis that extends up into the glottis or near the vocal folds may require an extended resection with posterior laryngofissure and grafting
A-ctr.variant2: Pediatric partial CTR: the posterior cricoid plate is thin and the margin under the folds is small
A-ctr.variant3: Nonrecurrent right inferior laryngeal nerve (aberrant right subclavian artery): enters the larynx from a more horizontal course
A-ctr.variant4: Prior tracheostomy or prior resection with scarred planes
A-ctr.supply1: Tracheal supply — Segmental lateral vessels from the inferior thyroid artery (upper trachea) and bronchial arteries (lower trachea)
A-ctr.supply2: Laryngeal supply — Superior and inferior laryngeal arteries; cricothyroid branch of the superior thyroid artery
A-ctr.supply3: Motor — RLN to intrinsic laryngeal muscles; external branch of the SLN to cricothyroid
A-ctr.supply4: Sensory — RLN to the subglottis; internal branch of the SLN to the supraglottis
A-ctr.pimp1: Q: Where does the RLN enter the larynx? A: Just posterior to the cricothyroid joint, deep to the inferior constrictor
A-ctr.pimp2: Q: Why is the posterior cricoid plate preserved? A: It supports the cricoarytenoid joints and posterior cricoarytenoid muscles and protects the RLN entry
A-ctr.pimp3: Q: How does the trachea receive its blood supply? A: Segmental vessels entering laterally (inferior thyroid and bronchial arteries)
A-ctr.pimp4: Q: Name two laryngeal release maneuvers. A: Suprahyoid (Montgomery) and infrahyoid (thyrohyoid, Dedo) release
A-ctr.pimp5: Q: Which laryngeal release is associated with more postoperative dysphagia? A: Infrahyoid (thyrohyoid) release
A-ctr.pimp6: Q: How much adult trachea can be resected? A: Roughly up to half with full mobilization and release maneuvers; much less without
A-ctr.pimp7: Q: What is the Grillo stitch? A: A chin-to-chest suture holding the neck flexed to protect the anastomosis
A-ctr.pimp8: Q: What does the distal tracheal membranous flap do in a CTR? A: Resurfaces the denuded posterior cricoid plate
OR-ctr.step1: Expose the cricoid and proximal trachea widely enough to define the entire stenotic segment and permit a tension-free anastomosis while protecting both recurrent laryngeal nerves.
OR-ctr.step2: Mobilize the cervical trachea circumferentially only to the degree necessary, preserving lateral blood supply and avoiding unnecessary nerve dissection.
OR-ctr.step3: Transect the trachea below the stenosis and establish cross-field ventilation as needed.
OR-ctr.step4: Resect the stenotic trachea and diseased anterior/lateral cricoid while preserving the posterior cricoid plate and recurrent-nerve entry regions.
OR-ctr.step5: Prepare healthy proximal and distal mucosal/cartilaginous edges and perform posterior then lateral/anterior anastomotic suturing with accurate mucosal apposition.
OR-ctr.step6: Release additional tracheal/laryngeal attachments only if needed to remove tension rather than accepting a tight anastomosis.
OR-ctr.step7: Tie/complete the anastomosis with the neck flexed, then test for air leak and inspect the lumen endoscopically.
OR-ctr.step8: Maintain neck-flexion precautions and establish the postoperative airway plan with explicit concern for dehiscence and bilateral RLN function.

## Injection Laryngoplasty  (slug: injection-laryngoplasty, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-injection-laryngoplasty.summary: Injection laryngoplasty bulks a weak or paralyzed vocal fold by placing material in the paraglottic space, lateral to the thyroarytenoid muscle, so the fold moves medially and the glottic gap closes. The material must go deep (lateral to TA) and not into the superficial lamina propria, which would stiffen the vibrating cover.
A-injection-laryngoplasty.layer1: Approach choice — peroral (transoral, under GA or awake); transcricothyroid membrane; transthyroid cartilage; transthyrohyoid membrane. Choose the route by patient tolerance, cartilage ossification and target. Every route aims for the same space.
A-injection-laryngoplasty.layer2: Skin and external landmarks (percutaneous) — thyroid notch; inferior border of thyroid ala; cricothyroid membrane; cricoid arch. Palpate the thyroid notch, inferior thyroid border and cricoid. The cricothyroid artery crosses the upper membrane, so a low entry is used for the transcricothyroid route.
A-injection-laryngoplasty.layer3: Transcricothyroid route — cricothyroid membrane just off midline; subglottic submucosa; conus elasticus; paraglottic space under the fold. Enter the membrane a few millimeters lateral to midline and angle superiorly and laterally toward the fold. Watch endoscopically for the needle tip bulging under the fold; seeing it in the subglottic lumen means it is too medial.
A-injection-laryngoplasty.layer4: Transthyroid route — thyroid lamina at the fold level; inner perichondrium; paraglottic space. Pass through the lower half of the ala at the estimated fold level; this is easier in younger patients with unossified cartilage.
A-injection-laryngoplasty.layer5: Transthyrohyoid route — thyrohyoid membrane above the notch; pre-epiglottic space and petiole region; laryngeal vestibule; superior surface of the fold. Enter in or near the midline above the notch and angle down; the needle enters the endolarynx and then the fold under direct view. Staying near midline avoids the superior laryngeal neurovascular bundle, which pierces the membrane laterally.
A-injection-laryngoplasty.layer6: Target: paraglottic space — lateral to the thyroarytenoid muscle; lateral to the vocal process (posterior target); mid-membranous lateral fold (anterior target). Most surgeons inject first lateral to the vocal process, then mid-membranous if needed. Medialization is seen as the fold edge moving in and the gap closing.
A-injection-laryngoplasty.layer7: Assessment — glottic closure; fold contour; airway. Overinject slightly only with materials that resorb partially; check airway and swallowing before discharge.
A-injection-laryngoplasty.landmark1: Vocal process — The white, pointed tip of the arytenoid at the posterior end of the membranous fold, seen with endoscopy (The posterior injection site is lateral to it)
A-injection-laryngoplasty.landmark2: Cricothyroid membrane — Soft gap between the inferior thyroid border and the cricoid arch, palpated in the midline (Entry point for the transcricothyroid approach)
A-injection-laryngoplasty.landmark3: Thyroid notch — V-shaped notch at the top of the thyroid prominence (The transthyrohyoid entry is just above it; the fold level is classically about halfway down from it (individual variation; confirm endoscopically))
A-injection-laryngoplasty.landmark4: Superficial lamina propria (Reinke's space) — A whitish wheal or blanching of the epithelium during injection signals superficial placement (Stop and redirect deeper; superficial injection stiffens the cover)
A-injection-laryngoplasty.danger1: Superficial lamina propria — where: Just under the vibratory epithelium along the free edge; protect: Inject lateral to the TA muscle, keep the needle deep and watch for blanching; injury: Stiff fold with dysphonia
A-injection-laryngoplasty.danger2: Airway — where: Glottis and subglottis; protect: Avoid overmedialization, avoid bilateral injection in a narrow glottis, monitor for edema; injury: Airway obstruction
A-injection-laryngoplasty.danger3: Cricothyroid artery and other laryngeal vessels — where: Across the upper cricothyroid membrane; superior laryngeal artery through the thyrohyoid membrane; protect: Use a low entry in the membrane; stay near the midline when crossing the thyrohyoid membrane; aspirate before injecting; injury: Hematoma; intravascular injection
A-injection-laryngoplasty.danger4: Superior laryngeal neurovascular bundle — where: Pierces the thyrohyoid membrane laterally, below the greater horn of the hyoid; protect: Use a midline transthyrohyoid entry; injury: Bleeding, supraglottic sensory loss
A-injection-laryngoplasty.variant1: Ossified thyroid cartilage in older men: transthyroid route difficult
A-injection-laryngoplasty.variant2: Vocal-fold height mismatch or large posterior gap from arytenoid malposition: injection may not fully correct
A-injection-laryngoplasty.variant3: Narrow glottis or bilateral impairment: risk of airway compromise
A-injection-laryngoplasty.variant4: Scar or sulcus where the problem is vibratory stiffness rather than gap
A-injection-laryngoplasty.supply1: Arterial supply — Superior laryngeal artery (via thyrohyoid membrane), inferior laryngeal artery (with RLN), cricothyroid artery
A-injection-laryngoplasty.supply2: Motor — RLN to TA, LCA, IA and PCA; external branch of SLN to cricothyroid
A-injection-laryngoplasty.supply3: Sensory — Internal branch of SLN above the folds; RLN below the folds
A-injection-laryngoplasty.pimp1: Q: Name the layers of the vocal fold. A: Epithelium, superficial lamina propria (Reinke's space), intermediate and deep lamina propria (vocal ligament), thyroarytenoid muscle
A-injection-laryngoplasty.pimp2: Q: Where should injectate be placed? A: In the paraglottic space lateral to the thyroarytenoid muscle
A-injection-laryngoplasty.pimp3: Q: What does the body-cover theory say? A: Cover (epithelium and SLP) vibrates over a stiffer body (vocal ligament and TA)
A-injection-laryngoplasty.pimp4: Q: Which material is classically overinjected because it resorbs? A: Autologous fat
A-injection-laryngoplasty.pimp5: Q: Which artery crosses the cricothyroid membrane? A: The cricothyroid branch of the superior thyroid artery
A-injection-laryngoplasty.pimp6: Q: Through which membrane does the internal branch of the SLN enter the larynx? A: Thyrohyoid membrane, with the superior laryngeal artery
A-injection-laryngoplasty.pimp7: Q: Which intrinsic laryngeal muscle receives bilateral innervation? A: Interarytenoid (transverse and oblique arytenoid)
OR-injection-laryngoplasty.step1: Confirm the target fold and glottic insufficiency pattern endoscopically before injection.
OR-injection-laryngoplasty.step2: Choose the approach and needle trajectory that places material lateral to the thyroarytenoid rather than superficially in the vibratory epithelium.
OR-injection-laryngoplasty.step3: Enter the posterolateral or mid-membranous injection site under continuous endoscopic visualization.
OR-injection-laryngoplasty.step4: Inject in small increments while watching medialization and avoiding intravascular, subepithelial, or trans-airway extrusion.
OR-injection-laryngoplasty.step5: Distribute material as needed to correct the specific gap rather than simply maximizing fold bulk.
OR-injection-laryngoplasty.step6: Stop at the planned degree of correction, accounting for expected early overcorrection with some injectates.
OR-injection-laryngoplasty.step7: Reassess phonation, airway, and swallowing as appropriate to anesthetic setting.
OR-injection-laryngoplasty.step8: Observe for edema, bleeding, or airway compromise before discharge.

## Laryngeal Botulinum Toxin Injection  (slug: laryngeal-botox, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-laryngeal-botox.summary: Botulinum toxin weakens the overactive laryngeal muscles of spasmodic dysphonia: the thyroarytenoid (and sometimes lateral cricoarytenoid) in adductor type and the posterior cricoarytenoid in abductor type. Accuracy depends on reaching the target through the cricothyroid membrane or behind the thyroid ala and confirming it with EMG activation; safety depends on avoiding bilateral abductor weakness and diffusion into swallowing muscles.
A-laryngeal-botox.layer1: Positioning and landmarks — thyroid notch; inferior border of thyroid cartilage; cricothyroid membrane; cricoid. Neck slightly extended. Palpate and mark the midline, the cricothyroid space and the cricoid. Local anesthetic is optional; injecting into the airway causes cough.
A-laryngeal-botox.layer2: Cricothyroid membrane (TA approach) — skin; subcutaneous tissue; cricothyroid membrane just off midline; cricothyroid muscle (lateral, on the outside of the membrane). Pass the hollow EMG needle through the membrane a few millimeters lateral to midline. Angle superiorly and laterally toward the fold, staying submucosal.
A-laryngeal-botox.layer3: Thyroarytenoid target — TA muscle under the vocal fold; subglottic mucosa. Ask the patient to say 'eee'. A brisk EMG burst means the tip is in TA. Loss of signal and cough suggest the needle is in the airway.
A-laryngeal-botox.layer4: Lateral cricoarytenoid target (if used) — LCA muscle (posterolateral to TA). Angle more laterally and posteriorly; LCA also activates with phonation.
A-laryngeal-botox.layer5: Posterior cricoarytenoid target (abductor type) — posterior border of the thyroid ala; posterior cricoid lamina; PCA muscle. Rotate the larynx by hooking the posterior thyroid border, pass the needle behind the ala to the cricoid lamina, then withdraw slightly. Confirm with a sniff (PCA activates). An alternative (transtracheal) route passes through the cricothyroid membrane, across the subglottic airway and through the posterior cricoid lamina into the PCA.
A-laryngeal-botox.landmark1: Cricothyroid membrane — Soft gap between the inferior thyroid border and the cricoid arch (Entry point for TA and LCA injections)
A-laryngeal-botox.landmark2: Posterior border of the thyroid ala — Palpable edge behind the oblique line, felt when the larynx is rotated (PCA approach passes behind it)
A-laryngeal-botox.landmark3: Cricoid lamina — Hard surface felt by the needle tip behind the larynx (PCA lies on it; withdraw slightly after touching it)
A-laryngeal-botox.landmark4: EMG activation — Phonation ('eee') for TA/LCA; sniff for PCA; upward pitch glide for cricothyroid (Confirms the target muscle before injecting)
A-laryngeal-botox.danger1: Bilateral PCA — where: Behind the larynx on both sides; protect: Inject one side at a time, stagger sides, and check abduction before treating the other; injury: Bilateral abductor weakness, stridor
A-laryngeal-botox.danger2: Pharyngeal constrictors — where: Next to the PCA and posterior larynx; protect: Use a small volume and accurate EMG placement; injury: Dysphagia and aspiration
A-laryngeal-botox.danger3: Cricothyroid muscle — where: On the outer surface of the cricothyroid membrane, anterolaterally; protect: Check that the EMG signal is not a pitch-glide signal; injury: Lower pitch, poor pitch range
A-laryngeal-botox.danger4: Cricothyroid artery — where: Across the upper cricothyroid membrane; protect: Use a low entry and aspirate; injury: Bleeding, hematoma
A-laryngeal-botox.danger5: Airway — where: Subglottic lumen; protect: Stay submucosal; watch for cough; injury: Cough, laryngospasm, airway injection
A-laryngeal-botox.variant1: Calcified or ossified cartilage that changes needle path
A-laryngeal-botox.variant2: Short neck or obesity making landmarks hard to palpate
A-laryngeal-botox.variant3: Mixed or tremor phenotypes needing different target muscles
A-laryngeal-botox.supply1: Motor — RLN to TA, LCA and PCA; external branch of SLN to cricothyroid
A-laryngeal-botox.supply2: Arterial supply — Superior and inferior laryngeal arteries; cricothyroid artery across the membrane
A-laryngeal-botox.pimp1: Q: Which muscles are injected in adductor spasmodic dysphonia? A: Thyroarytenoid, sometimes with the lateral cricoarytenoid
A-laryngeal-botox.pimp2: Q: Which muscle is injected in abductor spasmodic dysphonia? A: Posterior cricoarytenoid
A-laryngeal-botox.pimp3: Q: How does botulinum toxin A work? A: Cleaves SNAP-25 and blocks presynaptic acetylcholine release
A-laryngeal-botox.pimp4: Q: What is the activation task for the PCA on EMG? A: Sniff
A-laryngeal-botox.pimp5: Q: What is the activation task for the cricothyroid? A: Upward pitch glide
A-laryngeal-botox.pimp6: Q: How long does a typical injection last? A: About 3-4 months
A-laryngeal-botox.pimp7: Q: Why stagger PCA injections? A: To avoid bilateral abductor weakness and airway obstruction
OR-laryngeal-botox.step1: Confirm the dystonia/tremor phenotype and target muscle before injection; review prior dose/response and breathiness or dysphagia history.
OR-laryngeal-botox.step2: Position for EMG-guided or endoscopic injection and identify the cricothyroid membrane/cartilage landmarks.
OR-laryngeal-botox.step3: Advance the needle into the intended thyroarytenoid/lateral cricoarytenoid or alternative target while monitoring EMG activity when used.
OR-laryngeal-botox.step4: Confirm target activation with phonation/sniff task rather than injecting based on surface anatomy alone.
OR-laryngeal-botox.step5: Deliver the planned low-volume dose and avoid traversing the airway more than necessary.
OR-laryngeal-botox.step6: Repeat contralaterally only if the treatment plan is bilateral and symmetric.
OR-laryngeal-botox.step7: Observe briefly for bleeding, vasovagal symptoms, or airway reaction.
OR-laryngeal-botox.step8: Document exact muscle, side, dose, dilution, and prior-response context for future titration.
