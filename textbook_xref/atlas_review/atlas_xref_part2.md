
## Endoscopic Orbital / Subperiosteal Abscess Drainage  (slug: orbital-abscess, region: Rhinology / Skull Base)

A-orbital-abscess.summary: A subperiosteal orbital abscess forms when ethmoid infection crosses the thin lamina papyracea, or travels along valveless veins and neurovascular foramina, and collects between the bone and the periorbita. Endoscopic drainage removes the lamina over a medial collection and opens the diseased sinuses. The periorbita is the barrier that protects the medial rectus and orbital fat. The anterior and posterior ethmoid arteries and the optic canal mark the superior and posterior limits.
A-orbital-abscess.layer1: Nasal access — inflamed nasal mucosa; middle turbinate; uncinate; maxillary ostium. Decongest well. Uncinectomy and maxillary antrostomy show the orbital floor and give a reliable lateral reference at a known height in an inflamed, bloody field.
A-orbital-abscess.layer2: Ethmoidectomy — ethmoid bulla; anterior ethmoid cells; basal lamella; skull base. Clear the ethmoid cells adjacent to the collection, which lies at the level shown on axial and coronal CT. Identify the skull base so the superior limit is known.
A-orbital-abscess.layer3: Skeletonize and remove lamina papyracea — lamina papyracea; frontoethmoidal suture line with the AEA and PEA (superior limit); lacrimal bone / posterior lacrimal crest (anterior). Thin bone may already be eroded and pus may appear. Remove the lamina over the collection with a Cottle or curette, keeping below the ethmoid arteries.
A-orbital-abscess.layer4: Subperiosteal space — subperiosteal abscess; periorbita. Drain and culture. The space between the bone and the periorbita is open and the periorbita is usually intact; incise it only if an intraorbital (intraconal or extraconal) collection needs drainage.
A-orbital-abscess.layer5: Periorbita and orbital contents (if opened) — extraconal fat; medial rectus (deep to the periorbita, mid-height of the medial wall); superior oblique (superomedial). Make shallow incisions and use no powered instruments in the fat. Ask anesthesia for forced duction or globe movement if anatomy is unclear.
A-orbital-abscess.layer6: Source sinuses and closure — maxillary, ethmoid, frontal and sphenoid drainage pathways. Open the involved sinuses. Avoid tight nasal packing against the opened lamina, because it transmits pressure to the orbit.
A-orbital-abscess.landmark1: Lamina papyracea — Trace the medial orbital wall up from the orbital floor (seen through the maxillary antrostomy); it lies in the plane of the medial maxillary wall (The bone to remove over the abscess)
A-orbital-abscess.landmark2: Abscess location — Match the craniocaudal and anteroposterior position on CT to ethmoid landmarks (bulla, basal lamella); pus often appears through eroded bone (Directs the bone window)
A-orbital-abscess.landmark3: Anterior and posterior ethmoid arteries — Along the frontoethmoidal suture at the top of the lamina where it meets the skull base (Superior limit of bone removal)
A-orbital-abscess.landmark4: Optic canal — Posterior limit, in the posterolateral sphenoid / Onodi cell; classically about 6 mm behind the PEA, but reported distances are about 4-7 mm and can be shorter, so confirm on CT (Do not extend posterior decompression without intent)
A-orbital-abscess.landmark5: Medial rectus — Deep to the periorbita and a thin fat layer at about mid-height of the medial wall; ballotting the globe moves it (Most commonly injured muscle in endoscopic orbital work)
A-orbital-abscess.danger1: Optic nerve — where: Orbital apex and optic canal, posterior to the PEA; protect: Limit posterior bone removal, use CT orientation, and avoid packing or pressure; injury: Vision loss
A-orbital-abscess.danger2: Medial rectus — where: Deep to the periorbita over the mid medial wall; protect: Keep the periorbita intact unless necessary, make shallow incisions, and use no microdebrider in orbital fat; injury: Diplopia, restrictive or paretic strabismus
A-orbital-abscess.danger3: Anterior and posterior ethmoid arteries — where: Top of the lamina at the frontoethmoidal suture; protect: Stay below them and use bipolar if cut; injury: Orbital hematoma and orbital compartment syndrome
A-orbital-abscess.danger4: Skull base — where: Above the ethmoid cells; inflamed bone may be soft; protect: Identify the skull base early; injury: CSF leak, intracranial spread
A-orbital-abscess.danger5: Nasolacrimal sac — where: Anterior to the lamina at the lacrimal fossa; protect: Stay posterior to the lacrimal bone; injury: Epiphora
A-orbital-abscess.variant1: Dehiscent or eroded lamina papyracea
A-orbital-abscess.variant2: Superior or lateral subperiosteal abscess (often of frontal origin), which usually needs an external approach
A-orbital-abscess.variant3: Small, incompletely pneumatized sinuses in young children
A-orbital-abscess.variant4: Onodi cell bringing the optic nerve forward
A-orbital-abscess.variant5: Low or asymmetric skull base
A-orbital-abscess.supply1: Arterial — Ophthalmic artery (ICA) giving the AEA and PEA
A-orbital-abscess.supply2: Venous — Valveless superior and inferior ophthalmic veins to the cavernous sinus and pterygoid plexus (spread of infection)
A-orbital-abscess.supply3: Nerves — CN III inferior division to the medial rectus; V1 (nasociliary to the ethmoidal nerves); optic nerve via the optic canal
A-orbital-abscess.pimp1: Q: Give the Chandler classification. A: I preseptal cellulitis, II orbital cellulitis, III subperiosteal abscess, IV orbital abscess, V cavernous sinus thrombosis
A-orbital-abscess.pimp2: Q: What separates preseptal from postseptal infection? A: The orbital septum (from the orbital rim periosteum to the tarsal plates)
A-orbital-abscess.pimp3: Q: Why does ethmoid infection reach the orbit easily? A: Thin and sometimes dehiscent lamina, neurovascular foramina, and valveless ophthalmic veins
A-orbital-abscess.pimp4: Q: What passes through the annulus of Zinn? A: CN III (superior and inferior divisions), nasociliary nerve and CN VI; the optic nerve and ophthalmic artery enter through the optic canal within the ring
A-orbital-abscess.pimp5: Q: What passes through the superior orbital fissure outside the annulus? A: Frontal, lacrimal and trochlear nerves and the superior ophthalmic vein
A-orbital-abscess.pimp6: Q: Innervation of the medial rectus? A: Inferior division of the oculomotor nerve
A-orbital-abscess.pimp7: Q: Contents of the optic canal? A: Optic nerve and ophthalmic artery
OR-orbital-abscess.step1: Review contrast CT to localize the subperiosteal/orbital collection and its sinus source, then document baseline vision, pupils, color vision, and extraocular movement before drainage.
OR-orbital-abscess.step2: Establish endoscopic access through the diseased ethmoid/maxillary/frontal pathway needed to reach the collection while defining the skull base and lamina papyracea.
OR-orbital-abscess.step3: Skeletonize the lamina over the abscess and remove a controlled segment of bone, keeping the periorbita intact until the intended drainage site is clearly identified.
OR-orbital-abscess.step4: Open or elevate the periorbita at the collection, drain purulence under direct visualization, and obtain cultures before irrigation when feasible.
OR-orbital-abscess.step5: Decompress the full subperiosteal pocket without blindly instrumenting orbital fat or injuring the medial rectus and other extraocular structures.
OR-orbital-abscess.step6: Open the causative sinus drainage pathways adequately so persistent infected sinus disease cannot immediately re-seed the orbit.
OR-orbital-abscess.step7: Reinspect the orbit/sinus interface for decompression and hemostasis and avoid tight nasal packing that transmits pressure to the orbit.
OR-orbital-abscess.step8: Repeat and document postoperative vision, pupils, color vision, and extraocular movement immediately; any deterioration triggers urgent orbital-compartment reassessment.

## Septoplasty  (slug: septoplasty, region: Rhinology / Skull Base)

A-septoplasty.summary: Septoplasty corrects an obstructing septum by working in the avascular subperichondrial and subperiosteal planes. It removes or straightens deviated quadrangular cartilage, perpendicular plate, vomer and maxillary crest while preserving a dorsal and caudal L-strut that holds up the nasal dorsum and tip. Safety rests on keeping at least one mucoperichondrial flap intact at each point, protecting the keystone area, and never twisting the perpendicular plate where it joins the cribriform plate.
A-septoplasty.layer1: Infiltration and incision — septal mucosa; caudal septum; membranous septum / columella; medial crural footplates. Hemitransfixion incision at the caudal septal margin gives access to the caudal septum and nasal spine. A Killian incision 1-1.5 cm behind the caudal edge preserves the caudal attachments but leaves caudal deflections unreachable.
A-septoplasty.layer2: Subperichondrial flap — mucoperichondrium; quadrangular cartilage (blue-gray). Cut through the perichondrium until the bluish cartilage is seen, then elevate with a Cottle elevator in a bloodless, white, glistening plane. Bleeding and pink tissue mean you are too superficial.
A-septoplasty.layer3: Inferior tunnels and maxillary crest — maxillary crest and premaxilla; decussating fibres at the chondro-premaxillary junction; periosteum of the nasal floor; incisive canal / nasopalatine nerve (anterior floor). Perichondrium and periosteum are not continuous at the crest, so tears are common here. Raise a separate inferior subperiosteal tunnel from the nasal floor and join it to the superior tunnel by sharp division of the fibres.
A-septoplasty.layer4: Bony-cartilaginous junction and contralateral side — chondro-ethmoidal junction (posterosuperior); chondro-vomerine junction (posteroinferior, common spur site); contralateral mucoperichondrium / mucoperiosteum. Disarticulate the cartilage from the bone posteriorly and cross to the opposite side there. Elevate the contralateral subperiosteal flap over the bone.
A-septoplasty.layer5: Bony septum — perpendicular plate of ethmoid (superior / posterior); vomer (inferior / posterior); sphenoid rostrum; cribriform plate (superior attachment of the perpendicular plate). Remove deviated bone with through-cutting forceps. Do not rock or twist the perpendicular plate, because force is transmitted to the cribriform plate.
A-septoplasty.layer6: Cartilage resection and L-strut — dorsal strut; caudal strut; keystone area (septum, upper lateral cartilages and nasal bones junction); anterior nasal spine. Resect or score only the obstructing segment and keep a dorsal and caudal strut of at least about 1-1.5 cm. A caudal deflection may need repositioning and suturing to the nasal spine periosteum.
A-septoplasty.layer7: Closure — hemitransfixion incision; mucoperichondrial flaps; quilting sutures; splints. Close the incision and quilt the flaps together to remove dead space. Repair any tears so that tears on the two sides do not lie opposite each other.
A-septoplasty.landmark1: Subperichondrial plane — Score the cartilage until it shows blue-gray and elevate under the shiny perichondrium; the correct plane is nearly bloodless (Wrong-plane elevation causes bleeding, flap thinning and perforation)
A-septoplasty.landmark2: Bony-cartilaginous junction — Feel the change in resistance as the Freer passes from cartilage to bone posteriorly; disarticulate along this line (Safe place to cross to the opposite side and reach deviated bone)
A-septoplasty.landmark3: Maxillary crest — Along the nasal floor at the base of the septum; reach it from an inferior tunnel elevated off the nasal floor (Spurs and crest deviation cause floor obstruction, and the decussating fibres here tear flaps)
A-septoplasty.landmark4: Keystone area — Junction under the rhinion where the dorsal septum meets the nasal bones and upper lateral cartilages; palpate the dorsum externally (Resection or disruption here causes saddle nose)
A-septoplasty.landmark5: Anterior nasal spine — Palpable at the base of the columella in the hemitransfixion incision; the caudal septum sits on or beside it (Anchor point for repositioning a dislocated caudal septum)
A-septoplasty.danger1: Opposing mucoperichondrial flaps — where: Both sides of the septum; most at risk over spurs and the maxillary crest; protect: Stay strictly subperichondrial, use separate inferior tunnels, and repair tears so they are not opposite each other; injury: Septal perforation
A-septoplasty.danger2: Dorsal and caudal L-strut / keystone — where: Dorsal septum beneath the nasal bones and the caudal septum above the nasal spine; protect: Preserve at least about 1-1.5 cm of strut, avoid resection at the keystone, and do not twist the perpendicular plate; injury: Saddle nose, tip ptosis, columellar retraction
A-septoplasty.danger3: Cribriform plate / skull base — where: Superior attachment of the perpendicular plate; protect: Through-cutting instruments and no rocking or twisting of high posterior bone; injury: CSF rhinorrhea, anosmia
A-septoplasty.danger4: Nasopalatine nerve and vessels — where: Incisive canal at the anterior nasal floor and premaxilla; protect: Avoid aggressive dissection and resection at the premaxilla; injury: Numbness of the upper incisors and anterior palate, bleeding
A-septoplasty.danger5: Septal blood supply (Kiesselbach area, posterior septal artery) — where: Anterior septum (Little's area) and posterior septum near the sphenoid face; protect: Stay in the subperichondrial plane, quilt or splint, and control bleeding before closure; injury: Septal hematoma leading to cartilage necrosis or abscess, and epistaxis
A-septoplasty.variant1: Caudal septal dislocation off the anterior nasal spine
A-septoplasty.variant2: Spur at the chondro-vomerine junction along the maxillary crest
A-septoplasty.variant3: Contralateral concha bullosa or compensatory inferior turbinate hypertrophy
A-septoplasty.variant4: Pneumatized septum or sphenoid rostrum extension
A-septoplasty.variant5: Prior septal surgery with scarred or absent cartilage (flaps adhere and tear easily)
A-septoplasty.supply1: Arterial (Kiesselbach plexus) — Anterior ethmoid (ICA), sphenopalatine posterior septal branch (ECA), greater palatine via the incisive canal, superior labial septal branch (facial)
A-septoplasty.supply2: Posterior — Posterior septal artery crossing the sphenoid face below the ostium (pedicle of the nasoseptal flap)
A-septoplasty.supply3: Sensory — Nasopalatine nerve (V2) posteroinferiorly; anterior ethmoidal nerve (V1) anterosuperiorly
A-septoplasty.supply4: Framework — Quadrangular cartilage, perpendicular plate of ethmoid, vomer, maxillary and palatine crests, premaxilla
A-septoplasty.pimp1: Q: Which arteries form Kiesselbach plexus? A: Anterior ethmoid, sphenopalatine (septal branch), greater palatine and superior labial arteries
A-septoplasty.pimp2: Q: Name the components of the nasal septum. A: Quadrangular cartilage, perpendicular plate of ethmoid, vomer, maxillary and palatine crests; membranous septum anteriorly
A-septoplasty.pimp3: Q: Why do flaps tear at the maxillary crest? A: Perichondrium and periosteum are not continuous there (decussating fibres), so separate inferior tunnels are needed
A-septoplasty.pimp4: Q: Minimum L-strut width? A: At least about 1 cm dorsally and caudally; many surgeons leave 1.5 cm
A-septoplasty.pimp5: Q: What is the keystone area? A: The junction of the dorsal septum, nasal bones and upper lateral cartilages; disruption causes saddle deformity
A-septoplasty.pimp6: Q: Normal internal nasal valve angle? A: About 10-15 degrees, between the septum and the caudal edge of the upper lateral cartilage
A-septoplasty.pimp7: Q: Why is septal hematoma urgent? A: The cartilage depends on perichondrial diffusion; hematoma causes necrosis, abscess and saddle nose
A-septoplasty.pimp8: Q: What passes through the incisive canal? A: Nasopalatine nerve and the terminal greater palatine / sphenopalatine (nasopalatine) vessels
OR-septoplasty.step1: Infiltrate the septum, make the planned hemitransfixion/Killian-type incision, and elevate a subperichondrial flap with the cartilage continuously visible.
OR-septoplasty.step2: Cross to the opposite side only at a controlled point and preserve an intact contralateral mucoperichondrial flap whenever possible.
OR-septoplasty.step3: Define the deviated cartilage/bone and the maxillary crest while maintaining an adequate dorsal and caudal L-strut.
OR-septoplasty.step4: Resect or reshape only the obstructing septal components; avoid opposing mucosal tears and uncontrolled high posterior dissection.
OR-septoplasty.step5: Address the bony-cartilaginous junction and spurs under direct visualization, protecting the skull base superiorly.
OR-septoplasty.step6: Reposition remaining cartilage so the caudal septum is stable in the midline and the airway is improved bilaterally.
OR-septoplasty.step7: Close mucosal incisions and quilting sutures to eliminate dead space and reduce hematoma risk.
OR-septoplasty.step8: Place splints only when indicated and confirm bilateral airway patency and hemostasis.

## Endoscopic Sphenopalatine Artery Ligation  (slug: spa-ligation, region: Rhinology / Skull Base)

A-spa-ligation.summary: Most posterior epistaxis comes from the sphenopalatine artery (SPA), the terminal branch of the internal maxillary artery. The SPA enters the nose through the sphenopalatine foramen at the back of the middle meatus, just behind the crista ethmoidalis. Control works only if every branch is found, because the artery often divides into two or more branches before or at the foramen. Dissection stays out of the pterygopalatine fossa and away from the orbit above.
A-spa-ligation.layer1: Expose the posterior lateral wall — middle turbinate (posterior attachment / tail); posterior fontanelle; maxillary antrostomy (optional); posterior wall of maxillary sinus. Clear clot and decongest, and medialize the middle turbinate. A maxillary antrostomy shows the posterior maxillary wall; the foramen sits just medial and superior to it.
A-spa-ligation.layer2: Mucoperiosteal flap — vertical incision on the lateral wall about 1 cm anterior to the middle turbinate tail / horizontal attachment; mucoperiosteum; perpendicular plate of palatine bone. Elevate a subperiosteal flap posteriorly toward the foramen. Staying on bone keeps the flap intact and the field drier.
A-spa-ligation.layer3: Crista ethmoidalis — crista ethmoidalis (vertical ridge on the palatine bone); sphenopalatine foramen (just posterior). The crista is the key landmark: the foramen and artery lie immediately behind it. Removing the crista with a Kerrison widens the exposure of the artery and its branches.
A-spa-ligation.layer4: Identify all branches — posterior lateral nasal artery; posterior septal artery (runs medially across the sphenoid face); accessory foramina / additional branches. Elevate superiorly and inferiorly around the foramen to find every trunk. Clip and/or bipolar each branch. Inspect for an accessory foramen before laying the flap back.
A-spa-ligation.layer5: Reassess persistent bleeding — anterior and posterior ethmoid arteries (superior); septal sources; contralateral supply; greater palatine contribution. Bleeding from high in the roof points to the ethmoid (ICA) system; these vessels are controlled surgically, not by embolization. Do not cauterize deeper into the pterygopalatine fossa or toward the orbit.
A-spa-ligation.landmark1: Sphenopalatine foramen — At the posterior end of the middle turbinate horizontal attachment, at the junction of the middle and superior meatus, directly behind the crista ethmoidalis (Where the SPA and its branches enter the nose)
A-spa-ligation.landmark2: Crista ethmoidalis — A small vertical bony ridge on the perpendicular plate of the palatine bone, felt under the elevated flap just anterior to the foramen (The most reliable landmark; the artery emerges just posterior to it)
A-spa-ligation.landmark3: Posterior maxillary wall — Seen through a maxillary antrostomy; the foramen lies superomedial to it (Helps when the crista is small or absent)
A-spa-ligation.landmark4: Posterior septal artery — Leaves the foramen and runs medially across the anterior sphenoid face below the ostium (An alternative septal source that must be included in control)
A-spa-ligation.landmark5: Ethmoid arteries — Along the skull base, the AEA at the back of the frontal recess and the PEA in front of the sphenoid face (Bleeding from high in the roof points here instead of the SPA)
A-spa-ligation.danger1: Multiple SPA branches / accessory foramina — where: At and around the foramen; branching is often proximal to the foramen; protect: Wide subperiosteal exposure above and below the crista and check for additional branches before closing; injury: Missed branch and early rebleeding
A-spa-ligation.danger2: Orbit (lamina papyracea and orbital floor / apex) — where: Superolateral to the foramen if dissection drifts up and forward through the ethmoid or maxillary roof; protect: Keep dissection at the middle turbinate tail level and do not drill or cauterize superolaterally; injury: Orbital injury, hematoma
A-spa-ligation.danger3: Pterygopalatine fossa contents (internal maxillary artery, pterygopalatine ganglion, V2, vidian nerve) — where: Directly behind the foramen; protect: Control the vessel at the foramen and avoid deep cautery or packing into the fossa; injury: Uncontrolled bleeding, V2 numbness, dry eye from vidian / ganglion injury
A-spa-ligation.danger4: Descending (greater) palatine artery — where: In the pterygopalatine canal, inferior to the foramen within the palatine bone; protect: Avoid inferior drilling in the perpendicular plate; injury: Palatal ischemic symptoms, bleeding
A-spa-ligation.danger5: Skull base / sphenoid — where: Posterosuperior, above the sphenoid face; protect: Stay at the foramen level; injury: CSF leak
A-spa-ligation.variant1: The SPA divides into two or more branches before entering the nose in many patients
A-spa-ligation.variant2: Accessory foramen posterior or inferior to the main foramen
A-spa-ligation.variant3: Small or absent crista ethmoidalis
A-spa-ligation.variant4: Significant ethmoid (ICA system) contribution to posterior bleeding
A-spa-ligation.variant5: Hypervascular lesion (for example juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma in an adolescent male) as the true source
A-spa-ligation.supply1: Arterial — ECA, then internal maxillary artery (third / pterygopalatine segment), then SPA, which divides into the posterior lateral nasal and posterior septal arteries
A-spa-ligation.supply2: Anastomoses — With the AEA and PEA (ophthalmic, from the ICA), the greater palatine and the superior labial; posterior inferior meatus plexus (Woodruff)
A-spa-ligation.supply3: Neural (pterygopalatine fossa) — V2, pterygopalatine ganglion, vidian nerve (parasympathetic to the lacrimal gland and nasal glands)
A-spa-ligation.pimp1: Q: What are the three segments of the internal maxillary artery? A: Mandibular (first), pterygoid (second) and pterygopalatine (third); the SPA is the terminal branch of the third
A-spa-ligation.pimp2: Q: Main landmark for the sphenopalatine foramen? A: Crista ethmoidalis; the foramen is just posterior to it
A-spa-ligation.pimp3: Q: What bones form the sphenopalatine foramen? A: The sphenopalatine notch of the palatine bone (between its orbital and sphenoid processes), closed above by the sphenoid body
A-spa-ligation.pimp4: Q: What are the two main SPA branches in the nose? A: Posterior lateral nasal artery and posterior septal artery
A-spa-ligation.pimp5: Q: Why is AEA bleeding not embolized? A: It is an ophthalmic (ICA) branch; embolization risks blindness and stroke
A-spa-ligation.pimp6: Q: What is Woodruff plexus? A: A vascular plexus on the posterior lateral wall near the inferior meatus, historically described as venous; a source of posterior epistaxis
A-spa-ligation.pimp7: Q: Why does ECA ligation fail more often than SPA ligation? A: It is far from the bleeding point, so collaterals from the opposite ECA and from the ICA refill the nose
OR-spa-ligation.step1: For endoscopic SPA control, decongest and clear clot until the posterior lateral nasal wall is visible; elevate the mucoperiosteal flap in the posterior middle meatus and use the crista ethmoidalis as the practical landmark just anterior to the sphenopalatine foramen. Expose far enough posteriorly and superior/inferiorly to identify the arterial branches rather than cauterizing one apparent trunk through a narrow window. Multiple branches and accessory foramina are common, so complete control requires deliberate inspection for additional posterior nasal/SPA branches before the flap is replaced.
OR-spa-ligation.step2: If bleeding persists or recurs after apparently adequate SPA control, stop assuming the same branch is the source. Re-clear the field and reassess for a missed SPA/posterior nasal branch, contralateral supply, septal source, anterior or posterior ethmoidal contribution, tumor/vascular lesion, or an iatrogenic pseudoaneurysm. A superior source near the roof or bleeding pattern inconsistent with the SPA territory should redirect the operation rather than provoke deeper blind cautery around the skull base or orbit.
OR-spa-ligation.step3: Persistent or recurrent bleeding not controlled by packing/cautery should prompt explicit selection between further surgical arterial control and endovascular embolization based on source localization, hemodynamic stability, prior surgery, comorbidity, anatomy and immediately available expertise; neither modality should be taught as universally mandatory before the other. If embolization is chosen, angiography must account for dangerous external-to-internal carotid and ophthalmic anastomoses because non-target embolization can cause stroke, blindness or tissue necrosis. Suspected internal-carotid injury, pseudoaneurysm or bleeding after skull-base/transsphenoidal surgery is a different major-vessel pathway: use temporizing tamponade/resuscitation and urgent neurointerventional/skull-base escalation rather than blind deep clipping or cautery.
OR-spa-ligation.step4: Create middle-meatal access and elevate a mucoperiosteal flap along the posterior lateral nasal wall toward the crista ethmoidalis.
OR-spa-ligation.step5: Identify the sphenopalatine foramen by direct anatomy rather than following bleeding blindly posteriorly.
OR-spa-ligation.step6: Expose the arterial branches as they emerge from the foramen, recognizing that multiple branches/foramina may be present.
OR-spa-ligation.step7: Clip or cauterize each identified branch under direct visualization, protecting surrounding mucosa and avoiding uncontrolled deep thermal spread.
OR-spa-ligation.step8: Inspect superior and inferior to the main foramen for accessory branches if bleeding control is incomplete.
OR-spa-ligation.step9: Return the mucosal flap to cover exposed bone and treated vessels.
OR-spa-ligation.step10: Reinspect the nasal cavity and nasopharynx for persistent bleeding from a second source.
OR-spa-ligation.step11: Pack selectively and document blood-loss/resuscitation needs and recurrence plan.

## Endoscopic Sphenoidotomy  (slug: sphenoidotomy, region: Rhinology / Skull Base)

A-sphenoidotomy.summary: The sphenoid sinus lies directly against the optic nerves, cavernous ICAs, sella and clivus, so the opening must start at the natural ostium and be enlarged inferiorly and medially, away from these structures. The ostium is found medial to the superior turbinate in the sphenoethmoidal recess. Inside, carotid and optic bulges and septations that insert on them are identified before any lateral or superior work.
A-sphenoidotomy.layer1: Transnasal corridor — inferior and middle turbinates; choana and posterior choanal arch; septum; superior turbinate. Lateralize or medialize the middle turbinate gently. Look above the choana, between the septum and the superior turbinate, to find the sphenoethmoidal recess.
A-sphenoidotomy.layer2: Sphenoethmoidal recess and ostium — sphenoethmoidal recess; natural sphenoid ostium (medial to the superior turbinate); olfactory mucosa (superior). The ostium is usually medial to the superior turbinate, above the level of its inferior edge and within about 1 cm of the midline. It is classically described as about 1-1.5 cm above the choanal arch, but cadaveric series report up to about 2-3 cm, so the superior turbinate is the more reliable guide. Resecting the lower part of the superior turbinate improves exposure; preserve its upper part, which carries olfactory mucosa.
A-sphenoidotomy.layer3: Enlarge inferiorly and medially — anterior sphenoid wall; sphenoid rostrum / posterior septum; posterior septal artery (crosses the sphenoid face below the ostium). Use a Kerrison or mushroom punch directed inferiorly and medially. The posterior septal artery crosses between the ostium and the choana; bipolar it, or preserve it if a nasoseptal flap may be needed.
A-sphenoidotomy.layer4: Transethmoid alternative — posterior ethmoid; Onodi cell; anterior sphenoid wall. If coming through the ethmoid, enter the sphenoid at its lowest and most medial point. Confirm the opening against the transnasal ostium so a posterior ethmoid or Onodi cell is not mistaken for the sphenoid.
A-sphenoidotomy.layer5: Sinus survey — sella turcica (posterosuperior midline); planum sphenoidale (superior); clival recess (posteroinferior); optic prominence (superolateral); carotid prominence (lateral, paraclival / parasellar); lateral opticocarotid recess; intersinus septum. Identify the bulges before using powered instruments laterally or superiorly. Intersinus septa are often off midline and may insert onto the carotid prominence; remove them with through-cutting tools, never by twisting.
A-sphenoidotomy.layer6: Lateral recess (when pneumatized) — V2 / foramen rotundum (superolateral); vidian canal (floor, inferomedial); lateral recess under the greater wing. A pneumatized lateral recess extends between V2 and the vidian nerve. It is a common site for lateral sphenoid encephalocele and CSF leak.
A-sphenoidotomy.landmark1: Natural sphenoid ostium — Medial to the superior turbinate in the sphenoethmoidal recess, close to the septum (within about 1 cm of the midline), above the level of the superior turbinate's inferior edge. Classically about 1-1.5 cm above the posterior choanal arch, though reported distances range up to about 2-3 cm. Gently lateralize the superior turbinate or resect its inferior part to see it. (Safe entry point, inferior and medial to the optic nerve and ICA)
A-sphenoidotomy.landmark2: Posterior septal artery — Crosses the anterior sphenoid face horizontally between the inferior margin of the ostium and the choanal arch (Bleeds with inferior enlargement; it is the nasoseptal flap pedicle)

OR-sphenoidotomy.step7: Treat intrasinus disease under direct visualization, keeping powered instrumentation away from the optic nerve/carotid unless their course is unequivocally defined.
OR-sphenoidotomy.step8: Confirm a widely patent ostium and hemostasis before completion.

## Open Partial / Conservation Laryngectomy  (slug: conservation-laryngectomy, region: Head & Neck Oncology)

A-conservation-laryngectomy.summary: Conservation laryngectomy removes laryngeal cancer while keeping the three functions (airway, voice, swallowing) by preserving at least one functioning cricoarytenoid unit (arytenoid, cricoid, its RLN and SLN supply). Vertical hemilaryngectomy removes one hemilarynx through a thyrotomy; supraglottic laryngectomy removes the larynx above the ventricles; supracricoid laryngectomy removes the thyroid cartilage and both folds, reconstructing with cricohyoidopexy or cricohyoidoepiglottopexy.
A-conservation-laryngectomy.layer1: Exposure — apron flap; strap muscles (sternohyoid, thyrohyoid); thyroid isthmus; external perichondrium. Elevate the thyrohyoid and outer perichondrium off the thyroid ala (kept for closure). Tracheotomy is placed away from the reconstruction.
A-conservation-laryngectomy.layer2: Superior laryngeal neurovascular bundles — superior laryngeal artery; internal branch of SLN on the thyrohyoid membrane. Preserve the internal SLN on the preserved side (sensation protects against aspiration). In supraglottic laryngectomy, at least one SLN is kept.
A-conservation-laryngectomy.layer3: Inferior constrictor and pyriform — inferior constrictor on the oblique line; pyriform sinus mucosa; inferior cornu / cricothyroid joint. Release the constrictor and pyriform mucosa from the posterior thyroid ala. In supracricoid surgery, disarticulate the cricothyroid joints carefully, as the RLN enters directly behind them.
A-conservation-laryngectomy.layer4: Framework cuts — thyroid notch; vocal fold level (classically taught at about the midpoint between notch and lower border; often somewhat higher); cricothyroid membrane; hyoid. For supraglottic resection the cut runs just above the anterior commissure level. The commissure is classically placed at about the midpoint of the anterior thyroid cartilage height, but cadaver and CT studies often find it at or above the midpoint with wide individual variation, so confirm endoscopically (e.g., needle through the cricothyroid membrane or ventricle) before cutting. Vertical cuts for hemilaryngectomy are made at or slightly off midline.
A-conservation-laryngectomy.layer5: Laryngeal entry and internal spaces — pre-epiglottic space; paraglottic space; ventricle; anterior commissure (Broyles ligament). Enter away from tumor (e.g., vallecula or pyriform for supraglottic; cricothyroid membrane for supracricoid). The pre-epiglottic space is removed with supraglottic laryngectomy and with SCPL-CHP; it is largely preserved in SCPL-CHEP.
A-conservation-laryngectomy.layer6: Cricoarytenoid unit — arytenoid (vocal process, muscular process); cricoarytenoid joint; posterior cricoarytenoid; RLN branches entering posteriorly. Preserve at least one mobile arytenoid with its RLN and SLN; the posterior arytenoid mucosa and the joint must be kept intact.
A-conservation-laryngectomy.layer7: Reconstruction — cricoid; hyoid; epiglottis remnant (CHEP); tongue base; residual perichondrium/straps. Cricohyoidopexy sutures pass around the cricoid, through the tongue base and around the hyoid. Arytenoid is pulled anteriorly to prevent posterior tilt; supraglottic closure approximates thyroid perichondrium to tongue base.
A-conservation-laryngectomy.landmark1: Glottic level on the external framework — Classically at about the midpoint between the thyroid notch and the inferior border of the thyroid cartilage anteriorly; studies often place the anterior commissure at or above the midpoint, with wide individual variation; confirm endoscopically (Guides supraglottic horizontal cuts and anterior commissure protection)
A-conservation-laryngectomy.landmark2: Cricothyroid joint / RLN entry — Follow the posterior border of the thyroid ala to the inferior cornu; the RLN enters immediately posterior and deep to it (Disarticulation in supracricoid laryngectomy must protect the RLN)
A-conservation-laryngectomy.landmark3: Internal SLN — Pierces the thyrohyoid membrane posterolaterally just below the greater cornu of the hyoid with the superior laryngeal artery (Preserving sensation prevents aspiration)
A-conservation-laryngectomy.landmark4: Pre-epiglottic space — Fat anterior to the epiglottis between the hyoid and thyroid cartilage, deep to the thyrohyoid membrane (Resected in supraglottic laryngectomy and SCPL-CHP; commonly invaded by infrahyoid epiglottic tumors)
A-conservation-laryngectomy.landmark5: Arytenoid — Endolaryngeally at the posterior glottis, vocal process at the posterior end of the true fold, sitting on the posterolateral cricoid lamina (Preserved functional unit)
A-conservation-laryngectomy.danger1: Recurrent laryngeal nerve (preserved side) — where: Enters behind the cricothyroid joint; branches to PCA and adductors posteriorly; protect: Careful disarticulation staying on cartilage; avoid deep posterior dissection near the joint; injury: Loss of the functional arytenoid (aspiration, airway failure)
A-conservation-laryngectomy.danger2: Internal SLN (preserved side) — where: Thyrohyoid membrane posterolaterally; protect: Identify and preserve on at least one side; injury: Supraglottic anesthesia, chronic aspiration
A-conservation-laryngectomy.danger3: Hypoglossal nerve — where: Above the greater hyoid cornu; protect: Stay on bone when releasing the hyoid; injury: Tongue weakness, worse swallowing after pexy
A-conservation-laryngectomy.danger4: Oncologic margins — where: Anterior commissure, paraglottic space, cricoid, interarytenoid region; protect: Strict patient selection; frozen margins; injury: Recurrence requiring total laryngectomy
A-conservation-laryngectomy.danger5: Airway — where: Postoperative edema and reconstruction; protect: Temporary tracheostomy; injury: Obstruction, stenosis
A-conservation-laryngectomy.danger6: Cartilage (perichondrium) — where: Remaining thyroid/cricoid, especially after radiation; protect: Preserve perichondrium; careful patient selection; injury: Chondronecrosis
A-conservation-laryngectomy.variant1: Laryngocele through the thyrohyoid membrane.
A-conservation-laryngectomy.variant2: Ossified thyroid cartilage (older patients) that changes cut technique and tumor invasion appearance.
A-conservation-laryngectomy.variant3: Female larynx with smaller dimensions and a less acute thyroid angle, shifting external landmarks.
A-conservation-laryngectomy.variant4: Extralaryngeal branching of the RLN on the preserved side.
A-conservation-laryngectomy.supply1: Arterial — Superior laryngeal (from superior thyroid) through thyrohyoid membrane; cricothyroid branch; inferior laryngeal (from inferior thyroid) with the RLN
A-conservation-laryngectomy.supply2: Sensory — Internal SLN above the folds; RLN below
A-conservation-laryngectomy.supply3: Motor — RLN: PCA (only abductor), LCA, interarytenoid, thyroarytenoid; EBSLN: cricothyroid
A-conservation-laryngectomy.supply4: Compartments — Pre-epiglottic space (anterior), paraglottic spaces (lateral), bounded by conus elasticus inferiorly and quadrangular membrane medially
A-conservation-laryngectomy.pimp1: Q: What is the cricoarytenoid unit? A: One arytenoid, the cricoid, and the associated RLN and SLN, the minimum functional unit for a conservation laryngectomy
A-conservation-laryngectomy.pimp2: Q: What are the boundaries of the paraglottic space? A: Thyroid cartilage laterally, conus elasticus inferomedially, quadrangular membrane superomedially, pyriform sinus mucosa posteriorly; continuous with pre-epiglottic space anteriorly
A-conservation-laryngectomy.pimp3: Q: What structures are removed in a supracricoid laryngectomy? A: Entire thyroid cartilage, both true and false folds, both ventricles and paraglottic spaces, and up to one arytenoid; with CHP also the whole epiglottis and pre-epiglottic space, with CHEP the infrahyoid epiglottis (petiole)
A-conservation-laryngectomy.pimp4: Q: What is the difference between CHP and CHEP? A: CHP: cricohyoidopexy (entire epiglottis and pre-epiglottic space removed). CHEP: cricohyoidoepiglottopexy (suprahyoid epiglottis preserved and included in the pexy)
A-conservation-laryngectomy.pimp5: Q: Which muscle is the only vocal fold abductor? A: Posterior cricoarytenoid
A-conservation-laryngectomy.pimp6: Q: What is the Broyles ligament and why is it relevant? A: The anterior commissure tendon inserting into the thyroid cartilage, lacking perichondrium, a route for cartilage invasion
A-conservation-laryngectomy.pimp7: Q: Where is the glottis relative to the external thyroid cartilage? A: Classically at about the midpoint of the anterior vertical height between the notch and inferior border (often slightly higher, and variable, so confirm endoscopically)
A-conservation-laryngectomy.pimp8: Q: Which nerve must be preserved to limit aspiration after supraglottic laryngectomy? A: Internal branch of the superior laryngeal nerve (at least one)
OR-conservation-laryngectomy.step1: Expose the laryngeal framework and neck according to the planned vertical or supracricoid conservation resection, completing indicated neck dissection before opening the pharynx/larynx.
OR-conservation-laryngectomy.step2: Define tumor extent endoscopically and externally, identifying the anterior commissure, paraglottic/pre-epiglottic spaces, cricoarytenoid unit, thyroid cartilage, and oncologic margins that must be preserved.
OR-conservation-laryngectomy.step3: Perform framework cuts and enter the larynx through a tumor-safe site, keeping at least one functional cricoarytenoid unit when required by the chosen conservation operation.
OR-conservation-laryngectomy.step4: Complete mucosal and deep soft-tissue cuts around the tumor with orientation to the arytenoid, pyriform sinus, tongue base, and subglottis; remove the specimen en bloc.
OR-conservation-laryngectomy.step5: Orient the specimen and obtain/confirm margins from the patient bed as indicated before reconstructing the airway.
OR-conservation-laryngectomy.step6: Reconstruct the residual larynx according to the operation—reapproximate mucosa/framework or perform cricohyoido(epiglotto)pexy—without excessive tension or rotational distortion.
OR-conservation-laryngectomy.step7: Create/secure the temporary tracheostomy and feeding access when required, then test the reconstruction for patency, hemostasis, and pharyngeal integrity.
OR-conservation-laryngectomy.step8: Close drains/neck and establish explicit postoperative airway, aspiration, swallowing-rehabilitation, and decannulation milestones.

## Head & Neck Free-Flap Reconstruction — Core Framework  (slug: free-flap-basics, region: Head & Neck Oncology)

A-free-flap-basics.summary: Free-flap reconstruction moves tissue with its own artery and vein from a donor site and reconnects it to recipient vessels in the neck. Success depends on two anatomic pairings: a flap whose pedicle length and caliber match the defect, and recipient vessels (ECA branches, IJV system or EJV) with healthy intima reached by a tension-free, twist-free path.
A-free-flap-basics.layer1: Recipient neck exposure — facial artery and vein (at the mandible or in level I); superior thyroid artery; lingual artery; ECA trunk; IJV and its tributaries (facial, common facial); EJV. Choose recipient vessels near the defect: facial for oral/midface, superior thyroid for pharynx/larynx. Preserve them during neck dissection.
A-free-flap-basics.layer2: Flap harvest: radial forearm (RFFF) — radial artery and venae comitantes in the lateral intermuscular septum between brachioradialis and flexor carpi radialis; cephalic vein; superficial radial nerve (under brachioradialis); lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve. Confirm ulnar collateral flow (Allen test). Preserve paratenon on tendons for graft take; protect the superficial radial nerve.
A-free-flap-basics.layer3: Flap harvest: anterolateral thigh (ALT) — descending branch of lateral circumflex femoral artery (from profunda femoris); septocutaneous or musculocutaneous perforators through vastus lateralis; intermuscular septum between rectus femoris and vastus lateralis; motor nerve to vastus lateralis. Perforators cluster near the midpoint of a line from the ASIS to the superolateral patella. Preserve the motor nerve to vastus lateralis running with the pedicle.
A-free-flap-basics.layer4: Flap harvest: fibula — peroneal artery and venae comitantes (posterior to the fibula, between flexor hallucis longus and tibialis posterior); posterior crural septum skin perforators; common peroneal nerve at the fibular neck; distal fibula (at least ~6 cm, commonly cited 6-8 cm, for ankle stability). Preserve proximal fibula (peroneal nerve) and distal fibula (ankle syndesmosis). Assess the leg vessels for peronea magna before harvest.
A-free-flap-basics.layer5: Pedicle dissection and division — artery; venae comitantes or superficial vein; adventitia. Trace to adequate length and caliber; divide only when recipient vessels are ready, to keep ischemia time short.
A-free-flap-basics.layer6: Inset and pedicle routing — defect lining and cover; subcutaneous tunnel or over-mandible path; mandible edge. Inset enough to prevent gravity traction before the anastomosis; route the pedicle without kink, twist or compression beneath tight tunnels or bone edges.
A-free-flap-basics.layer7: Microvascular anastomosis — recipient artery end; recipient vein (end-to-end or end-to-side to IJV); venous coupler. Trim to healthy intima, remove adventitia only at the cut end, irrigate with heparinized saline. Two venous anastomoses (deep and superficial systems) are used by some surgeons.
A-free-flap-basics.layer8: Closure — drains; neck skin. Place drains away from the pedicle; avoid tight neck closure or tracheostomy ties over the pedicle.
A-free-flap-basics.landmark1: Facial artery (recipient) — At the antegonial notch on the inferior mandibular border just anterior to masseter, or deep to the posterior submandibular gland arising from the ECA (Close to oral and midface defects)
A-free-flap-basics.landmark2: Superior thyroid artery (recipient) — First anterior ECA branch just above the carotid bifurcation, arching anteroinferiorly toward the upper thyroid pole (Reliable for laryngopharyngeal reconstruction)
A-free-flap-basics.landmark3: IJV tributaries — Common facial vein entering the IJV near the hyoid level; end-to-side directly to the IJV if tributaries are unusable (Low-pressure, high-flow venous outflow)
A-free-flap-basics.landmark4: Radial artery pedicle — Pulse proximal to the wrist crease between FCR and brachioradialis; follow the lateral intermuscular septum proximally to the brachial bifurcation (Long, consistent pedicle)
A-free-flap-basics.landmark5: ALT perforators — Doppler within a ~3 cm radius of the midpoint of the ASIS-to-superolateral-patella line, usually in the inferolateral quadrant (Skin paddle design)
A-free-flap-basics.landmark6: Peroneal artery — Posterolateral leg; through the posterior crural septum, dissect between FHL and tibialis posterior behind the fibula (Fibula bone flap pedicle)
A-free-flap-basics.danger1: Pedicle geometry — where: From flap to recipient vessels across the mandible or neck; protect: Check for twist (mark the vessel), kink and tension before closure; position the head as postoperatively; injury: Arterial thrombosis or venous congestion
A-free-flap-basics.danger2: Recipient vessels in irradiated or dissected necks — where: Facial, superior thyroid, transverse cervical; protect: Inspect for intimal disease; consider transverse cervical or contralateral vessels; vein graft for gaps; injury: Thrombosis, failure
A-free-flap-basics.danger3: Donor nerves — where: Superficial radial (RFFF), common peroneal at fibular neck (fibula), motor nerve to vastus lateralis (ALT); protect: Identify and preserve during harvest; injury: Hand dorsum numbness; foot drop; quadriceps weakness
A-free-flap-basics.danger4: Donor hand/foot perfusion — where: Radial artery (hand), peroneal artery (foot, if peronea magna); protect: Allen test; leg vessel imaging or exam; injury: Hand or foot ischemia
A-free-flap-basics.danger5: Ankle stability — where: Distal fibula; protect: Leave at least ~6 cm of distal fibula (commonly cited 6-8 cm) and a similar proximal segment below the fibular head; injury: Ankle instability
A-free-flap-basics.variant1: Incomplete palmar arch / ulnar dominance problems (abnormal Allen test) precluding RFFF.
A-free-flap-basics.variant2: Peronea magna (peroneal artery as the dominant or sole foot supply), precluding fibula harvest on that side.
A-free-flap-basics.variant3: ALT perforators absent or arising from the oblique or transverse branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery.
A-free-flap-basics.variant4: Superficial or high-origin radial artery.
A-free-flap-basics.variant5: Absent or poor-quality facial vein after level I dissection.
A-free-flap-basics.supply1: RFFF — Radial artery, venae comitantes and cephalic vein; sensate via lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve
A-free-flap-basics.supply2: ALT — Descending branch of lateral circumflex femoral artery (profunda femoris); sensate via lateral femoral cutaneous nerve
A-free-flap-basics.supply3: Fibula — Peroneal artery (from the tibioperoneal trunk) with endosteal and periosteal supply; skin via posterior crural septal perforators
A-free-flap-basics.supply4: Scapula/subscapular system — Circumflex scapular artery (scapular/parascapular), angular branch of thoracodorsal (scapular tip); latissimus via thoracodorsal
A-free-flap-basics.supply5: Recipient — ECA branches (facial, superior thyroid, lingual), transverse cervical artery; IJV system, EJV
A-free-flap-basics.pimp1: Q: What is the pedicle of the ALT flap and its origin? A: Descending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery, from the profunda femoris
A-free-flap-basics.pimp2: Q: Between which muscles does the radial artery run in the forearm? A: Brachioradialis (lateral) and flexor carpi radialis (medial), in the lateral intermuscular septum
A-free-flap-basics.pimp3: Q: What nerve runs under brachioradialis and is at risk with RFFF? A: Superficial branch of the radial nerve
A-free-flap-basics.pimp4: Q: How much distal fibula should be preserved and why? A: At least about 6 cm (commonly cited 6-8 cm) to preserve the ankle mortise/syndesmosis
A-free-flap-basics.pimp5: Q: What is peronea magna? A: A dominant peroneal artery supplying the foot (hypoplastic anterior/posterior tibial arteries); fibula harvest could cause foot ischemia
A-free-flap-basics.pimp6: Q: What is the first anterior branch of the ECA? A: Superior thyroid artery
A-free-flap-basics.pimp7: Q: Which recipient artery is commonly preserved outside of radiation fields after neck dissection? A: Transverse cervical artery (from the thyrocervical trunk) in the lower posterior neck
A-free-flap-basics.pimp8: Q: Which flap uses the angular branch of the thoracodorsal artery? A: Scapular tip flap
OR-free-flap-basics.step1: Define the defect requirements—lining, cover, bone/support, dead space, and function—and select recipient vessels before harvesting the flap.
OR-free-flap-basics.step2: Harvest the chosen flap in its anatomic plane while identifying the arterial/venous pedicle and protecting donor nerves, tendons, and critical structures.
OR-free-flap-basics.step3: Trace the pedicle to obtain adequate caliber and length, then divide it only when the recipient bed and vessels are ready for transfer.
OR-free-flap-basics.step4: Prepare recipient artery and vein to healthy ends with enough mobility for a tension-free course, removing obstructing adventitia without injuring the vessel wall.
OR-free-flap-basics.step5: Position the flap and pedicle first, then perform venous and arterial microvascular anastomoses under magnification with no twist, tension, or size-mismatch problem left uncorrected.
OR-free-flap-basics.step6: Release clamps and assess inflow/outflow by clinical appearance, bleeding, and Doppler; correct vasospasm, kink, compression, or anastomotic defect before final inset.
OR-free-flap-basics.step7: Complete flap inset while protecting the pedicle from mandibular edge, drain, closure, or neck-position compression and close the donor site appropriately.
OR-free-flap-basics.step8: Confirm perfusion and establish a documented flap-monitoring and immediate re-exploration pathway for any postoperative signal or clinical change.

## Free-Flap Compromise — Take-Back / Salvage  (slug: free-flap-takeback, region: Head & Neck Oncology)

A-free-flap-takeback.summary: A failing free flap is rescued by reopening the neck, freeing the pedicle from external compression, and revising thrombosed anastomoses before the flap passes the point of no reflow. Venous failure (congestion, rapid dark bleeding) is more common than arterial failure (pale, cool, no bleeding). The anatomy to master is the pedicle path from flap to recipient vessels, and alternate recipient vessels if the first choice is unusable.
A-free-flap-takeback.layer1: Neck opening — sutures and staples; platysma; hematoma. Open the neck incision fully over the pedicle; evacuate hematoma, which itself can compress the vein.
A-free-flap-takeback.layer2: Pedicle survey — flap artery and vein(s); pedicle path over the mandible or through tunnels; drains. Trace from the flap to the anastomoses looking for twist, kink, tension, compression by drains, tracheostomy ties or bone edges.
A-free-flap-takeback.layer3: Venous anastomosis — recipient vein (IJV tributary, IJV end-to-side, EJV); coupler ring or sutured anastomosis; flap vena comitans. A distended dark flap-side vein with a flat recipient-side vein localizes venous thrombosis at or near the anastomosis. Strip test or direct inspection.
A-free-flap-takeback.layer4: Arterial anastomosis — recipient artery (facial, superior thyroid, lingual, ECA, transverse cervical); flap artery. Check pulsation and Doppler distal to the anastomosis; arterial spasm can be treated with topical vasodilator, while thrombus requires take-down.
A-free-flap-takeback.layer5: Thrombectomy and revision — clot; intima; flap microvasculature. Remove clot (Fogarty catheter or milking), irrigate with heparinized saline, trim back to healthy intima, and re-anastomose. Thrombolytics may be infused into the flap artery with venous effluent drained (not returned to the patient).
A-free-flap-takeback.layer6: Alternate recipient vessels — contralateral facial / superior thyroid; transverse cervical artery and vein; IJV end-to-side; cephalic vein transposition; vein grafts (e.g., saphenous). Choose a new recipient if the original vessel is damaged, has poor inflow, or the geometry is unsafe.
A-free-flap-takeback.layer7: Re-inset and closure — flap; pedicle course; neck skin. Loosen tight inset; consider skin graft or leave the neck partly open rather than closing under tension.
A-free-flap-takeback.landmark1: Pedicle — Follow the flap's vascular hilum (as described in the op note) toward the neck; Doppler along its course (Locates the site of obstruction)
A-free-flap-takeback.landmark2: Venous anastomosis — Usually at the IJV system near the common facial vein or a tributary at the hyoid level, or the EJV on the SCM (Most common thrombosis site)
A-free-flap-takeback.landmark3: Arterial anastomosis — Facial artery at the mandible or level I, or superior thyroid artery just above the carotid bifurcation (Check inflow)
A-free-flap-takeback.landmark4: Transverse cervical vessels — Lower posterior triangle above the clavicle, crossing anterior to the anterior scalene and phrenic nerve, deep to the omohyoid (Alternate recipient outside prior fields)
A-free-flap-takeback.landmark5: IJV — Lateral in the carotid sheath; end-to-side to its anterior wall (Reliable venous outflow)
A-free-flap-takeback.danger1: Carotid artery / IJV — where: Next to the recipient vessels; dissected or irradiated neck; protect: Gentle dissection; control before manipulating vessels; vascular clamps at hand; injury: Hemorrhage
A-free-flap-takeback.danger2: Pedicle itself — where: Throughout the neck; protect: Identify early; do not cut sutures or dissect blindly near the pedicle; injury: Avulsion, flap loss
A-free-flap-takeback.danger3: Hypoglossal, vagus, and marginal mandibular nerves — where: Re-exploration fields in levels I-III; protect: Recognize them in scar; minimal cautery; injury: Tongue weakness, vocal fold paralysis, lip weakness
A-free-flap-takeback.danger4: Phrenic nerve / brachial plexus — where: Deep to transverse cervical vessels in the posterior triangle; protect: Stay superficial to the prevertebral fascia; injury: Hemidiaphragm paralysis, arm weakness
A-free-flap-takeback.danger5: Reperfusion and no-reflow — where: Flap microvasculature after prolonged ischemia; protect: Rapid return to OR; thrombolysis judiciously; injury: Flap loss despite patent anastomoses
A-free-flap-takeback.variant1: Two venous anastomoses (deep and superficial systems); one may remain patent and mask the other's failure.
A-free-flap-takeback.variant2: Buried flaps monitored with implantable Doppler or an external skin paddle.
A-free-flap-takeback.variant3: Recipient vessels altered by prior radiation or neck dissection, requiring the transverse cervical or contralateral vessels.
A-free-flap-takeback.variant4: Hypercoagulable state (e.g., factor V Leiden) causing recurrent thrombosis.
A-free-flap-takeback.supply1: Recipient arteries — Facial, superior thyroid, lingual, ECA trunk, transverse cervical
A-free-flap-takeback.supply2: Recipient veins — IJV and tributaries (common facial, facial, superior thyroid), EJV, cephalic vein (transposed)
A-free-flap-takeback.supply3: Signs of venous failure — Congested, purple, brisk dark bleeding on pinprick, rapid capillary refill
A-free-flap-takeback.supply4: Signs of arterial failure — Pale, cool, no bleeding, slow or absent capillary refill
A-free-flap-takeback.pimp1: Q: Which is more common after head and neck free flaps: arterial or venous thrombosis? A: Venous thrombosis
A-free-flap-takeback.pimp2: Q: How do you differentiate venous from arterial compromise on exam? A: Venous: congested, purple, rapid refill, dark brisk bleeding. Arterial: pale, cool, slow or absent refill, no bleeding
A-free-flap-takeback.pimp3: Q: When do most free-flap thromboses occur? A: Within the first 24-72 hours (most in the first 24 hours)
A-free-flap-takeback.pimp4: Q: What is the no-reflow phenomenon? A: Failure of microcirculatory perfusion after prolonged ischemia despite patent anastomoses, from endothelial swelling and microthrombi
A-free-flap-takeback.pimp5: Q: Where are the transverse cervical vessels found? A: Lower posterior triangle above the clavicle, crossing anterior to the anterior scalene and phrenic nerve, from the thyrocervical trunk
A-free-flap-takeback.pimp6: Q: Name two alternate venous options if the IJV system is unavailable. A: EJV (contralateral) or cephalic vein transposed to the neck; vein graft to a distant vein
A-free-flap-takeback.pimp7: Q: Why is time important in salvage? A: Salvage rates fall with longer ischemia; early take-back gives the best results
OR-free-flap-takeback.step1: Treat suspected vascular compromise as time-critical: reopen the recipient site promptly and expose the pedicle/anastomoses before prolonged diagnostic delay.
OR-free-flap-takeback.step2: Release every potential external cause of obstruction first—tight inset, hematoma, neck position, pedicle twist, kink, or drain compression—and reassess flow.
OR-free-flap-takeback.step3: Inspect arterial inflow and venous outflow separately using clinical bleeding/Doppler and direct vessel appearance to localize the failure.
OR-free-flap-takeback.step4: If thrombosis is present, take down the affected anastomosis, remove clot completely, irrigate the lumen, and trim back to healthy intima rather than sewing through damaged vessel.
OR-free-flap-takeback.step5: Perform thrombectomy and revise the arterial or venous anastomosis; select a new recipient vessel or vein graft when the original geometry or vessel quality remains unsafe.
OR-free-flap-takeback.step6: Use pharmacologic thrombolysis/vasodilator maneuvers only within the team's established salvage protocol and after correcting the mechanical/technical cause.
OR-free-flap-takeback.step7: Reperfuse and observe the flap long enough to confirm sustained inflow/outflow, then revise inset so the repaired pedicle lies in a protected, noncompressed course.
OR-free-flap-takeback.step8: Close loosely enough for monitoring and document the revised vessels, ischemia interval, anticoagulation plan, and a very low threshold for repeat exploration.

## Selective Neck Dissection  (slug: neck-dissection, region: Head & Neck Oncology)

A-neck-dissection.summary: Selective neck dissection removes the nodal levels at risk for a given primary site while preserving the SCM, IJV and spinal accessory nerve. The specimen is the fibrofatty contents enclosed between the investing layer of deep cervical fascia superficially and the carotid sheath and prevertebral (deep) fascia deep. Safety depends on finding CN XI in level II, the hypoglossal nerve deep to the digastric, and keeping the floor of the dissection superficial to the prevertebral fascia where the phrenic nerve and brachial plexus lie.
A-neck-dissection.layer1: Incision and subplatysmal flaps — skin; platysma; external jugular vein and great auricular nerve (deep to platysma, on SCM); marginal mandibular branch of CN VII (deep to platysma, superficial to facial vessels). Raise flaps in the subplatysmal plane to the mandible superiorly, clavicle inferiorly, midline anteriorly and trapezius posteriorly as needed. Identify the marginal branch or use the Hayes Martin maneuver (ligate and reflect the facial vein superiorly).
A-neck-dissection.layer2: Level I (if included) — submental triangle (anterior digastric bellies, hyoid); submandibular gland; facial artery and vein; mylohyoid; lingual nerve and submandibular ganglion; Wharton duct; hypoglossal nerve. Retract the mylohyoid anteriorly to expose the lingual nerve (superior, attached to the ganglion), the duct (middle) and CN XII (inferior, with the ranine veins) on the hyoglossus.
A-neck-dissection.layer3: Release of the fascia along SCM — investing (superficial) layer of deep cervical fascia; anterior border of SCM; great auricular nerve. Incise the fascia along the anterior SCM border and peel it posteriorly off the muscle's deep surface, exposing the posterior extent of levels II-IV.
A-neck-dissection.layer4: Level II and CN XI — posterior belly of digastric (roof); CN XI; IJV; occipital artery; level IIA (anterior/inferior to XI) and IIB (posterior/superior to XI). CN XI crosses the IJV (usually lateral/superficial to it) high in level II, near the level of the C1 transverse process (palpable between the mastoid tip and mandibular angle), then enters the deep surface of the upper SCM. Clear IIB by passing the tissue under the nerve; avoid stretching the nerve.
A-neck-dissection.layer5: Hypoglossal nerve and upper carotid — CN XII looping below the digastric; occipital artery / SCM branch (sling over XII); ranine veins; carotid bifurcation and ECA branches. XII crosses superficial (lateral) to the ICA and ECA just below the digastric, deep to the venous plexus. Bleeding from the ranine veins near XII should be controlled with fine ligatures, not cautery sweeps.
A-neck-dissection.layer6: Levels III-IV along the carotid sheath — IJV; common carotid; vagus (posterior between them); ansa cervicalis (on the sheath); omohyoid (III-IV border); cervical plexus roots (posterior limit). Dissect the packet off the sheath anterior to posterior. Preserve the cervical plexus sensory branches, which mark the posterior limit of a selective dissection; the phrenic nerve arises from C3-C5 deep to the prevertebral fascia.
A-neck-dissection.layer7: Floor of the neck — prevertebral (deep) fascia; phrenic nerve on anterior scalene (lateral to medial descent); brachial plexus trunks between anterior and middle scalenes; transverse cervical artery. Keep the dissection superficial to the deep fascia. The phrenic nerve runs from lateral to medial on the anterior scalene, deep to the fascia.
A-neck-dissection.layer8: Lower level IV and venous angle — thoracic duct (left, arching into the IJV-subclavian junction); right lymphatic duct; transverse cervical vessels; vagus. Clamp and ligate the inferior tissue near the venous angle. Check with Valsalva for chyle before closure.
A-neck-dissection.landmark1: Spinal accessory nerve (level II) — Just inferior to the posterior belly of digastric, crossing superficial to the IJV in most patients near the level of the C1 transverse process; enters the deep surface of the upper third of the SCM. Erb point (posterior SCM, where great auricular wraps) locates it in level V ~1 cm superior (Must be preserved; defines IIA/IIB)
A-neck-dissection.landmark2: Hypoglossal nerve — Just deep and inferior to the posterior belly of digastric, crossing laterally over the ICA and ECA, beneath the ranine veins and the occipital SCM artery sling (Upper anterior limit of the level II dissection)
A-neck-dissection.landmark3: Posterior belly of digastric — Retract the upper SCM; muscle runs from the mastoid notch to the hyoid intermediate tendon ('Resident's friend': structures deep to it (ICA, IJV, XI, XII, X) are protected if you stay inferior and superficial)
A-neck-dissection.landmark4: Phrenic nerve — On the anterior surface of the anterior scalene deep to the prevertebral fascia, running lateral to medial as it descends; stimulation produces diaphragm twitch (Floor of the dissection must stay superficial to it)
A-neck-dissection.landmark5: Omohyoid — Inferior belly crosses the IJV obliquely at about the level of the cricoid (Surgical boundary between levels III and IV and landmark for the IJV)
A-neck-dissection.landmark6: Thoracic duct — Low left neck, posterior to the IJV and carotid, arching anteriorly over the subclavian artery, phrenic nerve and anterior scalene to enter the IJV-subclavian junction (Injury causes chyle leak)
A-neck-dissection.danger1: Spinal accessory nerve — where: Level II crossing the IJV; level V emerging from SCM posterior border toward trapezius; protect: Identify early, minimal skeletonization and traction, avoid cautery near it; injury: Shoulder syndrome: trapezius weakness, shoulder droop, winging, pain
A-neck-dissection.danger2: Hypoglossal nerve — where: Deep to the digastric and ranine veins, crossing the carotid arteries; protect: Control ranine veins with fine ligatures; identify before clamping in upper level II/I; injury: Ipsilateral tongue weakness and atrophy, deviation toward the injured side
A-neck-dissection.danger3: Vagus nerve — where: Posterior in the carotid sheath between the carotid and IJV; protect: Avoid clamping within the sheath; identify before ligating the IJV; injury: Vocal fold paralysis, dysphagia, aspiration
A-neck-dissection.danger4: Phrenic nerve and brachial plexus — where: Deep to the prevertebral fascia on the anterior scalene and between scalenes; protect: Keep the floor superficial to the fascia; preserve cervical roots; injury: Hemidiaphragm paralysis; upper limb weakness
A-neck-dissection.danger5: Thoracic duct — where: Left venous angle (variants on the right or bilateral); protect: Ligate inferior tissue; Valsalva check; injury: Chyle leak / chylous fistula
A-neck-dissection.danger6: Marginal mandibular branch — where: Deep to platysma and superficial to the facial vessels, up to ~1-2 cm below the mandible; protect: Hayes Martin maneuver; dissect deep to the submandibular fascia; injury: Lower lip depressor weakness
A-neck-dissection.danger7: IJV and carotid — where: Medial limit of levels II-IV; protect: Dissect along the vein with sharp technique; ligate small tributaries; injury: Hemorrhage, air embolism (IJV)
A-neck-dissection.variant1: CN XI passes medial (deep) to the IJV in a minority (reported from a few percent up to ~30% across series; 19% in an 84-neck cadaver study) and rarely (~1%) passes through a split or fenestrated IJV.
A-neck-dissection.variant2: Thoracic duct may terminate on the right or bilaterally, as multiple channels.
A-neck-dissection.variant3: High carotid bifurcation (near the angle of the mandible) or low bifurcation.
A-neck-dissection.variant4: Duplicated or fenestrated IJV encircling CN XI.
A-neck-dissection.variant5: Accessory phrenic nerve, often from the nerve to subclavius.
A-neck-dissection.supply1: Arterial — Common carotid → ICA (no neck branches) and ECA (superior thyroid, lingual, facial, ascending pharyngeal, occipital, posterior auricular, maxillary, superficial temporal); transverse cervical and suprascapular from the thyrocervical trunk
A-neck-dissection.supply2: Venous — IJV with facial, lingual and superior/middle thyroid tributaries; EJV superficial on SCM
A-neck-dissection.supply3: Motor nerves — CN XI (SCM, trapezius); CN XII (tongue); ansa cervicalis (straps); phrenic (C3-C5)
A-neck-dissection.supply4: Sensory — Cervical plexus: lesser occipital, great auricular, transverse cervical, supraclavicular nerves emerging at Erb point
A-neck-dissection.supply5: Lymphatics — Jugular chain levels II-IV, thoracic duct (left) and right lymphatic duct to the venous angle
A-neck-dissection.pimp1: Q: What divides level IIA from IIB? A: The spinal accessory nerve (vertical plane through CN XI)
A-neck-dissection.pimp2: Q: What is the boundary between levels III and IV? A: The inferior border of the cricoid cartilage (surgical landmark: omohyoid crossing the IJV)
A-neck-dissection.pimp3: Q: Which levels are removed in a supraomohyoid neck dissection? A: Levels I-III
A-neck-dissection.pimp4: Q: Which levels are removed in a lateral neck dissection for laryngeal cancer? A: Levels II-IV
A-neck-dissection.pimp5: Q: What is Erb point and what nerve is found near it? A: Where the cervical plexus sensory nerves emerge at the posterior SCM border (near the great auricular wrapping around SCM); CN XI is about 1 cm superior
A-neck-dissection.pimp6: Q: What is the 'sling' over the hypoglossal nerve? A: The SCM branch of the occipital artery (with accompanying veins) looping over CN XII
A-neck-dissection.pimp7: Q: Which nerve lies on the anterior scalene? A: Phrenic nerve, deep to the prevertebral fascia, descending lateral to medial
A-neck-dissection.pimp8: Q: Where does the thoracic duct terminate? A: Left venous angle (junction of left IJV and subclavian vein), arching from behind the carotid sheath
A-neck-dissection.pimp9: Q: Which structures are preserved in a modified radical neck dissection type III? A: CN XI, IJV and SCM (all three)
OR-neck-dissection.step1: Raise subplatysmal flaps to the planned superior/inferior limits and define the anterior border of SCM, midline, mandible, and clavicle.
OR-neck-dissection.step2: Identify and preserve the greater auricular nerve when appropriate, then expose the posterior belly of digastric and locate CN XI near its SCM entry or jugular relationship.
OR-neck-dissection.step3: Open the fascia along SCM and mobilize the lymphatic packet medially while preserving the carotid sheath unless oncologic involvement requires sacrifice.
OR-neck-dissection.step4: Clear level II around the upper IJV/CN XI, protecting hypoglossal nerve and avoiding traction injury to the accessory nerve.
OR-neck-dissection.step5: Continue inferiorly through levels III and IV, keeping the specimen on the deep cervical fascia and protecting phrenic nerve/brachial plexus at the floor.
OR-neck-dissection.step6: On the left low neck, identify and control the thoracic duct/major lymphatics before dividing tissue near the venous angle; on the right, remain alert to the right lymphatic duct.
OR-neck-dissection.step7: Clear level V or level I only when included in the planned dissection, with deliberate protection of CN XI, marginal mandibular nerve, hypoglossal/lingual nerves, and submandibular structures as applicable.
OR-neck-dissection.step8: Deliver the specimen by oriented levels, inspect preserved IJV/carotid/vagus/CN XI and the low-neck lymphatic bed, then obtain hemostasis and place drains.

## Oral Cavity Composite Resection  (slug: oral-composite, region: Head & Neck Oncology)

A-oral-composite.summary: A composite resection removes an oral cavity cancer en bloc with the adjacent mandible (marginal or segmental) and floor-of-mouth soft tissue, usually in continuity with the neck dissection. The key relationships are the mylohyoid diaphragm separating the sublingual from the submandibular space, the lingual nerve and Wharton duct crossing in the floor of mouth, the hypoglossal nerve and lingual artery on either side of hyoglossus, and the inferior alveolar nerve inside the mandible.
A-oral-composite.layer1: Neck dissection and vessel control — level I contents (submandibular gland, facial artery and vein); lingual artery origin from ECA; hypoglossal nerve; recipient vessels for free flap. The neck is usually done first: identify XII, the lingual and facial arteries and the recipient vessels, and define the inferior extent of the floor-of-mouth resection from below.
A-oral-composite.layer2: Access — lip-split (midline or stepped) with cheek flap; visor flap (degloving); pull-through beneath the mandible; mental nerve exiting the mental foramen. Choice depends on tumor site and size. Lip-split with mandibulotomy (paramedian, between canine and lateral incisor or in front of the mental foramen) preserves the inferior alveolar nerve when the mandible is not resected.
A-oral-composite.layer3: Mucosal cuts — gingiva and alveolar mucosa; floor-of-mouth mucosa; ventral and lateral tongue; sublingual gland and Wharton duct. Mark margins (commonly ~1 cm clinical margin) with palpation of deep extent. Wharton duct runs superficially in the floor of mouth from the submandibular gland deep lobe to the sublingual caruncle.
A-oral-composite.layer4: Floor of mouth musculature — mylohyoid (diaphragm); geniohyoid; genioglossus; hyoglossus. The mylohyoid attaches to the mylohyoid line on the inner mandible; tumor through it reaches the submandibular space. Hyoglossus is the key layer: lingual artery deep to it, lingual nerve, duct and CN XII superficial.
A-oral-composite.layer5: Lingual nerve and duct crossing — lingual nerve; submandibular ganglion; Wharton duct. The lingual nerve enters the floor of mouth lateral (superficial) to the duct near the third molar, loops under the duct from lateral to medial, then rises into the tongue. Sacrifice if involved; send the proximal margin for perineural tumors.
A-oral-composite.layer6: Mandibulectomy — periosteum; cortex and cancellous bone; inferior alveolar canal and nerve; mental foramen; teeth roots. Marginal mandibulectomy requires enough residual height (commonly cited at least ~1 cm of residual inferior mandible; some advise 1.5 cm or more, especially if irradiated or atrophic) and no medullary invasion; segmental resection for gross bone invasion or irradiated/edentulous thin mandible. Inferior alveolar nerve margin is sent when invasion is present.
A-oral-composite.layer7: Deep release and specimen — genial tubercles (genioglossus/geniohyoid attachments); lingual artery branches (deep lingual, sublingual); deep tongue musculature. Release of genial muscles in anterior resections destabilizes tongue support (airway risk); a tracheostomy is often needed.
A-oral-composite.layer8: Reconstruction — recipient artery (facial, superior thyroid) and vein; free flap (RFFF, ALT, fibula); mandible plate. Restore separation of the oral cavity from the neck with watertight closure; reconstruct segmental defects with vascularized bone when possible.
A-oral-composite.landmark1: Hypoglossal nerve (neck) — In the submandibular triangle, retract the digastric tendon and mylohyoid; the nerve lies on hyoglossus beneath the ranine veins, inferior to the duct and lingual nerve (Protects tongue motor function and guides deep resection)
A-oral-composite.landmark2: Lingual nerve (neck) — Retract mylohyoid anteriorly and pull the submandibular gland down; the nerve is the superior structure, tethered to the gland by the submandibular ganglion (Identify and protect or sample margin)
A-oral-composite.landmark3: Lingual artery — Arises from the ECA at the level of the greater hyoid cornu, passes deep to hyoglossus above the hyoid (Ligation in the neck controls tongue and floor of mouth bleeding)
A-oral-composite.landmark4: Mental foramen — Most often below the second premolar or between the first and second premolars, roughly midway between alveolar crest and inferior border in dentate patients (Mental nerve preservation; mandibulotomy site anterior to it)
A-oral-composite.landmark5: Mylohyoid line — Inner mandibular ridge running from below the third molar obliquely toward the symphysis (Mylohyoid attachment; tumor crossing it enters the submandibular space)
A-oral-composite.landmark6: Wharton duct — Cannulate the punctum at the sublingual caruncle beside the frenulum; trace it posterolaterally along the floor of mouth (Protect or reimplant ductal outflow; sign of deep extent)
A-oral-composite.danger1: Hypoglossal nerve — where: On lateral surface of hyoglossus, deep in the floor of mouth / tongue base; protect: Identify in the neck first and keep in view from below; injury: Tongue paralysis, dysarthria, dysphagia (bilateral: airway obstruction)
A-oral-composite.danger2: Lingual nerve — where: Floor of mouth crossing Wharton duct; lingual to the third molar against the mandible; protect: Identify and preserve when not involved; frozen margin if perineural invasion; injury: Tongue anesthesia and taste loss (anterior two-thirds)
A-oral-composite.danger3: Lingual artery — where: Deep to hyoglossus; protect: Ligate in the neck or control under vision in the resection; injury: Hemorrhage, tongue ischemia if bilateral
A-oral-composite.danger4: Inferior alveolar nerve and mandible — where: Within the mandibular canal from lingula to mental foramen; protect: Mandibulotomy anterior to the mental foramen; marginal mandibulectomy above the canal; injury: Lip and chin numbness; pathologic fracture if marginal resection too deep
A-oral-composite.danger5: Marginal mandibular nerve — where: Neck flaps over the facial vessels; protect: Subplatysmal flap with Hayes Martin maneuver; injury: Lower lip weakness
A-oral-composite.danger6: Airway — where: Tongue base loses support after genial muscle release; edema; flap bulk; protect: Plan tracheostomy for anterior / extensive resections; injury: Postoperative obstruction
A-oral-composite.variant1: Mylohyoid boutonniere (gaps in the mylohyoid) allowing sublingual tissue or tumor to herniate into the submandibular space.
A-oral-composite.variant2: Edentulous atrophic mandible with the canal near the alveolar crest, precluding marginal mandibulectomy.
A-oral-composite.variant3: Lingual artery arising from a common linguofacial trunk.
A-oral-composite.variant4: Accessory mental foramina.
A-oral-composite.supply1: Arterial — Lingual artery (deep lingual, sublingual, dorsal lingual branches); facial artery (submental branch); inferior alveolar artery (maxillary) in the mandible
A-oral-composite.supply2: Sensory — Lingual nerve (V3) anterior two-thirds of tongue and floor of mouth, carrying chorda tympani taste fibers; inferior alveolar / mental nerve (V3) to teeth, lower lip and chin
A-oral-composite.supply3: Motor — CN XII to intrinsic and extrinsic tongue muscles (except palatoglossus); nerve to mylohyoid (V3) to mylohyoid and anterior digastric; C1 via XII to geniohyoid
A-oral-composite.supply4: Lymphatics — Floor of mouth and anterior tongue to levels I-III; tip of tongue to submental and possibly level III-IV; midline lesions drain bilaterally
A-oral-composite.pimp1: Q: Describe the relationship of the lingual nerve to Wharton duct. A: Lingual nerve starts lateral and superior, loops under the duct from lateral to medial, then ascends medial to it into the tongue
A-oral-composite.pimp2: Q: Which structures lie superficial versus deep to hyoglossus? A: Superficial: lingual nerve, submandibular duct, CN XII. Deep: lingual artery, glossopharyngeal nerve, stylohyoid ligament
A-oral-composite.pimp3: Q: What innervates the mylohyoid? A: Nerve to mylohyoid, a branch of the inferior alveolar nerve (V3)
A-oral-composite.pimp4: Q: What is the required residual mandible height after marginal mandibulectomy? A: Commonly cited as at least about 1 cm of residual inferior mandibular height (more, often 1.5 cm, in the irradiated or atrophic mandible)
A-oral-composite.pimp5: Q: Where is the mental foramen? A: Most often below the second premolar or between the premolars, about midway in mandibular height in dentate adults
A-oral-composite.pimp6: Q: How does SCC typically enter the dentate versus edentulous mandible? A: Dentate: through the dental sockets (occlusal ridge). Edentulous: through the occlusal ridge cortex/pores, sometimes the canal
A-oral-composite.pimp7: Q: What depth-of-invasion threshold changes oral cavity T stage in AJCC 8? A: DOI >5 mm moves T1 to T2, >10 mm to T3 (for tumors in the relevant size categories)
A-oral-composite.pimp8: Q: Why does anterior floor-of-mouth resection threaten the airway? A: Releasing genioglossus/geniohyoid from the genial tubercles removes tongue base support, allowing posterior collapse
OR-oral-composite.step1: Perform the planned neck exposure/dissection first when needed to control vessels, identify recipient vessels, and define the deep oncologic boundary.
OR-oral-composite.step2: Mark mucosal margins around the oral cavity primary and identify the relationship to mandible, floor of mouth, tongue musculature, lingual/hypoglossal nerves, and Wharton duct.
OR-oral-composite.step3: Make mucosal and soft-tissue cuts around the tumor, maintaining specimen orientation and controlling lingual/facial vessels as encountered.
OR-oral-composite.step4: Perform marginal or segmental mandibulectomy at oncologically appropriate bony margins when bone is involved or required for access.
OR-oral-composite.step5: Release the deep tongue/floor-of-mouth attachments while protecting uninvolved carotid-space and nerve structures.
OR-oral-composite.step6: Deliver the specimen en bloc, orient it, and obtain mucosal/deep/bony margins as indicated.
OR-oral-composite.step7: Prepare the defect and recipient vessels, then reconstruct lining, mandibular continuity, tongue/floor volume, and external cover according to the defect.
OR-oral-composite.step8: Confirm airway, flap perfusion, hemostasis, occlusion when relevant, and drain/feeding access before closure.

## Pharyngocutaneous Fistula / Salivary Leak Management  (slug: pharyngocutaneous-fistula, region: Head & Neck Oncology)

A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.summary: A pharyngocutaneous fistula is a breakdown of the neopharyngeal closure after laryngectomy (or other pharyngeal repair) that lets saliva track through the neck to the skin. The critical anatomy is the course of the tract relative to the carotid sheath, because saliva bathing an irradiated carotid causes blowout; repair requires debridement to a vascular bed, watertight mucosal closure and coverage with vascularized tissue such as pectoralis major.
A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.layer1: Assessment of the tract — cutaneous opening (often near the stoma or upper neck); tract through subcutaneous tissue; neopharyngeal defect (often at the closure T-junction or tongue base). Barium swallow or CT defines the internal opening and track. The T-junction of a T-closure (where the horizontal tongue-base limb meets the vertical limb) and the tongue base suture line are classic weak points.
A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.layer2: Opening and debridement — skin flaps; platysma; necrotic subcutaneous tissue and fascia. Open the wound widely enough to drain all pockets; debride to bleeding tissue.
A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.layer3: Carotid sheath — common carotid artery; IJV; vagus; carotid bulb. Determine whether the carotid is exposed or bathed in saliva. Exposed vessels need immediate vascularized coverage; a sentinel bleed is an emergency.
A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.layer4: Pharyngeal defect — neopharyngeal mucosa; residual constrictor muscle; tongue base; esophageal inlet. Mobilize mucosa to allow a tension-free watertight closure; if tissue is poor or the defect large, patch with a flap.
A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.layer5: Vascularized coverage: pectoralis major — thoracoacromial artery pectoral branch (on the deep surface); lateral pectoral nerve; clavicle (pivot); deltopectoral groove and cephalic vein. The pedicle runs on the undersurface of the muscle, descending from about the midclavicle and then curving medially along the acromion-to-xiphoid line. Tunnel the flap over the clavicle into the neck to cover the carotid and buttress the repair.
A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.layer6: Salivary diversion — salivary bypass tube; controlled fistula (pharyngostome) if needed; nasogastric or gastrostomy feeding route. Keep saliva away from the great vessels; drains positioned away from the carotid and flap pedicles.
A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.landmark1: Carotid artery — Lateral to the neopharynx deep to the SCM (or its absence after radical dissection); palpable pulse beneath the fistula bed (Determines urgency of coverage)
A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.landmark2: Internal fistula opening — Methylene blue or water swallowed/instilled through the mouth; endoscopy at the closure lines (T-junction, tongue base) (Target for closure)
A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.landmark3: Thoracoacromial pedicle (PMMF) — Draw a line from the acromion to the xiphoid and a perpendicular from the midclavicle; the pedicle descends along the perpendicular on the muscle's deep surface, then follows the acromion-xiphoid line (Workhorse vascularized coverage)
A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.landmark4: Stoma and trachea — Inferior to the closure; fistula tract may track toward the stoma (Protect the airway from saliva and flap bulk)
A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.landmark5: Free-flap pedicle (if prior flap) — Review prior operative note; Doppler signal in the neck (Avoid injury during debridement)
A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.danger1: Carotid artery — where: Adjacent to the fistula tract, especially after radiation and neck dissection; protect: Early vascularized coverage (pectoralis or free flap); divert saliva; plan endovascular backup for sentinel bleeds; injury: Carotid blowout (high mortality, stroke)
A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.danger2: IJV — where: Lateral to the carotid; protect: Gentle debridement near the sheath; injury: Hemorrhage, thrombosis
A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.danger3: Free-flap or PMMF pedicle — where: Neck tunnels and near the mandible or clavicle; protect: Identify and protect; avoid compression by drains or dressings; injury: Flap loss
A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.danger4: Airway — where: Stoma inferior to the fistula; protect: Stoma care and cuffed tube if saliva soils the airway; injury: Aspiration pneumonia
A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.danger5: Neopharyngeal lumen — where: Closure line; protect: Avoid excessive mucosal resection; patch rather than narrow; injury: Stricture and dysphagia

A-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.variant2: Hypothyroidism and malnutrition (risk factors with anatomic healing effects).

OR-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.step2: Open/drain infected or necrotic tissue adequately and debride nonviable wound edges until a healthy vascularized bed is present.
OR-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.step3: Identify and protect great vessels and reconstructive pedicles; obtain vascularized coverage urgently if carotid exposure is present.

OR-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.step6: Separate salivary tract from neck skin/great vessels and obliterate dead space without compressing flap pedicles.
OR-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.step7: Place drains away from exposed vessels/anastomoses and confirm hemostasis before external closure.
OR-pharyngocutaneous-fistula.step8: Maintain enteral nutrition away from the repair and define postoperative wound, leak-testing, antibiotic, and carotid-bleed emergency plans.

## TORS Tonsil / Base-of-Tongue Resection  (slug: tors, region: Head & Neck Oncology)

A-tors.summary: TORS removes oropharyngeal cancer from inside out, so the surgeon meets the anatomy in reverse of a cervical approach: mucosa, then constrictors, then the parapharyngeal fat that contains the carotid and its branches. For radical tonsillectomy the superior constrictor is the deep margin and the ICA lies just beyond the parapharyngeal fat; for tongue base resection the lingual artery and hypoglossal nerve run deep and lateral near the hyoid level.
A-tors.layer1: Exposure — teeth / gums; lips; tongue; endotracheal tube (contralateral). Use a retractor (FK, Crowe-Davis or similar) to center the target. Protect dentition and check tongue ischemia time.
A-tors.layer2: Mucosal incisions (radical tonsillectomy) — anterior pillar (palatoglossus); soft palate; posterior pillar (palatopharyngeus); glossotonsillar sulcus; pterygomandibular raphe. Incise lateral to the anterior pillar along the pterygomandibular raphe, superiorly into the soft palate and inferiorly to the glossotonsillar sulcus.
A-tors.layer3: Superior constrictor (deep margin) — superior constrictor; buccopharyngeal fascia on its lateral surface. In radical tonsillectomy the constrictor is taken with the specimen. Lateral to it, the buccopharyngeal fascia separates the pharynx from parapharyngeal fat.
A-tors.layer4: Parapharyngeal fat — prestyloid parapharyngeal fat; medial pterygoid (lateral); styloglossus and stylopharyngeus; glossopharyngeal nerve (along stylopharyngeus). The medial pterygoid lies lateral; stay medial to it. Styloglossus runs anteroinferiorly into the tongue; the glossopharyngeal nerve follows stylopharyngeus toward the tongue base.
A-tors.layer5: Arterial branches — tonsillar branch of facial artery; ascending palatine artery; ascending pharyngeal artery; lingual artery dorsal branches; ICA (posterolateral, beyond the fat). Clip named branches (both sides of the vessel to be divided) before transection. A pulsatile structure posterolateral in the fat may be the ICA, especially if retropharyngeally displaced; stop and reassess.
A-tors.layer6: Tongue base resection — lingual tonsil; intrinsic and extrinsic tongue muscle (genioglossus, hyoglossus); vallecula; hyoid and pharyngoepiglottic fold. Carry the inferior cut to the vallecula. Deep and lateral, the lingual artery lies medial to hyoglossus near the greater cornu, with CN XII lateral to hyoglossus.
A-tors.layer7: Bed inspection and cervical ligation — exposed parapharyngeal fat or vessels; transcervical ECA branch ligation (lingual, facial, ascending pharyngeal). Many surgeons ligate feeding ECA branches through the neck dissection (often staged or concurrent) to reduce severe postoperative hemorrhage.
A-tors.landmark1: Pterygomandibular raphe — Palpable band from hamulus to the posterior mylohyoid line, lateral to the anterior tonsillar pillar (Lateral mucosal incision for radical tonsillectomy; buccinator in front, superior constrictor behind)
A-tors.landmark2: Medial pterygoid muscle — Lateral to the superior constrictor and parapharyngeal fat; vertical fibers running toward the mandibular angle (Lateral margin of the tonsil resection; trismus if transgressed)
A-tors.landmark3: Styloglossus / stylopharyngeus — Muscular bands crossing the parapharyngeal fat from posterosuperior (styloid) to the tongue and pharynx (Glossopharyngeal nerve runs on stylopharyngeus; the ICA lies just posterior (deep) to the styloid muscles)
A-tors.landmark4: Lingual artery at tongue base — Deep in the lateral tongue base, near the level of the greater hyoid cornu, deep (medial) to hyoglossus in the lateral part of the tongue base (Major bleeding source; clip before transection)
A-tors.landmark5: Hyoid / vallecula — Inferior limit of the tongue base cut; palpate the hyoid transcervically or see the vallecula at the epiglottis tip (Defines inferior margin and protects supraglottis)
A-tors.danger1: Internal carotid artery — where: Posterolateral to the tonsil fossa, beyond the parapharyngeal fat and posterior to the styloid muscles; classically cited ~2-2.5 cm in adults but can be under 1 cm, especially if medialized (retropharyngeal course) or tortuous; protect: Preoperative imaging; stay medial to the styloid musculature; avoid deep energy dissection in fat; injury: Catastrophic hemorrhage, stroke
A-tors.danger2: Lingual and facial artery branches — where: Tongue base (lingual) and tonsil bed (tonsillar branch of facial, ascending palatine); protect: Clip before cutting; consider transcervical ligation; injury: Postoperative hemorrhage (can be fatal through the airway)
A-tors.danger3: Hypoglossal nerve — where: Lateral to hyoglossus in deep lateral tongue base; protect: Avoid deep lateral extension beyond hyoglossus unless oncologically needed; injury: Tongue weakness, dysphagia
A-tors.danger4: Glossopharyngeal nerve — where: On stylopharyngeus in the lateral tonsil bed / tongue base; protect: Preserve when uninvolved; injury: Taste and sensory loss posterior tongue, dysphagia
A-tors.danger5: Lingual nerve — where: Lateral tongue / floor of mouth anterior to the tonsil; protect: Avoid extending anterior-lateral cuts into the floor of mouth; injury: Ipsilateral tongue numbness and taste loss (anterior two-thirds)
A-tors.danger6: Velopharyngeal function — where: Soft palate resection extent; protect: Limit palate resection; consider reconstruction; injury: Velopharyngeal insufficiency
A-tors.variant1: Retropharyngeal (medialized) or tortuous ICA, a classic contraindication or warning for TORS radical tonsillectomy.
A-tors.variant2: Kinking or coiling of the ICA.
A-tors.variant3: Common lingual-facial trunk from the ECA.
A-tors.variant4: Prominent or large lingual tonsil tissue obscuring tumor margins.
A-tors.supply1: Tonsil arterial supply — Tonsillar branch of facial (main); ascending palatine (facial); dorsal lingual (lingual); ascending pharyngeal; descending palatine (maxillary)
A-tors.supply2: Tongue base supply — Lingual artery (deep to hyoglossus) and its dorsal lingual branches
A-tors.supply3: Sensory — CN IX to tonsil and posterior third of tongue; internal SLN to vallecula
A-tors.supply4: Motor — Pharyngeal plexus (X) to constrictors and palatal muscles (except tensor veli palatini, V3); CN XII to tongue (except palatoglossus, X)
A-tors.pimp1: Q: What is the main arterial supply of the palatine tonsil? A: Tonsillar branch of the facial artery (entering the inferior pole)
A-tors.pimp2: Q: Which muscle forms the bed (deep margin) of the tonsil? A: Superior constrictor
A-tors.pimp3: Q: What lies lateral to the superior constrictor at the tonsil? A: Buccopharyngeal fascia, then parapharyngeal fat, medial pterygoid, and posterolaterally the styloid muscles and ICA
A-tors.pimp4: Q: Which nerve runs with stylopharyngeus? A: Glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX), which also supplies stylopharyngeus (its only motor target)
A-tors.pimp5: Q: Where does the lingual artery run relative to hyoglossus? The hypoglossal nerve? A: Lingual artery deep (medial) to hyoglossus; CN XII superficial (lateral) to it
A-tors.pimp6: Q: What extends between the hamulus and the mandible? A: Pterygomandibular raphe, joining buccinator and superior constrictor
A-tors.pimp7: Q: What carotid finding is a red flag for TORS radical tonsillectomy? A: A medialized (retropharyngeal) ICA close to the tonsil fossa
A-tors.pimp8: Q: Which palatal muscle is not innervated by the pharyngeal plexus? A: Tensor veli palatini (V3)
OR-tors.step1: Obtain stable transoral exposure with the target centered and protect teeth, tongue, lips, and endotracheal tube before docking.
OR-tors.step2: Identify the tumor margins and deep muscular plane, then plan mucosal cuts with orientation to lingual artery, glossopharyngeal nerve, carotid/parapharyngeal space, and mandible.
OR-tors.step3: Make the mucosal incision around the lesion and develop the deep plane under three-dimensional visualization, maintaining an intact specimen when possible.
OR-tors.step4: Control named or sizable arterial branches before transection rather than relying on delayed postoperative hemostasis.
OR-tors.step5: Complete the superior/inferior/deep cuts with adequate oncologic margin while preserving uninvolved functional tongue-base/pharyngeal tissue.
OR-tors.step6: Remove and orient the specimen and send additional margins from the patient bed when indicated.
OR-tors.step7: Inspect the deep bed for exposed carotid/parapharyngeal structures and secure meticulous hemostasis with Valsalva.
OR-tors.step8: Determine whether the defect needs secondary-intention healing, local flap, or free-flap reconstruction and set the postoperative airway/bleeding plan.

## Total Laryngectomy  (slug: total-laryngectomy, region: Head & Neck Oncology)

A-total-laryngectomy.summary: Total laryngectomy removes the larynx from hyoid to upper trachea, permanently separating the airway (end tracheostoma) from the pharynx, which is closed to restore swallowing. The larynx is freed from the constrictors laterally, the tongue base superiorly and the cervical esophagus posteriorly; the carotid sheaths lie just lateral, and the superior laryngeal pedicles and thyroid/parathyroid tissue must be managed at the sides.
A-total-laryngectomy.layer1: Apron flap — skin; platysma; anterior jugular veins; investing fascia. Raise subplatysmal flaps from above the hyoid to the clavicles. Incorporate or plan a separate stoma site.
A-total-laryngectomy.layer2: Neck dissection and lateral release — SCM; carotid sheath (CCA, IJV, vagus); omohyoid. Neck dissection (when indicated) exposes the carotid sheath, which is retracted laterally to show the larynx and pharyngeal constrictors.
A-total-laryngectomy.layer3: Strap muscles and thyroid — sternohyoid, sternothyroid, thyrohyoid, omohyoid; thyroid isthmus and lobes; superior and inferior thyroid vessels; parathyroids. Straps are divided low and kept on the specimen if tumor extends anteriorly. Divide the isthmus; keep the contralateral (or both) lobes with their parathyroids when oncologically safe, or remove the ipsilateral lobe for subglottic or transglottic extension.
A-total-laryngectomy.layer4: Superior laryngeal neurovascular bundles — superior laryngeal artery (from superior thyroid artery); internal branch of SLN; thyrohyoid membrane. The bundle pierces the thyrohyoid membrane posterolaterally, near the tip of the greater hyoid cornu. Ligate the artery as it enters the membrane.
A-total-laryngectomy.layer5: Hyoid and suprahyoid release — suprahyoid muscles (mylohyoid, geniohyoid, hyoglossus, digastric tendon sling and stylohyoid insertion); greater cornua; hypoglossal nerve and lingual artery above the greater cornu. Release the suprahyoid attachments along the hyoid body, staying on the bone. Near the greater cornu, CN XII and the lingual artery are superior and lateral; skeletonize the cornu closely.
A-total-laryngectomy.layer6: Constrictor release and pyriform mucosa — inferior constrictor attachment to the oblique line of thyroid cartilage; outer perichondrium; pyriform sinus mucosa (medial to the thyroid ala). Incise the constrictor along the posterior thyroid ala and elevate the pyriform sinus mucosa off the inner ala on the less-involved side to save mucosa for closure.
A-total-laryngectomy.layer7: Tracheal division and airway — trachea (2nd-4th rings typically); tracheoesophageal party wall; RLNs (sacrificed with specimen); esophagus. Divide the trachea below tumor with adequate margin (bevelled for a larger stoma) and transfer the airway. Separate the posterior tracheal wall from the esophagus.
A-total-laryngectomy.layer8: Pharyngeal entry and specimen removal — vallecula (suprahyoid entry); epiglottis; aryepiglottic folds; postcricoid mucosa; cervical esophagus. Enter the pharynx at the site farthest from tumor (vallecula, or pyriform on the opposite side) and cut mucosa under vision around the larynx, finishing at the postcricoid region.
A-total-laryngectomy.layer9: Pharyngeal closure and stoma — pharyngeal mucosa; constrictor muscle (second layer); cricopharyngeus myotomy; tracheostoma to skin. Close mucosa in a T or straight-line pattern; reinforce with constrictors or a pectoralis/free flap in salvage. Myotomy or pharyngeal plexus neurectomy reduces spasm affecting TE speech.
A-total-laryngectomy.landmark1: Superior laryngeal neurovascular bundle — Posterolateral thyrohyoid membrane, just anteroinferior to the tip of the greater hyoid cornu, deep to the thyrohyoid (Ligation controls the main laryngeal arterial supply)
A-total-laryngectomy.landmark2: Hypoglossal nerve — Superior to the greater cornu of the hyoid, running anteriorly on the hyoglossus with the lingual veins; lingual artery runs deep to hyoglossus (Must be protected during hyoid release)
A-total-laryngectomy.landmark3: Pyriform sinus — Lies medial to the thyroid ala posteriorly; after dividing the constrictor along the posterior ala, elevate the perichondrium to see mucosa on the inner ala (Mucosa saved here permits primary closure)
A-total-laryngectomy.landmark4: Vallecula — Directly above the hyoid body behind the tongue base; enter by dividing tissue superior to the hyoid and pulling the epiglottis forward (Usual tumor-free pharyngeal entry for glottic cancers)
A-total-laryngectomy.landmark5: Carotid sheath — Lateral to the thyroid ala and constrictors; retract SCM and identify the CCA pulse (Lateral limit and retracted throughout the release)
A-total-laryngectomy.landmark6: Esophagus — Posterior to the trachea, separated by the party wall; mobilize from below upward after tracheal division (Forms the lower closure and TEP site)
A-total-laryngectomy.danger1: Carotid artery — where: Immediately lateral to the larynx and pharynx; exposed in salvage necks; protect: Keep sheath retracted and covered; cover with vascularized tissue (pectoralis) in irradiated necks or if fistula likely; injury: Hemorrhage; delayed blowout with fistula
A-total-laryngectomy.danger2: Hypoglossal nerve and lingual artery — where: Above the greater cornu of the hyoid; protect: Stay on the hyoid bone during suprahyoid release; injury: Tongue weakness; bleeding
A-total-laryngectomy.danger3: Parathyroids and residual thyroid — where: Behind the thyroid lobes, on the inferior thyroid artery; protect: Preserve uninvolved lobe and its pedicle; autotransplant if devascularized; injury: Hypoparathyroidism and hypothyroidism (also from radiation)
A-total-laryngectomy.danger4: Pharyngeal mucosa — where: Pyriform sinuses and tongue base mucosa needed for closure; protect: Conserve uninvolved mucosa; tension-free closure (vascularized tissue if inadequate); injury: Pharyngocutaneous fistula, stricture
A-total-laryngectomy.danger5: Thoracic duct / mediastinal vessels — where: Low neck during paratracheal dissection; innominate artery anterior to the trachea low in the neck; protect: Ligate low tissue; keep stoma away from the innominate artery; injury: Chyle leak; innominate artery erosion
A-total-laryngectomy.variant1: High-riding innominate artery crossing the trachea in the low neck (relevant to stoma and tracheal division level).
A-total-laryngectomy.variant2: Tumor extension to the pre-epiglottic or paraglottic space or through the cricothyroid membrane, changing strap, thyroid and skin margins.
A-total-laryngectomy.variant3: Laryngocele through the thyrohyoid membrane at the SLN entry.
A-total-laryngectomy.variant4: Nonrecurrent right laryngeal nerve (relevant if a hemithyroid is preserved).
A-total-laryngectomy.supply1: Arterial supply — Superior laryngeal artery (from superior thyroid) through the thyrohyoid membrane; cricothyroid artery; inferior laryngeal artery (from inferior thyroid) entering with the RLN
A-total-laryngectomy.supply2: Sensory — Internal branch of SLN above the vocal folds; RLN below
A-total-laryngectomy.supply3: Motor — RLN to intrinsic muscles except cricothyroid; EBSLN to cricothyroid; pharyngeal plexus (X) to constrictors
A-total-laryngectomy.supply4: Lymphatics — Supraglottis rich, bilateral to levels II-III; glottis sparse; subglottis to prelaryngeal, pretracheal and paratracheal nodes
A-total-laryngectomy.pimp1: Q: Where does the superior laryngeal neurovascular bundle enter the larynx? A: Through the thyrohyoid membrane, posterolaterally, inferior to the greater cornu of the hyoid
A-total-laryngectomy.pimp2: Q: What are the boundaries of the pre-epiglottic space? A: Hyoepiglottic ligament superiorly, thyrohyoid membrane and thyroid cartilage anteriorly, epiglottis and thyroepiglottic ligament posteriorly
A-total-laryngectomy.pimp3: Q: What is Broyles ligament? A: The anterior commissure tendon inserting on the inner thyroid cartilage; a route for cartilage invasion
A-total-laryngectomy.pimp4: Q: Why perform a cricopharyngeal myotomy at laryngectomy? A: To reduce pharyngoesophageal spasm and improve tracheoesophageal speech and swallowing
A-total-laryngectomy.pimp5: Q: What muscle forms most of the pharyngoesophageal segment? A: Cricopharyngeus (lowest part of inferior constrictor) and upper esophageal muscle
A-total-laryngectomy.pimp6: Q: Main risk of an inferiorly placed stoma? A: Proximity to the innominate artery (erosion/hemorrhage) and difficulty with stoma care
A-total-laryngectomy.pimp7: Q: Which thyroid lobe is usually removed in transglottic or subglottic cancer? A: The ipsilateral lobe (direct extension and paratracheal nodal risk)
A-total-laryngectomy.pimp8: Q: Which lymphatic region of the larynx has the poorest drainage? A: The glottis (true vocal folds)
OR-total-laryngectomy.step1: Raise apron/neck flaps and perform the planned neck dissection or central exposure while preserving recipient vessels and uninvolved cranial nerves needed for reconstruction.
OR-total-laryngectomy.step2: Mobilize the larynx by releasing strap/thyroid attachments and identify/protect carotid sheaths, hypoglossal/vagus structures, and viable parathyroid tissue when thyroid is partially preserved.
OR-total-laryngectomy.step3: Divide the trachea below tumor with adequate margin, mature/control the distal tracheal airway, and transition ventilation through the neck.
OR-total-laryngectomy.step4: Enter the pharynx at a tumor-safe site and perform circumferential mucosal cuts under direct visualization to obtain oncologic margins.
OR-total-laryngectomy.step5: Release the larynx from the tongue base/hypopharynx and remove the specimen en bloc with indicated thyroid/pharyngeal components.
OR-total-laryngectomy.step6: Send/confirm margins as indicated and perform primary TEP at this stage only when planned and oncologically/reconstructively appropriate.
OR-total-laryngectomy.step7: Close the pharynx with a watertight tension-free closure or inset flap reconstruction, then test the closure as appropriate.
OR-total-laryngectomy.step8: Create a wide permanent tracheostoma without tension, secure drains/flap monitoring, and remember the patient can thereafter be ventilated only through the neck stoma.

## Transoral Laser Microsurgery — Laryngeal Cancer  (slug: transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer, region: Head & Neck Oncology)

A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.summary: Transoral laser microsurgery resects laryngeal cancer from inside the airway under microscopy, cutting through tumor when needed to read its depth. Resection depth follows the vocal fold layers (epithelium, superficial lamina propria, vocal ligament, thyroarytenoid muscle, paraglottic space, inner thyroid perichondrium) and is classified by the European Laryngological Society cordectomy types: I subepithelial, II subligamental, III transmuscular, IV total, V extended (Va-Vd) and VI anterior commissure. The anterior commissure (Broyles ligament) and the paraglottic space are the boundaries where tumor escapes.
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.layer1: Exposure — teeth / tooth guard; oropharynx; supraglottis; glottis and anterior commissure. Choose the largest laryngoscope allowing complete view, including the anterior commissure; external laryngeal pressure helps.
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.layer2: Supraglottic structures — epiglottis (suprahyoid and infrahyoid); aryepiglottic folds; false vocal folds; ventricle. False fold resection (ventriculectomy) improves exposure of the true fold and lateral extent.
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.layer3: Vocal fold epithelium and superficial lamina propria — stratified squamous epithelium; basement membrane; superficial lamina propria (Reinke space). ELS type I (subepithelial) cordectomy removes epithelium only; type II (subligamental) also removes Reinke space and the vocal ligament, cutting between the ligament and the vocalis muscle.
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.layer4: Vocal ligament and muscle — intermediate and deep lamina propria (vocal ligament); thyroarytenoid (vocalis) muscle. Type III (transmuscular) cuts through the TA muscle; type IV (total) extends to the inner perichondrium of the thyroid cartilage.
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.layer5: Paraglottic space and extension — paraglottic fat; inner thyroid perichondrium; false fold, arytenoid, subglottis, anterior commissure. Type V cordectomies extend to the contralateral fold (Va), arytenoid (Vb), subglottis (Vc) or ventricle/false fold (Vd); type VI includes the anterior commissure.
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.layer6: Anterior commissure and subglottis — Broyles ligament; thyroid cartilage at the commissure; conus elasticus; cricothyroid membrane. At the anterior commissure there is no inner perichondrium where the tendon inserts; tumor here may invade cartilage. Subglottic extension tracks along the conus elasticus.
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.layer7: Supraglottic TLM — pre-epiglottic space (through the epiglottis or hyoepiglottic ligament); superior laryngeal artery branches at the AE fold / lateral pharyngoepiglottic fold. Clip the superior laryngeal artery branches during lateral supraglottic resection to prevent postoperative bleeding.
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.landmark1: Anterior commissure — Point where the two true folds meet anteriorly; exposed with the laryngoscope tip in the petiole region plus external pressure (Critical margin and cartilage-invasion risk zone)
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.landmark2: Vocal process of arytenoid — Posterior end of the membranous vocal fold, felt as a firm point on palpation (Posterior limit of the membranous fold; arytenoid preservation)
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.landmark3: Ventricle — Recess between the true and false folds; open by resecting the false fold (Exposes the superior surface of the true fold and lateral tumor extent)
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.landmark4: Inner thyroid perichondrium — Pale fibrous plane lateral to the TA muscle and paraglottic fat (Deep limit of a type IV cordectomy)
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.landmark5: Superior laryngeal artery branches — Lateral to the AE fold / pharyngoepiglottic fold region in supraglottic resections (Major bleeding source requiring clips)
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.danger1: Airway fire triad — where: Laser, oxygen and endotracheal tube in the airway; protect: Laser-safe tube, low FiO2 (commonly below 30%), saline-filled cuff, wet pledgets, team time-out; injury: Airway fire and burn
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.danger2: Anterior commissure — where: Broyles ligament inserting into the thyroid cartilage; protect: Exposure adequate for full visualization; frozen or careful margin assessment; injury: Recurrence through cartilage; anterior web if both sides denuded
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.danger3: Superior laryngeal artery branches — where: Supraglottic lateral resections; protect: Clip vessels before they retract; injury: Postoperative hemorrhage, airway compromise
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.danger4: Arytenoid / cricoarytenoid joint — where: Posterior glottis; protect: Preserve when not involved; injury: Aspiration, dysphonia, posterior glottic stenosis
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.danger5: Deep thermal injury — where: Thyroid cartilage, vocal ligament; protect: Use the lowest effective laser energy, avoid char; injury: Chondritis, scarring
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.variant1: Difficult exposure: retrognathia, prominent teeth, limited neck extension, or a large tongue base.
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.variant2: Ossified thyroid cartilage that changes CT appearance of invasion.
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.variant3: Laryngocele or saccular cyst distorting supraglottic anatomy.
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.variant4: Anterior commissure tumor with subglottic extension (higher risk of cartilage invasion).
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.supply1: Arterial — Superior laryngeal artery (supraglottis); cricothyroid and inferior laryngeal arteries (glottis and subglottis)
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.supply2: Sensory — Internal SLN (above the folds); RLN (below)
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.supply3: Motor — RLN to intrinsic muscles except cricothyroid (EBSLN)
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.supply4: Lymphatics — Supraglottis rich and bilateral; glottis sparse (early glottic cancer rarely metastasizes); subglottis to paratracheal and prelaryngeal nodes
A-transoral-laser-laryngeal-cancer.pimp1: Q: Name the layers of the vocal fold from superficial to deep. A: Epithelium, superficial lamina propria (Reinke space), intermediate and deep lamina propria (vocal ligament), thyroarytenoid (vocalis) muscle

## Central Neck Dissection  (slug: central-neck, region: Thyroid / Parathyroid / Salivary)

A-central-neck.summary: Central neck dissection clears the level VI (and upper VII) nodal basin that drains the thyroid, larynx and cervical trachea. The compartment is bounded by the hyoid above, the innominate artery (right) or the corresponding axial plane (left) below, the common carotids laterally and the prevertebral fascia deep. Safety turns on finding the recurrent laryngeal nerve before the paratracheal packet is mobilized and on keeping the inferior thyroid artery branches to the parathyroids intact.
A-central-neck.layer1: Incision and subplatysmal flaps — skin; platysma (may be thin or absent in the midline); anterior jugular veins; investing layer of deep cervical fascia. Raise flaps deep to platysma and superficial to the anterior jugular veins, superiorly to the thyroid cartilage or hyoid and inferiorly to the sternal notch.
A-central-neck.layer2: Strap muscles — sternohyoid (superficial, medial); sternothyroid (deep, attaches to oblique line of thyroid cartilage); midline raphe (linea alba cervicalis); ansa cervicalis branches entering straps laterally and inferiorly. Divide the raphe vertically and retract straps laterally. Sternothyroid lies directly on the thyroid and inserts on the oblique line; dividing its upper fibers improves superior pole exposure if needed.
A-central-neck.layer3: Prelaryngeal (Delphian) and pretracheal nodes — cricothyroid membrane and muscle; pyramidal lobe (if present); isthmus; pretracheal fat and nodes down to the innominate artery. Clear the prelaryngeal tissue over the cricothyroid membrane from the hyoid level downward, staying superficial to the cricothyroid muscles. Inferiorly the pretracheal fat thins over the tracheal rings toward the thymus.
A-central-neck.layer4: Lateral boundary: carotid sheath — common carotid artery (medial in the sheath); internal jugular vein (lateral); vagus (posterior, between them). Open the fascia along the medial border of the common carotid from the superior thyroid pedicle level down to the thoracic inlet. This line is the lateral limit of level VI.
A-central-neck.layer5: Recurrent laryngeal nerve and tracheoesophageal groove — RLN; inferior thyroid artery and its branches; tracheoesophageal groove; paratracheal nodes (anterior and, on the right, posterior to the nerve). Identify the RLN inferiorly in the groove or at its crossing with the inferior thyroid artery, then trace it the length of the field. On the right, the nerve runs more obliquely (lateral-inferior to medial-superior) and nodal tissue lies both anterior and posterior to it; dissect both packets.
A-central-neck.layer6: Parathyroid preservation — superior parathyroid (posterior, deep to RLN plane, near cricothyroid junction); inferior parathyroid (anterior to RLN, near lower pole or in thyrothymic tissue); end-artery branches from the inferior thyroid artery. The inferior gland lies within or next to the dissection field and is most at risk. Peel it laterally on its pedicle off the specimen; examine the specimen and autotransplant any devascularized or inadvertently removed gland after frozen confirmation.
A-central-neck.layer7: Inferior release and thoracic inlet — thymus / thyrothymic ligament; inferior thyroid veins; innominate artery (right, crossing the trachea); left brachiocephalic vein (deeper, retrosternal). Release the specimen inferiorly at the innominate artery on the right; on the left, the consensus lower limit is the corresponding axial plane. The suprasternal notch is the level VI / VII boundary, so tissue between the notch and the innominate artery is superior level VII. Ligate thymic and inferior thyroid veins rather than blindly avulsing.
A-central-neck.layer8: Deep limit — esophagus (posterior and slightly left); prevertebral fascia; longus colli. The floor of the paratracheal dissection is the prevertebral fascia. The esophagus projects to the left of the trachea, so the left tracheoesophageal groove is deeper and the esophageal wall is closer.
A-central-neck.landmark1: Right RLN — In the triangle bounded by the trachea medially, the common carotid laterally and the inferior thyroid lobe superiorly, about 1-2 cm below the lower pole; it runs obliquely from lateral-inferior to medial-superior toward the groove (It loops under the subclavian artery, so it enters the neck more laterally and is at risk across the whole right paratracheal packet)
A-central-neck.landmark2: Left RLN — Directly in the left tracheoesophageal groove, running nearly vertically after looping under the aortic arch lateral to the ligamentum arteriosum (Its predictable groove course allows early identification low in the neck before nodal clearance)
A-central-neck.landmark3: RLN laryngeal entry — Deep to the inferior constrictor just posterior to the cricothyroid joint (inferior cornu of the thyroid cartilage), adjacent to Berry ligament (Fixed superior endpoint; the nerve is most vulnerable here where it is tethered)
A-central-neck.landmark4: Inferior thyroid artery — Arises from the thyrocervical trunk, passes medially behind the carotid sheath and enters the lower-mid posterior thyroid; follow it medially from the carotid (Main blood supply to both parathyroids on most sides; its crossing with the RLN is a classic nerve landmark)
A-central-neck.landmark5: Innominate artery — Palpate the pulsatile vessel crossing the trachea obliquely from left-inferior to right-superior at the thoracic inlet, deep to the sternal notch (Inferior limit of a consensus central neck dissection on the right; level VII (superior mediastinal) nodes lie between the suprasternal notch and the innominate artery)
A-central-neck.landmark6: Inferior parathyroid — Anterior to the plane of the RLN, within roughly 1-2 cm of the inferior thyroid pole or in the thyrothymic ligament; tan-orange, discrete, flattened, with a hilar vessel (Most often devascularized or removed with the central packet)
A-central-neck.danger1: Recurrent laryngeal nerve — where: In or near the tracheoesophageal groove; right nerve oblique with nodes on both sides; may branch extralaryngeally (motor fibers usually in the anterior branch); protect: Identify early and visualize the full length before dividing any paratracheal tissue; avoid traction and thermal spread near the nerve; neuromonitoring as an adjunct; injury: Ipsilateral vocal fold paralysis (dysphonia, aspiration); bilateral injury causes airway obstruction
A-central-neck.danger2: Parathyroid glands and their blood supply — where: Inferior glands within or adjacent to level VI; supplied mainly by end branches of the inferior thyroid artery; protect: Ligate inferior thyroid artery branches distal to parathyroid branches; dissect glands off the specimen on their pedicle; check the specimen; autotransplant devascularized tissue; injury: Transient or permanent hypoparathyroidism
A-central-neck.danger3: External branch of SLN — where: Near the superior thyroid pedicle when the upper prelaryngeal dissection extends to the superior pole, on the inferior constrictor heading to cricothyroid; protect: Ligate superior pole vessels individually on the capsule; injury: Loss of pitch range and vocal projection
A-central-neck.danger4: Trachea and esophagus — where: Medial and posterior floor of the dissection; esophagus to the left of the trachea; protect: Stay on the tracheal surface with fine dissection, avoid energy device contact on the tracheal wall, and identify the esophageal muscle fibers on the left; injury: Tracheal or esophageal perforation, delayed necrosis, fistula
A-central-neck.danger5: Innominate artery / left brachiocephalic vein — where: Thoracic inlet at the inferior limit, deep to the sternal notch; protect: Dissect the lower limit under vision; ligate thymic veins; do not clamp blindly in the inlet; injury: Major hemorrhage
A-central-neck.danger6: Thoracic duct / lymphatics (left) — where: Low left neck near the venous angle, lateral to the carotid; protect: Stay medial to the carotid; ligate lymphatic tissue at the inlet; injury: Chyle leak (uncommon in isolated level VI dissection)
A-central-neck.variant1: Nonrecurrent right laryngeal nerve (commonly cited ~0.3-1% of patients) arising directly from the cervical vagus, associated with an aberrant retroesophageal right subclavian artery (arteria lusoria); it runs medially to the larynx at a variable level, often near the inferior thyroid artery or higher. Left-sided nonrecurrence is very rare (situs inversus).
A-central-neck.variant2: Extralaryngeal branching of the RLN before laryngeal entry in a substantial minority of nerves; the anterior branch usually carries the adductor motor fibers.
A-central-neck.variant3: RLN position relative to the inferior thyroid artery varies (anterior, posterior or between branches), more variable on the right.
A-central-neck.variant4: Intrathymic or low-lying inferior parathyroid along the thyrothymic tract.
A-central-neck.variant5: Zuckerkandl tubercle (posterior projection of the lateral lobe), under which the RLN often runs.
A-central-neck.supply1: Arterial supply — Superior thyroid artery (1st anterior branch of ECA) to upper pole; inferior thyroid artery (thyrocervical trunk) to lower pole and parathyroids; thyroidea ima (inconstant) from the arch or innominate
A-central-neck.supply2: Venous drainage — Superior and middle thyroid veins to IJV; inferior thyroid veins to the brachiocephalic veins
A-central-neck.supply3: Motor nerves — RLN (all intrinsic laryngeal muscles except cricothyroid); EBSLN (cricothyroid); ansa cervicalis (strap muscles)
A-central-neck.supply4: Lymphatic drainage — Thyroid to prelaryngeal, pretracheal and paratracheal (level VI) nodes, then to lateral levels III-IV and level VII
A-central-neck.pimp1: Q: What are the boundaries of the central compartment (level VI)? A: Hyoid superiorly, suprasternal notch inferiorly, common carotid arteries laterally, prevertebral fascia deep
A-central-neck.pimp2: Q: What are the boundaries of level VII? A: Suprasternal notch superiorly (the border with level VI) to the innominate (brachiocephalic) artery inferiorly, in the superior mediastinum
A-central-neck.pimp3: Q: Why does the right RLN run more obliquely than the left? A: It loops under the right subclavian artery (higher and more lateral) whereas the left loops under the aortic arch at the ligamentum arteriosum
A-central-neck.pimp4: Q: What vascular anomaly predicts a nonrecurrent laryngeal nerve? A: Aberrant retroesophageal right subclavian artery (arteria lusoria), usually with no right recurrent loop
A-central-neck.pimp5: Q: Which parathyroid is most at risk in central neck dissection and why? A: The inferior gland; it lies anterior to the RLN plane in the lower pole/thyrothymic region that is removed with the level VI packet
A-central-neck.pimp6: Q: What is the Delphian node? A: The prelaryngeal node on the cricothyroid membrane; involvement suggests aggressive thyroid or laryngeal (subglottic) disease
A-central-neck.pimp7: Q: On the right side, where is the extra nodal packet relative to the RLN? A: Posterior (deep) to the nerve, between the RLN and the esophagus/prevertebral fascia
A-central-neck.pimp8: Q: Which intrinsic laryngeal muscle is not supplied by the RLN? A: Cricothyroid, supplied by the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve
OR-central-neck.step1: Expose the central compartment from hyoid/thyroid cartilage level to the innominate region as indicated, defining the trachea and carotid sheaths bilaterally.
OR-central-neck.step2: Identify the recurrent laryngeal nerve on the side of dissection before mobilizing nodal tissue from the tracheoesophageal groove.
OR-central-neck.step3: Preserve viable parathyroid glands and their blood supply; separate nodal tissue from glands rather than sacrificing the vascular pedicle with the packet.
OR-central-neck.step4: Remove prelaryngeal and pretracheal nodal tissue in continuity with the intended paratracheal compartment, keeping dissection on known anatomic planes.
OR-central-neck.step5: On the right, remain aware of the recurrent nerve's more oblique course; on the left, follow the nerve within the tracheoesophageal groove and protect the thoracic inlet structures.
OR-central-neck.step6: Control small inferior thyroid/thymic vessels as the specimen is released inferiorly, avoiding blind energy use near the RLN.
OR-central-neck.step7: Orient/specimen-label by compartment as required and inspect the tracheoesophageal grooves for residual target nodes without unnecessary nerve skeletonization.
OR-central-neck.step8: Confirm RLN integrity, parathyroid perfusion, and meticulous central-neck hemostasis before closure.

## Four-Gland Parathyroid Exploration  (slug: four-gland, region: Thyroid / Parathyroid / Salivary)

A-four-gland.summary: Bilateral exploration identifies all four parathyroid glands so that multigland hyperplasia or a double adenoma is not missed. Success depends on knowing the embryology: superior glands (fourth pouch) sit posterior and constant near the cricothyroid junction, deep to the RLN plane, while inferior glands (third pouch, with the thymus) are anterior to the RLN plane and far more variable in position. The RLN runs between the two glands' planes, so every search must be oriented to it.
A-four-gland.layer1: Incision, flaps and strap muscles — skin; platysma; anterior jugular veins; sternohyoid and sternothyroid. Low transverse incision in a skin crease; divide the midline raphe and retract straps laterally. Sternothyroid may need partial division superiorly for upper gland exposure.
A-four-gland.layer2: Thyroid mobilization — middle thyroid vein(s); thyroid lobe; carotid sheath (lateral). Ligate the middle thyroid vein and rotate the lobe anteromedially. This opens the space between the lobe and the carotid where both parathyroids and the RLN lie.
A-four-gland.layer3: Orientation to RLN and inferior thyroid artery — inferior thyroid artery (crossing transversely behind carotid to the lobe); RLN (in or near the tracheoesophageal groove). Their crossing point divides the field: superior glands lie above and deep (posterior) to it, inferior glands below and superficial (anterior).
A-four-gland.layer4: Superior parathyroid search — posterior surface of upper-mid lobe; cricothyroid junction / Zuckerkandl tubercle; fat posterior to the RLN. Typically about 1 cm above the RLN-ITA crossing, posterior to the RLN, at the level of the cricoid cartilage. Enlarged superior glands descend posteriorly into the tracheoesophageal groove and retroesophageal space.
A-four-gland.layer5: Inferior parathyroid search — lower pole (anterior, lateral or posterior surface); thyrothymic ligament; cervical thymic tongue. Usually within about 1-2 cm of the lower pole, anterior to the RLN plane. If absent, follow the thyrothymic ligament and thymus inferiorly.
A-four-gland.layer6: Contralateral exploration — mirror-image structures on the opposite side. Glands are often roughly symmetric in position, so the location of one side's glands guides the other side's search.
A-four-gland.layer7: Ectopic search for a missing gland — retroesophageal / paraesophageal space (superior); carotid sheath up to the angle of mandible (undescended inferior); intrathymic / anterior mediastinum (inferior); intrathyroidal (either). Search in order of probability based on which gland is missing; cervical thymectomy and ipsilateral thyroid lobectomy are later steps once other sites are exhausted.
A-four-gland.layer8: Resection and remnant — abnormal gland pedicle; most normal-appearing gland for subtotal remnant. For hyperplasia, leave a well-vascularized remnant (about the size of one to two normal glands; commonly cited ~40-60 mg, sources vary) and mark it with a clip or suture; create the remnant before removing the other glands.
A-four-gland.landmark1: Superior parathyroid — Posterior surface of the upper-mid lobe, roughly 1 cm cephalad to the RLN-ITA crossing and deep (dorsal) to the RLN, near the cricothyroid junction; often under a thin fascial layer, mobile in a fat lobule (Fourth pouch derivative with short descent; position is fairly constant)
A-four-gland.landmark2: Inferior parathyroid — Near the lower pole anterior (ventral) to the RLN plane; if not there, follow the thyrothymic ligament inferiorly into the cervical thymus (Third pouch derivative that descends with the thymus; widest position range)
A-four-gland.landmark3: RLN-ITA crossing — Trace the inferior thyroid artery medially from behind the carotid; the RLN crosses it near the posterior lobe at mid-lower level (Reference point dividing superior (posterior) and inferior (anterior) gland territories)
A-four-gland.landmark4: Gland identity cues — Tan to orange-brown, discrete, smooth, flattened oval, slides within fat; a hilar vessel; darkens when bruised. Fat is yellow and floats; lymph node is gray-pink; thyroid nodules are firmer and redder (Distinguishes gland from fat, node, thymus or thyroid nodule; confirm with frozen section if uncertain)
A-four-gland.landmark5: Retroesophageal space — Rotate the larynx/trachea medially and sweep a finger behind the esophagus toward the prevertebral fascia, superiorly near the cricopharyngeus (Common site of an enlarged descended superior gland)
A-four-gland.landmark6: Undescended inferior gland — Open the carotid sheath and search along it up to the carotid bifurcation and angle of the mandible, often in a thymic remnant (Explains a missing inferior gland with nothing in the thymus)
A-four-gland.danger1: Recurrent laryngeal nerve — where: Between superior (posterior) and inferior (anterior) gland planes; superior glands lie close behind it; protect: Identify the nerve on each side before removing glands from the groove; avoid cautery near it; injury: Vocal fold paralysis; bilateral exploration exposes both nerves
A-four-gland.danger2: Parathyroid vascular pedicles — where: End branches, mainly from the inferior thyroid artery, entering at a hilum; protect: Handle glands by surrounding fat; biopsy only the gland to be removed or the tip opposite the hilum; do not strip normal glands; injury: Permanent hypoparathyroidism, especially after subtotal resection
A-four-gland.danger3: Esophagus — where: Posteromedial; enlarged superior glands descend beside or behind it; protect: Blunt finger dissection in the retroesophageal space; bougie if needed; injury: Perforation
A-four-gland.danger4: Carotid sheath contents — where: Lateral during undescended gland search; vagus posterior in the sheath; protect: Open the sheath under vision; identify vagus before dissecting within; injury: Vascular injury; vagal injury (vocal fold paralysis; added superior laryngeal and pharyngeal deficits only if injured high, near the skull base)
A-four-gland.danger5: Innominate vessels / mediastinum — where: Inferiorly during cervical thymectomy; protect: Gentle traction-countertraction on the thymus; ligate thymic veins draining to the left brachiocephalic vein; injury: Venous hemorrhage
A-four-gland.variant1: Supernumerary glands (13% in Akerström's 503-autopsy series, many rudimentary; a clinically relevant fifth gland is often quoted at ~5%), most often in the thymus; relevant in hyperplasia (MEN1, renal HPT).
A-four-gland.variant2: Fewer than four glands found in about 3% of autopsies (Akerström), likely a missed gland; never assume three is normal.
A-four-gland.variant3: Undescended inferior gland (parathymus) high in the carotid sheath near the bifurcation or angle of mandible.
A-four-gland.variant4: Intrathyroidal parathyroid (uncommon, a few percent).
A-four-gland.variant5: Descended superior adenoma in the tracheoesophageal groove or retroesophageal space below the level of the inferior thyroid artery.
A-four-gland.supply1: Arterial supply — Inferior thyroid artery supplies most superior and inferior glands; superior thyroid artery or an anastomotic branch may supply the superior gland
A-four-gland.supply2: Embryology — Superior glands from the 4th pharyngeal pouch (with ultimobranchial body); inferior glands from the 3rd pouch with the thymus
A-four-gland.supply3: Motor nerves — RLN in the field bilaterally; EBSLN near the superior pole if mobilized
A-four-gland.supply4: Venous drainage — Thyroid venous plexus (superior, middle, inferior thyroid veins); thymic veins to left brachiocephalic vein
A-four-gland.pimp1: Q: From which pharyngeal pouches do the superior and inferior parathyroids arise? A: Superior from the 4th pouch; inferior from the 3rd pouch (with the thymus)
A-four-gland.pimp2: Q: Why are inferior parathyroids more variable in position? A: They migrate farther caudally with the descending thymus, so they can stop high (carotid sheath) or descend into the mediastinum
A-four-gland.pimp3: Q: Relative to the coronal plane of the RLN, where are the superior and inferior glands? A: Superior glands are posterior (dorsal) to the nerve; inferior glands are anterior (ventral)
A-four-gland.pimp4: Q: Main arterial supply of the parathyroids? A: Inferior thyroid artery (from the thyrocervical trunk)
A-four-gland.pimp5: Q: Where is the most common ectopic location for an inferior gland? For a superior gland? A: Inferior: thymus / thyrothymic ligament; superior: tracheoesophageal groove or retroesophageal space
A-four-gland.pimp6: Q: What is the Miami criterion for intraoperative PTH? A: A fall of more than 50% from the highest pre-excision value at 10 minutes after excision
A-four-gland.pimp7: Q: How much tissue is typically left in a subtotal parathyroidectomy? A: A well-vascularized remnant about the size of one to two normal glands (commonly cited ~40-60 mg), marked with a clip or suture
A-four-gland.pimp8: Q: What is the average weight of a normal parathyroid gland? A: Roughly 30-50 mg, about 5 x 3 x 1 mm
OR-four-gland.step1: Open the central neck through a low transverse incision, raise subplatysmal flaps, separate the strap muscles in the midline, and expose both thyroid lobes sufficiently for bilateral exploration.
OR-four-gland.step2: Begin on one side by mobilizing the thyroid medially and identifying the RLN and inferior thyroid artery relationship before searching directly behind the lobe.
OR-four-gland.step3: Identify the superior gland near the posterior upper/mid thyroid and the inferior gland along the lower pole–thyrothymic tract; preserve each gland's vascular pedicle while judging size and morphology.
OR-four-gland.step4: Repeat the same anatomic survey on the opposite side so that all four expected glands are accounted for before deciding what to remove.
OR-four-gland.step5: If a gland is missing, search embryologic ectopic pathways systematically—thyrothymic ligament/thymus, tracheoesophageal groove, retroesophageal space, carotid sheath, and mediastinal direction as indicated.
OR-four-gland.step6: Perform the planned subtotal resection or targeted removal of abnormal glands while leaving well-vascularized parathyroid tissue when clinically appropriate.
OR-four-gland.step7: Use intraoperative PTH in the context of multigland disease rather than as a substitute for a complete bilateral anatomic assessment.
OR-four-gland.step8: Recheck RLN integrity, remaining gland perfusion, and hemostasis bilaterally before strap and skin closure.

## Parathyroidectomy  (slug: parathyroidectomy, region: Thyroid / Parathyroid / Salivary)

A-parathyroidectomy.summary: Parathyroidectomy finds and removes abnormal parathyroid tissue. The glands have predictable embryologic positions relative to the recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) and the inferior thyroid artery (ITA). Superior glands (fourth pouch) lie posterior and dorsal to the coronal plane of the RLN at the upper-mid lobe. Inferior glands (third pouch, with the thymus) lie anterior and ventral to the nerve near the lower pole, and are the more variable and more often ectopic. Safety depends on seeing the RLN before dividing tissue in the tracheoesophageal groove and on preserving the blood supply of the normal glands.
A-parathyroidectomy.layer1: Incision and flaps — skin; platysma; anterior jugular veins; investing layer of deep cervical fascia. Use a short low transverse incision placed according to localization, and plan it so it can be extended. Raise limited subplatysmal flaps.
A-parathyroidectomy.layer2: Strap muscles — midline raphe (linea alba); sternohyoid (superficial); sternothyroid (deep, attached to the thyroid capsule). Open the midline and retract the straps laterally. A lateral approach between the SCM and the straps gives direct access to a posterior adenoma.
A-parathyroidectomy.layer3: Thyroid rotation — thyroid lobe; middle thyroid vein; carotid sheath (lateral). Divide the middle thyroid vein if needed and rotate the lobe medially to enter the plane behind the thyroid, between it and the carotid sheath.
A-parathyroidectomy.layer4: Tracheoesophageal groove and nerve — RLN; ITA; tubercle of Zuckerkandl; tracheoesophageal groove. Identify the RLN, or a nerve-safe zone, before dividing anything near the ITA. The right RLN runs more obliquely from lateral to medial; the left runs more vertically in the groove.
A-parathyroidectomy.layer5: Search by embryologic location — superior parathyroid (posterior upper-mid lobe, near the cricothyroid junction, about 1 cm above the ITA-RLN crossing); inferior parathyroid (lower pole, anterior / lateral, thyrothymic ligament); thymic tongue. A normal gland is yellow-tan to mahogany, flat and oval, may move in fat, and has a hilar vessel. An adenoma is larger and darker. Fat is yellower and lighter; lymph nodes are gray and firm; thyroid nodules are redder and continuous with the gland.
A-parathyroidectomy.layer6: Gland mobilization — adenoma capsule; vascular pedicle (usually from the ITA); RLN (deep and medial for superior glands). Dissect around the capsule without rupturing it (rupture risks parathyromatosis). Control the pedicle last. Superior adenomas often descend posteriorly along the esophagus, deep to the RLN.
A-parathyroidectomy.layer7: Ectopic search if IOPTH does not fall — thymus / anterior mediastinum (inferior); retroesophageal / paraesophageal space (superior); carotid sheath up to the angle of the mandible (undescended inferior); intrathyroidal; contralateral glands. Follow the embryology: inferior glands lie anywhere along the thymic descent; superior glands lie posteriorly in the tracheoesophageal groove or retroesophageal space, extending into the posterior mediastinum.
A-parathyroidectomy.landmark1: Superior parathyroid — Posterior surface of the upper-mid lobe at about the cricoid level, about 1 cm cranial to where the RLN crosses the ITA, dorsal to the RLN plane, often under the tubercle of Zuckerkandl (More constant position; a superior adenoma may sink posteriorly and inferiorly)
A-parathyroidectomy.landmark2: Inferior parathyroid — Ventral to the RLN plane, on the anterior, lateral or posterior surface of the lower pole, or in the thyrothymic ligament / thymic tongue (More variable location because of the longer descent with the thymus)
A-parathyroidectomy.landmark3: RLN — In the tracheoesophageal groove below the ITA, within the triangle bordered by the trachea / esophagus medially, the common carotid laterally and the ITA superiorly. Then follow it to its entry under the inferior constrictor behind the cricothyroid joint. (Must be seen before dividing tissue in the groove)
A-parathyroidectomy.landmark4: ITA — From the thyrocervical trunk, it rises behind the carotid and loops medially at the mid-lobe to reach the thyroid; its branches supply both parathyroids (Orientation for both glands and for the nerve)
A-parathyroidectomy.landmark5: Tubercle of Zuckerkandl — Posterolateral projection of the lobe at about the cricoid level; lift it medially (The RLN usually lies deep and medial to it, and the superior gland lies nearby)
A-parathyroidectomy.landmark6: Thyrothymic ligament — Band of tissue from the lower pole into the superior mediastinum; deliver it with gentle traction (Common site of an inferior gland or inferior adenoma)
A-parathyroidectomy.danger1: Recurrent laryngeal nerve — where: Tracheoesophageal groove; superior adenomas often lie posterior to it, inferior adenomas anterior; protect: Identify before dividing tissue in the groove, use no energy device near it, and trace a nonrecurrent nerve on the right if no nerve is found low; injury: Vocal fold paralysis, hoarseness, aspiration
A-parathyroidectomy.danger2: Inferior thyroid artery branches — where: Terminal branches to the parathyroids and the thyroid capsule; protect: Ligate adenoma pedicles close to the gland and preserve branches to normal glands; injury: Devascularization of normal glands, causing hypoparathyroidism
A-parathyroidectomy.danger3: Normal parathyroid glands — where: Superior and inferior positions bilaterally; protect: Minimal handling and do not biopsy unnecessarily; autotransplant a devascularized gland; injury: Permanent hypoparathyroidism
A-parathyroidectomy.danger4: Esophagus and trachea — where: Deep and medial during retroesophageal or paraesophageal exploration; protect: Blunt dissection along the prevertebral fascia and a bougie or nasogastric tube if needed; injury: Perforation, mediastinitis
A-parathyroidectomy.danger5: Neck hematoma — where: Central compartment beneath the strap muscles; protect: Valsalva check and meticulous hemostasis; injury: Airway compromise; open the wound at the bedside
A-parathyroidectomy.variant1: Nonrecurrent right inferior laryngeal nerve (about 0.3-0.8% of right sides in large series), almost always with a retroesophageal right subclavian artery (arteria lusoria); a left nonrecurrent nerve is exceptionally rare (about 0.004%) and needs situs inversus / a right-sided arch
A-parathyroidectomy.variant2: Supernumerary glands (most often in the thymus)
A-parathyroidectomy.variant3: Ectopic inferior gland: thymus or anterior mediastinum, undescended in the carotid sheath, intrathyroidal
A-parathyroidectomy.variant4: Ectopic superior gland: retroesophageal or retropharyngeal, posterior mediastinum along the tracheoesophageal groove
A-parathyroidectomy.variant5: Extralaryngeal branching of the RLN (motor fibres usually in the anterior branch)
A-parathyroidectomy.supply1: Arterial — ITA (thyrocervical trunk) supplies most superior and inferior glands; superior glands may receive STA branches or an anastomotic loop
A-parathyroidectomy.supply2: Venous — Thyroid veins: superior and middle to the IJV, inferior to the brachiocephalic vein
A-parathyroidectomy.supply3: Nerves — RLN in the tracheoesophageal groove; EBSLN at the superior pole (rarely exposed); cervical sympathetic chain posterolateral
A-parathyroidectomy.supply4: Embryology — Superior: fourth pharyngeal pouch (with the ultimobranchial body). Inferior: third pouch (with the thymus).
A-parathyroidectomy.pimp1: Q: Embryologic origin of the superior and inferior parathyroids? A: Superior from the fourth pouch; inferior from the third pouch with the thymus
A-parathyroidectomy.pimp2: Q: Why are inferior glands more often ectopic? A: They travel further, descending with the thymus, so they can stop high or end up in the mediastinum
A-parathyroidectomy.pimp3: Q: Relationship of each gland to the RLN? A: Superior glands are dorsal (posterior) to the coronal plane of the RLN; inferior glands are ventral (anterior)
A-parathyroidectomy.pimp4: Q: Most common ectopic site for an inferior gland? For a superior gland? A: Inferior: thymus / anterior mediastinum. Superior: tracheoesophageal groove / retroesophageal space.
A-parathyroidectomy.pimp5: Q: State the Miami criterion. A: IOPTH falls more than 50% from the highest pre-excision value at 10 minutes after excision
A-parathyroidectomy.pimp6: Q: What anomaly predicts a nonrecurrent laryngeal nerve? A: Retroesophageal (aberrant) right subclavian artery, on the right side
A-parathyroidectomy.pimp7: Q: Main blood supply of the parathyroids? A: Inferior thyroid artery
A-parathyroidectomy.pimp8: Q: Where is a parathyroid autograft usually placed? A: Minced into the SCM (or the brachioradialis forearm muscle for hyperplasia)
OR-parathyroidectomy.step1: Confirm the localized side and depth before incision; position and expose the central neck through a focused transverse opening that can be extended if localization fails.
OR-parathyroidectomy.step2: Separate the strap muscles and retract the thyroid lobe medially just enough to enter the expected parathyroid plane without stripping the thyroid capsule broadly.
OR-parathyroidectomy.step3: Identify the RLN or a dependable nerve-safe landmark before dividing tissue in the tracheoesophageal groove or near the inferior thyroid artery.
OR-parathyroidectomy.step4: Find the abnormal gland using its expected embryologic location—superior gland posterior to the upper/mid lobe; inferior gland near the lower pole or thyrothymic tract—and distinguish it from fat, thyroid, and lymph node.
OR-parathyroidectomy.step5: Develop the gland circumferentially while preserving the recurrent nerve and controlling the terminal vascular pedicle last, minimizing capsule violation.
OR-parathyroidectomy.step6: Remove the abnormal gland intact and obtain intraoperative PTH after excision using the planned timing protocol; interpret the result against the appropriate baseline.
OR-parathyroidectomy.step7: If PTH fails to fall appropriately, reassess localization, ectopic sites, double adenoma, and multigland disease before converting to broader exploration.
OR-parathyroidectomy.step8: Confirm hemostasis, nerve integrity when monitored, and absence of an expanding central-neck space before layered closure.
