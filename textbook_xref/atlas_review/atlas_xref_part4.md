
## Laryngeal Fracture Repair  (slug: laryngeal-fracture, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-laryngeal-fracture.summary: Laryngeal fracture repair restores the cartilage framework and mucosal lining after blunt or penetrating trauma. Airway first (often tracheostomy), then repair mucosa so no cartilage is exposed, resuspend the anterior commissure, and fix fractures to restore height and width.
A-laryngeal-fracture.layer1: Airway — tracheostomy site (rings 2-4); cricoid. Avoid intubation through an unstable larynx; awake tracheostomy is often safest.
A-laryngeal-fracture.layer2: Exposure — skin; platysma; strap muscles; thyroid cartilage; cricoid; cricothyroid membrane. A horizontal incision at about the level of the cricothyroid membrane. Separate the straps in the midline, then lift them off the thyroid ala to see every fracture line and the cricoid.
A-laryngeal-fracture.layer3: Laryngofissure (if needed) — midline thyroid cartilage; anterior commissure; vocal folds. Midline thyrotomy, entering through the cricothyroid membrane and cutting up through the anterior commissure under vision.
A-laryngeal-fracture.layer4: Mucosal repair — endolaryngeal mucosa; arytenoids; vocal folds. Close mucosal tears with fine absorbable sutures, cover exposed cartilage with local mucosa, and reduce a dislocated arytenoid. Resuspend the anterior ends of the vocal folds to the outer thyroid perichondrium or cartilage so the commissure sits at its original height.
A-laryngeal-fracture.layer5: Framework fixation — thyroid cartilage fractures; cricoid fractures. Reduce thyroid and cricoid fractures and fix them with miniplates (or wire or sutures in a soft cartilage). Restoring the anterior thyroid angle and cricoid ring maintains glottic and subglottic size.
A-laryngeal-fracture.layer6: Stent (if required) — endolaryngeal stent. Use for severe mucosal disruption or anterior commissure instability.
A-laryngeal-fracture.landmark1: Thyroid notch — V-shaped notch on top of the thyroid cartilage (Midline for laryngofissure)
A-laryngeal-fracture.landmark2: Anterior commissure — Classically about the midpoint of the midline thyroid cartilage height (cadaver studies place it at or somewhat above the midpoint); match the uninjured side and the original attachment (Must be resuspended at its original height)
A-laryngeal-fracture.landmark3: Cricoid — Complete ring below the cricothyroid membrane (Injury causes subglottic stenosis)
A-laryngeal-fracture.landmark4: RLN entry — Behind the cricothyroid joint (At risk with lateral cricoid fractures and laryngotracheal separation)
A-laryngeal-fracture.danger1: Airway — where: Larynx and subglottis; protect: Tracheostomy before manipulation; injury: Airway loss
A-laryngeal-fracture.danger2: Anterior commissure — where: Midline glottis; protect: Resuspend; injury: Web, dysphonia
A-laryngeal-fracture.danger3: RLN — where: Behind the cricothyroid joint; protect: Avoid dissection posteriorly; injury: Vocal-fold paralysis
A-laryngeal-fracture.danger4: Cricoid — where: Below the thyroid cartilage; protect: Repair and stabilize; injury: Subglottic stenosis
A-laryngeal-fracture.danger5: Esophagus and pharynx — where: Behind the larynx; protect: Inspect for injury; injury: Perforation and mediastinitis
A-laryngeal-fracture.variant1: Children: flexible cartilage, more soft-tissue injury than fracture
A-laryngeal-fracture.variant2: Older adults: ossified, brittle cartilage with comminuted fractures
A-laryngeal-fracture.variant3: Laryngotracheal separation with bilateral RLN injury
A-laryngeal-fracture.supply1: Arterial supply — Superior and inferior laryngeal arteries
A-laryngeal-fracture.supply2: Motor — RLN (intrinsic muscles); EBSLN (cricothyroid)
A-laryngeal-fracture.supply3: Sensory — Internal branch of SLN supraglottic; RLN subglottic
A-laryngeal-fracture.pimp1: Q: What is the Schaefer classification? A: Group I minor hematoma or laceration; II edema, hematoma, minor mucosal disruption, nondisplaced fracture; III massive edema, mucosal tear, exposed cartilage, displaced fracture, immobile fold; IV as III with unstable framework or anterior commissure disruption; V laryngotracheal separation
A-laryngeal-fracture.pimp2: Q: When should repair be done? A: Early: ideally within 24 hours and generally within 48 hours, once the airway is secure (some advocate waiting a few days for edema, but delayed repair is associated with worse voice and airway outcomes)
A-laryngeal-fracture.pimp3: Q: Why repair mucosa before cartilage? A: To avoid exposed cartilage and chondritis
A-laryngeal-fracture.pimp4: Q: What are the indications for a stent? A: Severe mucosal disruption, anterior commissure instability, comminuted fractures
A-laryngeal-fracture.pimp5: Q: What happens in laryngotracheal separation? A: Distal trachea retracts into the mediastinum; RLNs often injured
A-laryngeal-fracture.pimp6: Q: Why avoid intubation in an unstable larynx? A: It can worsen injury or create a false passage
A-laryngeal-fracture.pimp7: Q: Why are children less likely to fracture the larynx? A: Their larynx sits higher under the mandible and their cartilage is more elastic
A-laryngeal-fracture.pimp8: Q: Which cartilage injury most threatens long-term airway? A: Cricoid fracture (the only complete ring), leading to subglottic stenosis
OR-laryngeal-fracture.step1: Secure the airway using the safest route for the injury pattern, avoiding traumatic trans-laryngeal intubation when the framework is unstable.
OR-laryngeal-fracture.step2: Expose the laryngeal framework through a cervical incision and identify displaced thyroid/cricoid fractures and strap-muscle disruption.
OR-laryngeal-fracture.step3: Open the larynx only when needed to repair mucosal tears, arytenoid displacement, or exposed cartilage.
OR-laryngeal-fracture.step4: Reduce mucosal injury first so cartilage will not remain exposed to the airway.
OR-laryngeal-fracture.step5: Reduce and rigidly fix displaced framework fractures to restore laryngeal height, width, and anterior commissure geometry.
OR-laryngeal-fracture.step6: Repair vocal-fold/epiglottic/arytenoid injuries as indicated while preserving viable mucosa.
OR-laryngeal-fracture.step7: Use an endolaryngeal stent only when necessary for severe mucosal disruption/anterior-commissure stabilization, recognizing stent morbidity.
OR-laryngeal-fracture.step8: Reinspect endoscopically, confirm airway patency and hemostasis, and establish postoperative airway/voice/swallow surveillance.

## Medialization Thyroplasty  (slug: medialization, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-medialization.summary: Medialization (Isshiki type I) thyroplasty treats glottic insufficiency, usually from vocal-fold paralysis, by placing an implant through a window in the thyroid ala to push the paraglottic tissue and vocal fold medially. Success depends on placing the window at the level of the vocal fold rather than the ventricle or false fold, and keeping the inner perichondrium intact so the implant stays outside the airway.
A-medialization.layer1: Incision and flaps — skin; platysma; anterior jugular veins. A horizontal incision in a skin crease over the lower half of the thyroid ala gives access to the window. Raise subplatysmal flaps up to the thyroid notch and down to the cricoid.
A-medialization.layer2: Strap muscles — sternohyoid (superficial, split in the midline); thyrohyoid (deep, attaches to the oblique line); sternothyroid (inserts on the oblique line). Separate the sternohyoid in the midline and retract it laterally. Divide or elevate the thyrohyoid and sternothyroid off the lower thyroid ala along the oblique line on the operated side to expose the outer cartilage.
A-medialization.layer3: Outer perichondrium and thyroid ala — outer perichondrium; thyroid lamina; thyroid notch; inferior border of thyroid cartilage; oblique line. Incise the outer perichondrium and elevate it to expose the ala. Mark the midline, notch, inferior border and estimated level of the vocal fold. Ossified cartilage in older patients needs a drill instead of a knife or punch.
A-medialization.layer4: Thyroplasty window — anterior strut of cartilage (preserved); inferior strut of cartilage (preserved); window over the paraglottic space. Place the window parallel to and about 3-4 mm above the inferior border, with its anterior edge about 5-10 mm behind the midline (the smaller offset in women, the larger in men); typical windows are about 4 x 8 to 5 x 10 mm and vary by implant system. Keep anterior and inferior struts so the ala does not fracture.
A-medialization.layer5: Inner perichondrium — inner perichondrium; paraglottic space fat. Elevate the inner perichondrium from the cartilage around the window, often incising it to allow medial displacement. Do not tear through the paraglottic tissues into the ventricle or airway.
A-medialization.layer6: Paraglottic space and implant pocket — thyroarytenoid muscle (vocalis medially); lateral cricoarytenoid (posteroinferior); ventricle and saccule (superior); conus elasticus (inferior). The implant pushes the TA medially. Medialize at and slightly below the vocal-fold level; placing it too high bulges the false fold and ventricle. The deepest point is usually posterior along the membranous fold toward the vocal process, but posterior pressure is limited by the arytenoid.
A-medialization.layer7: Endoscopic and voice check, then closure — vocal fold position; glottic gap; airway caliber. Adjust implant size and position with the patient phonating under local anesthesia when possible and with flexible laryngoscopy. Close strap muscles and skin over a drain if needed.
A-medialization.landmark1: Thyroid notch and midline — Palpate the V-shaped superior notch; drop a vertical line from it to the inferior border along the prominence (All window measurements are taken from this line)
A-medialization.landmark2: Level of the vocal fold — Classically taught as about the midpoint of the vertical midline height of the thyroid cartilage (notch to inferior border), running back roughly parallel to the inferior border. Cadaver series vary: one dissection study put the anterior commissure nearer the junction of the upper two-fifths and lower three-fifths, and in many men the fold slopes down posteriorly. Confirm with a needle passed through the cartilage under endoscopic view. (The window and implant must be at this level; a window placed too high medializes the false fold and ventricle)
A-medialization.landmark3: Inferior border of the thyroid ala — The lower free edge of the cartilage just above the cricothyroid membrane and cricothyroid muscle (The window lies parallel to it and a few millimeters above it)
A-medialization.landmark4: Oblique line — A ridge running down and forward on the outer ala where the sternothyroid and thyrohyoid attach (The sternothyroid and thyrohyoid attach here and are released along it to expose the ala for the window)
A-medialization.landmark5: Inner perichondrium — A smooth white membrane seen once cartilage is removed; it separates the cartilage from the yellow paraglottic fat (Keeping it intact keeps the implant extraluminal)
A-medialization.danger1: Laryngeal mucosa (ventricle and airway) — where: Medial to the inner perichondrium and paraglottic tissue; the ventricle lies just above the fold level; protect: Elevate the inner perichondrium carefully and do not place the window too high; injury: Airway entry, implant exposure, infection and extrusion
A-medialization.danger2: Airway — where: The glottis narrows with medialization, more so with edema or hematoma; protect: Avoid overmedialization, control bleeding, and observe overnight when edema is a concern; injury: Stridor or airway obstruction from edema or hematoma
A-medialization.danger3: Thyroid ala integrity — where: Anterior and inferior struts around the window; protect: Preserve struts of a few millimeters and drill ossified cartilage gently; injury: Cartilage fracture, unstable implant, change in voice
A-medialization.danger4: Branches of the superior thyroid artery (including the cricothyroid artery) — where: On the lower ala and across the cricothyroid membrane; protect: Cauterize or ligate before making the window; injury: Hematoma in the paraglottic space
A-medialization.danger5: Pyriform sinus mucosa — where: Posterior to the posterior thyroid ala; protect: Do not extend the window or pocket posteriorly beyond the ala; injury: Pharyngeal fistula, infection
A-medialization.variant1: Smaller female larynx: smaller ala, so the window is smaller and sits closer to the midline
A-medialization.variant2: Ossified thyroid cartilage in older men: needs a drill and makes the window harder to shape
A-medialization.variant3: Vocal-fold height mismatch or a large posterior gap from arytenoid malposition: medialization alone may not close it (consider arytenoid adduction)
A-medialization.variant4: Prior radiation or neck surgery: fibrotic paraglottic tissue and higher complication risk
A-medialization.supply1: Arterial supply — Superior thyroid artery (superior laryngeal and cricothyroid branches); inferior thyroid artery (inferior laryngeal branch)
A-medialization.supply2: Motor — RLN to all intrinsic muscles except cricothyroid; external branch of SLN to cricothyroid
A-medialization.supply3: Sensory — Internal branch of SLN above the folds; RLN below the folds
A-medialization.pimp1: Q: Name the four Isshiki thyroplasty types. A: I medialization, II lateralization, III relaxation (shortening), IV tensioning (lengthening)
A-medialization.pimp2: Q: What is the only abductor of the vocal fold? A: Posterior cricoarytenoid
A-medialization.pimp3: Q: Which intrinsic muscle is not innervated by the RLN? A: Cricothyroid (external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve)
A-medialization.pimp4: Q: Where is the vocal fold relative to the external thyroid cartilage? A: Classically at about the midpoint of the vertical midline height, running back roughly parallel to the inferior border (individual variation is large, so confirm endoscopically)
A-medialization.pimp5: Q: Why preserve the inner perichondrium? A: It keeps the implant out of the airway and lowers extrusion and infection risk
A-medialization.pimp6: Q: Why might a large posterior glottic gap persist after medialization? A: The implant medializes the membranous fold but does not rotate the arytenoid or correct vocal process position or height
A-medialization.pimp7: Q: What attaches to the oblique line of the thyroid cartilage? A: Sternothyroid, thyrohyoid and the thyropharyngeal part of the inferior constrictor
A-medialization.pimp8: Q: What is the paraglottic space bounded by? A: Thyroid cartilage laterally, conus elasticus inferomedially, quadrangular membrane superomedially, and pyriform sinus mucosa posteriorly
OR-medialization.step1: Expose the thyroid cartilage through a horizontal neck incision and preserve perichondrium/strap anatomy needed for stable framework work.
OR-medialization.step2: Mark the true vocal-fold level using external laryngeal landmarks and create the thyroplasty window without violating the inner perichondrium.
OR-medialization.step3: Elevate the inner perichondrium carefully and test medialization with the patient phonating when awake technique is used.
OR-medialization.step4: Shape/position the implant to medialize the membranous fold while avoiding excessive posterior pressure or airway narrowing.
OR-medialization.step5: Use voice quality and/or flexible laryngoscopy to adjust implant depth, anterior-posterior position, and height.
OR-medialization.step6: Secure the implant so it cannot migrate into the airway or soft tissues.
OR-medialization.step7: Reinspect the larynx for airway caliber, vocal-fold position, edema, and mucosal integrity.
OR-medialization.step8: Obtain neck hemostasis and close without a compressive hematoma around the framework.

## Microlaryngoscopy / Microflap for Benign Vocal-Fold Lesion  (slug: microflap, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-microflap.summary: Benign vocal-fold lesions (polyps, cysts, some nodules) live in the superficial lamina propria, the soft gel layer that lets the cover vibrate. A microflap lifts the epithelium off the lesion through an incision lateral to the vibrating edge, removes the lesion and lays the epithelium back. Voice outcome depends on preserving superficial lamina propria and epithelium and staying out of the vocal ligament.
A-microflap.layer1: Mouth and oropharynx — upper incisors; lips; tongue (lingual nerve on its lateral floor); tongue base; vallecula. Protect teeth with a guard; avoid prolonged heavy suspension pressure on the tongue.
A-microflap.layer2: Supraglottis — epiglottis; aryepiglottic folds; false folds; ventricles. Position the laryngoscope to see the whole membranous fold from the anterior commissure to the vocal process.
A-microflap.layer3: Vocal-fold surface — epithelium (squamous); superior surface; free (vibrating) edge; inferior surface. Palpate the lesion and the contralateral fold. Infusing saline with dilute epinephrine into the superficial lamina propria can separate the layers and show how deep the lesion goes.
A-microflap.layer4: Incision — superior surface, lateral to the lesion. Cut on the superior surface lateral to the lesion, away from the vibrating medial edge.
A-microflap.layer5: Microflap plane — superficial lamina propria (Reinke's space); lesion; vocal ligament (intermediate and deep lamina propria) below. Elevate the epithelium medially within the superficial lamina propria. Stay above the shiny white vocal ligament.
A-microflap.layer6: Lesion removal and redraping — lesion; residual SLP; epithelial flap. Remove the lesion, keep normal SLP, and redrape the epithelium. Trim only redundant epithelium.
A-microflap.landmark1: Vocal ligament — Shiny, white, firm fibers deep to the gel-like superficial lamina propria; it resists dissection (The deep limit of the microflap)
A-microflap.landmark2: Superior arcuate line — The lateral edge of Reinke's space on the superior surface, seen as the line where infused saline stops spreading (Lateral limit of the incision and flap)
A-microflap.landmark3: Anterior commissure — The point where the two folds meet anteriorly, attached to the thyroid cartilage by Broyles ligament (Avoid opposing raw surfaces here (web risk))
A-microflap.landmark4: Vocal process — The posterior end of the membranous fold, white and firm (Posterior end of the vibratory fold)
A-microflap.landmark5: Mid-membranous fold — Midpoint of the membranous fold, between the anterior commissure and vocal process (Usual site of nodules and many polyps (point of greatest contact))
A-microflap.danger1: Vocal ligament — where: Deep to the superficial lamina propria; protect: Stay in the SLP plane; use saline infusion and gentle dissection; injury: Scar, stiff fold, poor voice
A-microflap.danger2: Free edge epithelium — where: Medial vibrating edge; protect: Incise laterally on the superior surface and preserve medial epithelium; injury: Scar and loss of mucosal wave
A-microflap.danger3: Anterior commissure — where: Front of the glottis; protect: Avoid bilateral raw surfaces anteriorly; stage bilateral surgery; injury: Anterior glottic web
A-microflap.danger4: Teeth and tongue — where: Under the laryngoscope; protect: Tooth guard; limit suspension time; injury: Dental injury, tongue numbness or taste change (lingual nerve), tongue edema
A-microflap.variant1: Epidermoid cyst adherent to the vocal ligament
A-microflap.variant2: Sulcus vocalis (invagination down to the ligament)
A-microflap.variant3: Vascular ectasias on the fold that bleed or cause hemorrhagic polyps
A-microflap.variant4: Limited exposure from small mouth, retrognathia or limited neck extension
A-microflap.supply1: Arterial supply — Superior and inferior laryngeal arteries; microvessels run parallel to the free edge
A-microflap.supply2: Sensory — Internal branch of SLN above the folds
A-microflap.supply3: Motor — RLN (TA forms the body of the fold)
A-microflap.pimp1: Q: Name the vocal-fold layers from superficial to deep. A: Epithelium, superficial lamina propria, intermediate lamina propria, deep lamina propria, thyroarytenoid muscle
A-microflap.pimp2: Q: What forms the vocal ligament? A: Intermediate and deep lamina propria
A-microflap.pimp3: Q: Where are nodules usually found? A: Mid-membranous fold (junction of anterior and middle thirds of the full fold)
A-microflap.pimp4: Q: Why incise lateral to the lesion? A: To keep the medial vibrating edge intact
A-microflap.pimp5: Q: What is Broyles ligament? A: The anterior commissure tendon attaching the vocal ligaments to the thyroid cartilage
A-microflap.pimp6: Q: What lies at the anterior and posterior ends of the vocal fold lamina propria? A: The maculae flavae
A-microflap.pimp7: Q: Which nerve is commonly injured by suspension laryngoscopy pressure on the tongue? A: Lingual nerve (tongue numbness, taste change)
OR-microflap.step1: Obtain atraumatic suspension-laryngoscopy exposure with the lesion centered and document the contralateral fold before manipulation.
OR-microflap.step2: Use magnification to define lesion depth and the relationship to epithelium, superficial lamina propria, vocal ligament, and anterior commissure.
OR-microflap.step3: Create the epithelial incision just lateral to the lesion at a site that preserves the vibratory edge and maximizes usable epithelium.
OR-microflap.step4: Elevate the microflap within the superficial lamina propria, separating epithelium from the lesion without entering vocal ligament.
OR-microflap.step5: Dissect/remove the lesion while preserving normal superficial lamina propria and avoiding unnecessary tissue on the medial edge.
OR-microflap.step6: Control pinpoint bleeding with the least traumatic method possible; avoid broad cautery that stiffens the fold.
OR-microflap.step7: Redrape the microflap and trim only clearly redundant/nonviable epithelium so the edge lies smooth without tension.
OR-microflap.step8: Reinspect both folds, document the final contour, and end suspension before pressure injury to tongue/teeth becomes significant.
OR-microflap.step9: Rescue step (only if it occurs): If an airway fire or sustained flash occurs, announce the fire and immediately stop laser/energy delivery. In parallel, anesthesia stops/disconnects oxidizer flow and the breathing circuit as appropriate; promptly remove the burning endotracheal tube and other burning material from the airway when present, and extinguish residual flame in the airway with saline or water. Do not continue the procedure or pause for diagnostic imaging while combustion is ongoing. Once the fire is extinguished, re-establish ventilation, initially avoiding supplemental oxygen/nitrous oxide if the patient's physiology permits and then titrating oxygen to clinical need.

## Rigid Tracheobronchoscopy  (slug: rigid-tracheobronchoscopy, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.summary: Rigid bronchoscopy places a straight ventilating tube from the mouth through the glottis into the trachea and mainstem bronchi, giving airway control, large-channel suction and therapeutic access. Safety depends on protecting the maxillary teeth and arytenoids on entry, keeping orientation to the posterior membranous wall, and knowing what lies outside the wall you are working on: the innominate artery anterior to the lower cervical trachea, the esophagus behind the membranous wall, and the pulmonary arteries around the distal bronchi.
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.layer1: Positioning and oral entry — maxillary incisors (fulcrum risk); lips; tongue; oropharynx. Sniffing/Boyce-Jackson position (neck flexed on chest, head extended on neck) aligns the oral, pharyngeal and laryngeal axes; protect the upper teeth with a guard and the left thumb, never lever on them.
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.layer2: Supraglottis — vallecula; epiglottis (laryngeal surface); aryepiglottic folds; arytenoids. Lift the epiglottis with the bronchoscope tip (or use a laryngoscope first); the arytenoids are posterior and are the structures most often injured on entry.
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.layer3: Glottis — true vocal folds; anterior commissure; posterior glottis / interarytenoid area. Rotate the bevel about 90 degrees so its leading edge lines up with one vocal fold, pass through the glottis, then rotate back to the midline.
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.layer4: Subglottis and cricoid — conus elasticus; cricoid ring (only complete cartilage ring); first tracheal ring. The cricoid cannot expand; an oversized scope here causes mucosal injury and edema.
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.layer5: Trachea — C-shaped cartilage rings (anterior and lateral); posterior membranous wall (trachealis); innominate artery pulsation (anterior, lower cervical/upper thoracic trachea); aortic arch impression (left anterolateral, distal trachea). With the patient supine and the surgeon at the head, the membranous wall is at the bottom of the view and the patient's right is on the surgeon's right.
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.layer6: Carina and mainstem bronchi — main carina (about T4-T5, sternal angle level); right main bronchus (shorter, wider, more vertical); right upper lobe orifice (lateral wall, close to carina); bronchus intermedius; left main bronchus (longer, passes under the aortic arch). Turn the patient's head to the left to align the scope with the right bronchus and to the right for the left bronchus.
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.layer7: Lobar and segmental bronchi — right middle and lower lobe orifices; superior segment orifices of both lower lobes (posterior wall); left upper lobe and lingula; pulmonary arteries adjacent to lobar bronchi. Use telescopes or a flexible scope through the rigid barrel for upper lobe and segmental views; distal biopsy risks pulmonary artery bleeding and pneumothorax.
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.landmark1: Glottic entry — Track the epiglottis tip, lift it, then find the arytenoids posteriorly and the dark glottic chink between the folds; turn the bevel so its edge parallels one fold (Atraumatic passage without arytenoid dislocation or cord laceration)
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.landmark2: Posterior membranous wall — Smooth wall without cartilage rings at the bottom of the view (supine patient, surgeon at head); longitudinal elastic folds are often visible (Fixed orientation reference and the wall most prone to tear)
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.landmark3: Main carina — Sharp vertical ridge at the end of the trachea, roughly 12-15 cm below the cords in adults (the trachea itself is about 10-13 cm); widened or blunted carina suggests subcarinal nodes (Reference point for measuring lesions and choosing bronchus)
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.landmark4: Right upper lobe orifice — On the lateral wall of the right main bronchus within about 1-2.5 cm of the carina; may need an angled telescope (Short right main bronchus makes RUL easy to miss or to occlude)
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.landmark5: Innominate artery — Pulsation on the anterior tracheal wall, generally in the lower cervical/upper thoracic trachea (can ride high, at or above the sternal notch) (Avoid laser, deep debridement or prolonged pressure on this segment)
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.danger1: Maxillary incisors and lips — where: Fulcrum for the bronchoscope; protect: Tooth guard, left-hand support, never lever on the teeth; note loose or capped teeth before induction; injury: Dental fracture, avulsion and aspiration of a tooth, lip laceration
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.danger2: Arytenoids and vocal folds — where: Posterior glottis at entry; protect: Pass under vision with the bevel rotated; do not force a large scope; injury: Arytenoid dislocation, cord laceration, posterior glottic scar
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.danger3: Posterior membranous wall and esophagus — where: Directly posterior to trachea and left main bronchus; protect: Keep scope axis central, no blind advancement, gentle dilation; injury: Tracheal tear, pneumomediastinum, mediastinitis, tracheoesophageal fistula
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.danger4: Innominate artery — where: Crosses the anterior trachea from left to right in the lower neck/upper chest; protect: Limit laser/coring of the anterior wall here; avoid prolonged anterior pressure; injury: Massive hemorrhage (tracheoinnominate fistula pattern)
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.danger5: Pulmonary arteries and visceral pleura — where: Beside lobar and segmental bronchi; protect: Avoid deep distal biopsy; keep forceps in view; low-pressure ventilation; injury: Hemorrhage, pneumothorax
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.danger6: Airway in an oxygen-rich field — where: Entire lumen during laser or electrocautery; protect: Lowest FiO2 tolerated (generally under 30-40%), no combustible material in beam path, saline ready; injury: Airway fire
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.variant1: Tracheal (pig) bronchus: right upper lobe or apical segment bronchus arising from the trachea above the carina
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.variant2: Tracheobronchomalacia with dynamic collapse of the membranous wall
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.variant3: Saber-sheath trachea (narrow transverse diameter, COPD)
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.variant4: Distorted carina or bronchi from mediastinal nodes, goiter or prior resection
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.supply1: Tracheal arterial supply — Segmental lateral branches: inferior thyroid artery to the upper trachea; bronchial arteries to the lower trachea and carina
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.supply2: Bronchial arteries — From the descending aorta or intercostal arteries; source of hemoptysis
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.supply3: Nerves — Vagus/RLN (sensory, cough, bronchoconstriction) and sympathetic trunk
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.supply4: Adjacent great vessels — Innominate artery anterior; aortic arch over the left main bronchus; pulmonary arteries anterior/lateral to bronchi
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.pimp1: Q: Why do aspirated objects favor the right main bronchus in adults? A: It is shorter, wider and more vertical (roughly 20-30 degrees from the tracheal axis versus roughly 40-50 degrees on the left)
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.pimp2: Q: At what vertebral level is the carina? A: About T4-T5, at the sternal angle
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.pimp3: Q: How many segmental bronchi are there on each side? A: Usually 10 on the right; 8-10 on the left because of combined apicoposterior and anteromedial basal segments
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.pimp4: Q: Which structure lies over the left main bronchus? A: The aortic arch, with the esophagus posterior
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.pimp5: Q: How is the trachea supplied, and why does it matter for resection? A: Segmental vessels enter laterally (inferior thyroid above, bronchial below), so circumferential dissection is avoided to prevent devascularization
A-rigid-tracheobronchoscopy.pimp6: Q: What is the only complete cartilaginous ring of the airway? A: The cricoid

## RRP Debridement / Laser-Safety Framework  (slug: rrp-debridement, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-rrp-debridement.summary: Recurrent respiratory papillomatosis is HPV-driven (mostly types 6 and 11) and tends to grow where squamous and ciliated epithelium meet. Surgery removes papilloma to keep the airway open and voice usable, not to cure; safety depends on preserving vocal-fold layers, avoiding anterior commissure webs, and preventing airway fire.
A-rrp-debridement.layer1: Exposure — teeth; tongue base; epiglottis; vallecula. Use atraumatic suspension and tooth guard. Agree with anesthesia on airway: laser-safe tube, jet ventilation or apnea.
A-rrp-debridement.layer2: Supraglottis survey — laryngeal surface of epiglottis; aryepiglottic folds; false folds; ventricles. Map disease at squamociliary junctions: limen vestibuli, laryngeal surface of the epiglottis, and the margins of the ventricles.
A-rrp-debridement.layer3: Glottis — true vocal folds (superior and undersurface); anterior commissure; posterior commissure. True-fold papilloma usually sits in the epithelium and superficial lamina propria. Treat it superficially with microdebrider or angiolytic laser, staying above the vocal ligament, and preserve as much normal epithelium as possible.
A-rrp-debridement.layer4: Anterior commissure — anterior ends of both folds; Broyles ligament; petiole of the epiglottis above. When both sides of the commissure are diseased, clear one side fully and leave the other partly treated, or leave a strip of untreated mucosa at the apex, then return at the next session. Raw opposing surfaces here form a web.
A-rrp-debridement.layer5: Subglottis and trachea — subglottis; trachea; carina; tracheostomy site (if present). Inspect the distal airway with a telescope. Treat only what is safely reachable. Tracheostomy sites and the carina are prone to papilloma; avoid seeding by keeping debris out of the distal airway.
A-rrp-debridement.layer6: Hemostasis and debris clearance — raw mucosal surfaces; loose papilloma fragments. Use topical vasoconstrictor pledgets rather than broad cautery. Suction all fragments so none are aspirated.
A-rrp-debridement.landmark1: Anterior commissure — Front of the glottis where both folds meet (Treat one side or leave a strip of normal mucosa to avoid web)
A-rrp-debridement.landmark2: Vocal ligament — White, firm layer below the gel-like SLP (Deep limit of debridement)
A-rrp-debridement.landmark3: Squamociliary junctions — Limen vestibuli, ventricle margins, laryngeal epiglottis, undersurface of folds (Common disease sites)
A-rrp-debridement.landmark4: Endotracheal tube cuff — Seen in the subglottis or upper trachea (Must be protected from laser with wet cottonoids and saline-filled cuff)
A-rrp-debridement.danger1: Airway (fire) — where: Laser beam path, ETT and oxygen; protect: Low FiO2 (as low as tolerated, about 30% or less), laser-safe tube, saline-filled cuff, wet pledgets; injury: Airway burn
A-rrp-debridement.danger2: Anterior commissure — where: Front of the glottis; protect: Avoid bilateral raw surfaces; injury: Web
A-rrp-debridement.danger3: Vocal ligament — where: Deep to SLP; protect: Superficial debridement only; injury: Scar and dysphonia
A-rrp-debridement.danger4: Airway edema — where: Glottis and subglottis; protect: Minimal thermal injury; consider steroids; injury: Postoperative airway compromise
A-rrp-debridement.variant1: Juvenile-onset RRP is usually more aggressive than adult-onset, with more frequent surgery
A-rrp-debridement.variant2: Distal tracheal or pulmonary spread (more aggressive disease)
A-rrp-debridement.variant3: Tracheostomy site seeding
A-rrp-debridement.supply1: Arterial supply — Superior and inferior laryngeal arteries
A-rrp-debridement.supply2: Sensory — Internal branch of SLN supraglottic; RLN subglottic

A-rrp-debridement.pimp2: Q: Where does papilloma tend to grow? A: At squamociliary junctions
A-rrp-debridement.pimp3: Q: What is the fire triad? A: Oxidizer, fuel, ignition source
A-rrp-debridement.pimp4: Q: CO2 laser wavelength and target? A: 10,600 nm, absorbed by water
A-rrp-debridement.pimp5: Q: KTP laser wavelength and target? A: 532 nm, absorbed by oxyhemoglobin
A-rrp-debridement.pimp6: Q: Why avoid tracheostomy in RRP? A: It may promote distal spread to the stoma and trachea
A-rrp-debridement.pimp7: Q: Which systemic adjuvant is now used for severe RRP? A: Bevacizumab
A-rrp-debridement.pimp8: Q: What staging system is common for RRP? A: Derkay (Coltrera-Derkay) staging
A-rrp-debridement.pimp9: Q: What vaccine is thought to reduce juvenile-onset RRP? A: Quadrivalent or 9-valent HPV vaccine (covers types 6 and 11)
OR-rrp-debridement.step1: Obtain atraumatic suspension microlaryngoscopy and map papilloma burden across supraglottis, true folds, anterior commissure, subglottis, and trachea before treatment.
OR-rrp-debridement.step2: Set laser/energy safety parameters with anesthesia, including reduced FiO2 when appropriate, eye protection, wet pledgets, and laser-safe airway equipment for laser cases.
OR-rrp-debridement.step3: Begin debulking at sites that improve exposure while preserving normal epithelium and the vocal-fold layered structure.
OR-rrp-debridement.step4: Remove papilloma with microdebrider/laser in small controlled increments, avoiding deep injury to vocal ligament, anterior commissure, and opposing raw surfaces.
OR-rrp-debridement.step5: Treat anterior commissure disease conservatively enough to reduce web risk rather than pursuing indiscriminate complete epithelial stripping.
OR-rrp-debridement.step6: Inspect subglottis/trachea for distal disease and treat only lesions safely accessible within the planned procedure.
OR-rrp-debridement.step7: Control bleeding with minimal thermal injury and remove all loose papilloma fragments from the airway.
OR-rrp-debridement.step8: Reinspect the airway and document residual intentional disease, adjunct injection if used, voice/airway plan, and interval for repeat surveillance.
OR-rrp-debridement.step9: Rescue step (only if it occurs): If an airway fire or sustained flash occurs, announce the fire and immediately stop laser/energy delivery. In parallel, anesthesia stops/disconnects oxidizer flow and the breathing circuit as appropriate; promptly remove the burning endotracheal tube and other burning material from the airway when present, and extinguish residual flame in the airway with saline or water. Do not continue the procedure or pause for diagnostic imaging while combustion is ongoing. Once the fire is extinguished, re-establish ventilation, initially avoiding supplemental oxygen/nitrous oxide if the patient's physiology permits and then titrating oxygen to clinical need.

## Tracheoesophageal Puncture  (slug: tep, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-tep.summary: After total laryngectomy the trachea ends at a permanent stoma and the pharynx drains into the esophagus with no connection between them. A tracheoesophageal puncture makes a controlled fistula through the shared party wall so a one-way valve can route lung air into the esophagus, where the pharyngoesophageal segment vibrates as the new voice source. Safety depends on a midline puncture about 5-10 mm below the stomal mucocutaneous junction and on protecting the posterior esophageal wall from the needle.
A-tep.layer1: Stoma survey — mucocutaneous junction of the stoma; posterior (12 o'clock) tracheal wall; stomal size and depth; crusting, granulation, prior TEP tract. Inspect the stoma before scoping. A small or deep stoma limits prosthesis access; a prior tract or prosthesis must be accounted for before a new puncture. A dislodged prosthesis that cannot be found must be assumed aspirated until bronchoscopy shows otherwise.
A-tep.layer2: Esophagoscopy — neopharynx; pharyngoesophageal (PE) segment (cricopharyngeus and lower inferior constrictor remnant); upper cervical esophagus behind the stoma. Pass a rigid esophagoscope (or a flexible scope with transillumination) to the level of the stoma. Rotate the open bevel anteriorly so it tents the party wall forward toward the trachea. The scope's back wall now shields the posterior esophageal wall.
A-tep.layer3: Party wall — posterior tracheal mucosa; membranous trachealis; areolar plane between trachea and esophagus; esophageal muscularis (circular and longitudinal); esophageal mucosa. Transillumination and palpation through the stoma confirm the site. The party wall is usually several millimeters thick and thicker after radiation or with a flap; a puncture that stops between the layers creates a false submucosal tract.
A-tep.layer4: Puncture — midline posterior tracheal wall about 5-10 mm below the stomal mucocutaneous junction; esophageal lumen inside the scope. Pass the needle straight posteriorly through the midline into the visible lumen of the scope, not angled up or down. Too high or too close to the edge risks tract migration into the stoma and extrusion; too low makes the prosthesis hard to reach and clean.
A-tep.layer5: Tract dilation and prosthesis — tracheal flange; esophageal flange; prosthesis shaft length. Pass a wire or catheter retrograde or anterograde as the system requires, dilate only to the prosthesis size, and seat the prosthesis so both flanges are fully deployed. The shaft length must match party-wall thickness; too short tents the tissue and leaks around, too long pistons.
A-tep.layer6: Final check — esophageal flange position; valve opening; periprosthetic seal. Confirm with endoscopy that the esophageal flange is open in the lumen, rotate to check seating, and test for leakage with colored water when awake.
A-tep.landmark1: Mucocutaneous junction at 12 o'clock — The line where stomal skin meets tracheal mucosa on the posterior wall; measure about 5-10 mm down from it in the midline (Standard puncture site that keeps the tract away from the stoma edge and accessible for care)
A-tep.landmark2: Esophageal lumen behind the stoma — Light from the scope transilluminates the posterior tracheal wall; pressing on the tracheal side moves the tip of the scope (Confirms the needle will enter the lumen and not the party-wall plane)
A-tep.landmark3: Pharyngoesophageal segment — The narrowing above the stoma level through which the scope passes, formed by the cricopharyngeus and lower inferior constrictor remnant (This is the vibrating sound source; spasm here causes poor voice (assessed by the insufflation test))
A-tep.landmark4: Midline of the posterior tracheal wall — Center of the flat membranous wall between the horseshoe ends of the last tracheal ring (Lateral punctures move toward the tracheoesophageal groove and are harder to maintain)
A-tep.danger1: Posterior esophageal wall and prevertebral space — where: Directly behind the esophageal lumen, in line with the needle; protect: Turn the esophagoscope bevel so its solid back wall lies behind the puncture site; puncture into a visible lumen; injury: Through-and-through perforation, retroesophageal cellulitis, mediastinitis
A-tep.danger2: Party-wall plane — where: Between the tracheal and esophageal walls; protect: Confirm the needle tip is in the esophageal lumen before dilating; injury: False passage, abscess, failed prosthesis
A-tep.danger3: Stoma edge — where: Above the puncture site; protect: Keep about 5-10 mm below the mucocutaneous junction; injury: Tract migration into the stoma, prosthesis extrusion, enlarged fistula
A-tep.danger4: Tracheal airway — where: Prosthesis and fragments can fall into the trachea; protect: Secure the prosthesis safety strap or inserter; account for every component; injury: Aspirated prosthesis, distal airway obstruction
A-tep.danger5: Recurrent laryngeal nerve remnants and thyroid — where: Laterally in the tracheoesophageal grooves and paratracheal tissue; protect: Stay midline; injury: Bleeding and difficult tract; nerves are no longer functional for voice after laryngectomy
A-tep.variant1: Thick, fibrotic party wall after radiation or with a pectoralis or free flap pharyngeal reconstruction
A-tep.variant2: Gastric pull-up or jejunal interposition reconstruction changes the tissue behind the stoma; puncture needs individual planning
A-tep.variant3: Small or retracted stoma that limits access
A-tep.variant4: Hypertonic PE segment (pharyngoesophageal spasm) that needs myotomy, plexus neurectomy or botulinum toxin for fluent speech
A-tep.supply1: Arterial supply — Inferior thyroid artery branches to the upper trachea and cervical esophagus
A-tep.supply2: Motor to PE segment — Pharyngeal plexus (vagus) to the constrictor remnant; this is the target of plexus neurectomy
A-tep.supply3: Sensory — Vagal branches; stoma skin from cervical plexus (transverse cervical nerve)
A-tep.pimp1: Q: Where is the standard puncture site? A: Midline posterior tracheal wall (12 o'clock) about 5-10 mm below the stomal mucocutaneous junction
A-tep.pimp2: Q: What is the voice source in TEP speech? A: Vibration of the pharyngoesophageal segment driven by pulmonary air
A-tep.pimp3: Q: What does the party wall consist of? A: Posterior membranous tracheal wall and anterior esophageal wall
A-tep.pimp4: Q: What test predicts fluent TE speech before secondary puncture? A: Esophageal insufflation (Taub) test
A-tep.pimp5: Q: Name three treatments for PE-segment spasm. A: Botulinum toxin injection, pharyngeal constrictor myotomy, pharyngeal plexus neurectomy
A-tep.pimp6: Q: Who introduced TEP with a voice prosthesis? A: Singer and Blom (1980)
A-tep.pimp7: Q: Leak through the valve versus around it: what does each suggest? A: Through: valve failure or debris; around: tract enlargement or wrong shaft length
A-tep.pimp8: Q: What muscle forms most of the PE segment? A: Cricopharyngeus with the lower inferior constrictor
OR-tep.step1: Retrieve an aspirated prosthesis under direct bronchoscopic visualization with appropriate foreign-body instruments, then inspect for retained fragments, mucosal injury and distal obstruction before declaring the airway clear. Respiratory distress, near-obstruction, difficult extraction or an unstable stoma airway moves the case to controlled airway/OR rescue rather than repeated blind forceps attempts through the stoma.
OR-tep.step2: After prosthesis extrusion, preserve the mature TEP tract when feasible because it can narrow or close, but tract preservation must not become blind instrumentation. If the tract is clearly identified and the local laryngectomy/SLP protocol supports it, an appropriately sized catheter or stent may be placed by a trained clinician as a temporary bridge until definitive prosthesis replacement; stop if there is resistance, uncertain tract anatomy, bleeding, pain, suspected false passage or tissue breakdown. A catheter is not a universal first-line treatment for every leaking prosthesis, and an unaccounted-for prosthesis must still be evaluated for airway aspiration.
OR-tep.step3: For leakage, first determine whether liquid passes through the valve or around the prosthesis. Through-device leakage suggests valve failure/debris and usually requires cleaning or prosthesis replacement; periprosthetic leakage should trigger assessment of prosthesis length/fit, tract enlargement, granulation/infection, swallowing pressure or stenosis rather than serial empiric upsizing. Significant aspiration, pneumonia/respiratory compromise, inability to maintain oral hydration safely, embedded prosthesis or recurrent tract breakdown warrants prompt ENT/SLP reassessment and definitive refitting or another tract-management strategy rather than accepting chronic aspiration for speech.
OR-tep.step4: Visualize the posterior tracheal wall and anterior esophageal wall with rigid/flexible esophagoscopy so the puncture site is clearly transilluminated/palpated.
OR-tep.step5: Choose a puncture site centered in the posterior tracheal wall with adequate distance from the stoma edge and party wall anatomy.
OR-tep.step6: Pass the puncture needle/catheter into the esophageal lumen under direct visualization rather than blind force.
OR-tep.step7: Guide the wire/catheter through the mouth or use the planned retrograde/anterograde system.
OR-tep.step8: Dilate the tract only to the size required for the prosthesis.
OR-tep.step9: Seat the voice prosthesis with both flanges fully deployed on the tracheal and esophageal sides.
OR-tep.step10: Confirm prosthesis rotation/patency and absence of leakage around or through the device.
OR-tep.step11: Reinspect for bleeding or false passage and document prosthesis type/size.
OR-tep.step12: Rescue step (only if it occurs): If a dislodged prosthesis cannot be physically accounted for, assume aspiration remains possible even when symptoms are mild. Inspect the stoma/TEP and airway, but do not let a normal or equivocal chest radiograph close the evaluation because aspirated prostheses may be radiographically occult. CT can help localize an unaccounted-for device in a stable patient, while flexible or rigid bronchoscopy—selected for airway stability, object position and available expertise—provides definitive airway assessment and retrieval when aspiration is suspected or confirmed.

## Tracheal Resection / Anastomosis  (slug: tracheal-resection, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-tracheal-resection.summary: Tracheal resection removes a stenotic or tumor-involved segment of trachea and rejoins the ends end to end. The trachea's blood supply is segmental and lateral, so the ends are mobilized anteriorly and posteriorly but only minimally around the sides; the RLNs run in the tracheoesophageal grooves and the innominate artery crosses the lower cervical trachea anteriorly.
A-tracheal-resection.layer1: Incision and flaps — skin; platysma; existing stoma (included if present). Low collar incision, with an upper partial sternotomy reserved for lesions low in the trachea.
A-tracheal-resection.layer2: Straps and thyroid — sternohyoid; sternothyroid; thyroid isthmus; pretracheal fascia. Separate the straps in the midline and divide the isthmus so each lobe can be pushed laterally.
A-tracheal-resection.layer3: Anterior tracheal plane — anterior tracheal rings; innominate artery (anterior, lower); pretracheal plane into the mediastinum. Free the anterior trachea with finger dissection down toward the carina, staying directly on the rings, behind the innominate artery.
A-tracheal-resection.layer4: Lesion and level — stenotic or tumor segment; normal rings above and below; cricoid above. Mark the upper and lower limits with needles under bronchoscopy. Encircle the trachea only just below the lesion, staying close to the tracheal wall to spare the RLNs.
A-tracheal-resection.layer5: Transection and cross-field ventilation — distal trachea; membranous wall; esophagus behind. Divide the trachea below the lesion, intubate the distal segment across the field, then resect to healthy edges.
A-tracheal-resection.layer6: Posterior wall and esophagus — membranous trachea; esophagus (posterior, slightly left); tracheoesophageal grooves with RLNs. Separate the membranous wall from the esophagus only along the resected segment and a short distance beyond.
A-tracheal-resection.layer7: Anastomosis and closure — tracheal ends; release maneuvers (suprahyoid, hilar); neck flexion. Place posterior then lateral and anterior sutures with the knots outside the lumen. Interpose tissue (e.g., strap muscle or thyroid) between the anastomosis and innominate artery if they touch.
A-tracheal-resection.landmark1: Cricoid — The firm complete ring at the top of the trachea, below the cricothyroid membrane (The upper limit for counting rings and for tension on the proximal end)
A-tracheal-resection.landmark2: Innominate artery — Pulsation felt on the anterior trachea as the finger enters the upper mediastinum, crossing from left-inferior to right-superior (Must be kept anterior to the dissection finger and separated from the anastomosis)
A-tracheal-resection.landmark3: Tracheoesophageal grooves — Rotate the trachea and look at the angle where its side wall meets the esophagus (RLN location; the dissection should stay on the tracheal wall)
A-tracheal-resection.landmark4: Lesion limits — Bronchoscopy with transillumination and needles passed through the anterior trachea (Sets resection length and avoids over-resection)
A-tracheal-resection.landmark5: Carina — Felt with a finger in the pretracheal plane as the bifurcation at about the level of the sternal angle (Defines how much distal trachea is available)
A-tracheal-resection.danger1: Recurrent laryngeal nerves — where: In or near the tracheoesophageal grooves on both sides; protect: Dissect directly on the tracheal wall and do not search for the nerves; injury: Vocal-fold paralysis, bilateral airway obstruction
A-tracheal-resection.danger2: Lateral tracheal blood supply — where: Segmental vessels entering along the lateral walls; protect: Limit circumferential dissection to about 1-2 cm from each cut end; injury: Ischemia, dehiscence and restenosis
A-tracheal-resection.danger3: Innominate artery — where: Anterior to the lower cervical trachea; protect: Stay on the trachea; interpose tissue if the artery lies on the anastomosis; injury: Tracheoinnominate fistula, hemorrhage
A-tracheal-resection.danger4: Esophagus — where: Directly behind the membranous trachea; protect: Keep a bougie or tube in it; separate gently; injury: Perforation, tracheoesophageal fistula
A-tracheal-resection.danger5: Anastomotic tension — where: Suture line; protect: Release maneuvers rather than more resection or tighter knots; neck flexion postoperatively; injury: Dehiscence, mediastinitis, restenosis
A-tracheal-resection.variant1: Nonrecurrent right inferior laryngeal nerve (with aberrant right subclavian artery)
A-tracheal-resection.variant2: High-riding innominate artery or a bovine arch that brings vessels close to the cervical trachea
A-tracheal-resection.variant3: Short neck, kyphosis or prior sternotomy limiting cervical exposure
A-tracheal-resection.variant4: Tumor (adenoid cystic, squamous) with submucosal spread beyond the visible margin
A-tracheal-resection.supply1: Tracheal supply — Inferior thyroid artery branches (upper), bronchial arteries (lower), plus small contributions from the subclavian, internal thoracic and innominate arteries
A-tracheal-resection.supply2: Motor — RLNs for intrinsic laryngeal muscles
A-tracheal-resection.supply3: Autonomic — Vagal and sympathetic tracheal plexus
A-tracheal-resection.pimp1: Q: How many tracheal rings and how long is the adult trachea? A: About 16-22 rings (sources vary; about 2 rings per cm) over roughly 10-13 cm, averaging about 11 cm
A-tracheal-resection.pimp2: Q: At what level is the carina? A: About the sternal angle (T4-T5)
A-tracheal-resection.pimp3: Q: What is the hilar release? A: Division of the inferior pulmonary ligament and pericardial release around the hilum to gain distal tracheal length
A-tracheal-resection.pimp4: Q: How far can you dissect circumferentially around each end? A: About 1-2 cm, to preserve lateral blood supply
A-tracheal-resection.pimp5: Q: Why put a muscle flap between the anastomosis and innominate artery? A: To reduce the risk of tracheoinnominate fistula
A-tracheal-resection.pimp6: Q: Where does the left RLN loop? A: Under the aortic arch, just behind the ligamentum arteriosum
A-tracheal-resection.pimp7: Q: Where does the right RLN loop? A: Under the right subclavian artery
A-tracheal-resection.pimp8: Q: Which tracheal tumor is known for submucosal and perineural spread? A: Adenoid cystic carcinoma
OR-tracheal-resection.step1: Expose the cervical trachea and map the stenotic segment relative to the cricoid, recurrent laryngeal nerves, thyroid, and innominate region.
OR-tracheal-resection.step2: Mobilize only enough trachea to achieve resection and a low-tension anastomosis, preserving segmental lateral blood supply.
OR-tracheal-resection.step3: Transect below the stenosis and transition to cross-field ventilation when required.
OR-tracheal-resection.step4: Resect the diseased rings to healthy mucosal/cartilage edges, avoiding excessive circumferential devascularization.
OR-tracheal-resection.step5: Place posterior anastomotic sutures first with precise mucosal apposition, then complete lateral and anterior sutures.
OR-tracheal-resection.step6: Use release maneuvers selectively if tension remains rather than over-resecting or tightening the suture line.

## Transnasal Esophagoscopy  (slug: transnasal-esophagoscopy, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.summary: TNE passes an ultrathin flexible scope from the nasal floor through the pharynx, the upper esophageal sphincter and the full esophagus to the gastroesophageal junction in an awake patient. Safety depends on a nontraumatic nasal path and on entering the cricopharyngeus under vision behind the cricoid, lateral to the midline, rather than pushing into the posterior hypopharyngeal wall (Killian dehiscence) or a Zenker pouch.
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.layer1: Nasal passage — nasal vestibule; nasal septum (medial); inferior turbinate (lateral); nasal floor / inferior meatus; middle turbinate (alternate, higher route). Pass along the nasal floor between septum and inferior turbinate; if a spur or narrow floor blocks, use the corridor between the inferior and middle turbinates rather than forcing past a spur.
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.layer2: Nasopharynx — choana; posterior end of inferior turbinate; torus tubarius and Eustachian tube orifice (lateral); fossa of Rosenmuller (posterior to torus); adenoid pad (posterosuperior). Flex the tip inferiorly at the choana to follow the dorsum of the soft palate; the posterior choana is a common site of contact bleeding.
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.layer3: Oropharynx and larynx — dorsum of soft palate; tongue base and vallecula; epiglottis; aryepiglottic folds; true and false vocal folds. Survey the larynx without touching the epiglottis or glottis; contact with the glottis can trigger cough or laryngospasm.
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.layer4: Hypopharynx — piriform sinuses (lateral to AE folds); postcricoid region (anterior wall, behind cricoid lamina); posterior pharyngeal wall. Inspect both piriform apices and the postcricoid area before entering the esophagus; these are blind spots on routine laryngoscopy.
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.layer5: Upper esophageal sphincter — cricopharyngeus (horizontal fibers, roughly C5-C6 level); Killian dehiscence (posterior, above cricopharyngeus); esophageal inlet. Approach the closed sphincter just off midline behind an arytenoid and advance as the patient swallows; never push against the posterior midline wall above the sphincter.
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.layer6: Cervical and thoracic esophagus — cervical esophagus (posterior to trachea); aortic arch impression (left lateral wall, pulsatile); left main bronchus impression (anterior wall, just below the arch); left atrial pulsation (anterior, lower thoracic). Record lesions by distance from the nasal ala; nasal distances run about 5 cm longer on average than the oral (incisor) distances used in GI reports (individual differences vary).
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.layer7: Gastroesophageal junction and stomach — squamocolumnar junction (Z-line); top of gastric folds; diaphragmatic hiatus pinch; proximal stomach / cardia. Compare Z-line, top of gastric folds and hiatal pinch to detect Barrett mucosa or hiatal hernia; retroflexion is limited with some ultrathin scopes.
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.landmark1: Nasal floor corridor — Keep the scope low, with the septum medial and the inferior turbinate lateral; the choana opens as a dark arch with the vomer in the midline (The widest, least vascular path and the best predictor of a comfortable pass)
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.landmark2: Esophageal inlet — Follow the posterior surface of an arytenoid into the postcricoid area just off midline; the cricopharyngeus appears as a closed horizontal slit that opens on swallow (Entering under vision avoids the Killian dehiscence and a Zenker pouch)
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.landmark3: Zenker diverticulum — A pouch in the posterior midline above the sphincter; the true lumen lies anterior to the pouch septum (the cricopharyngeal bar) (The scope tends to slide into the pouch, where perforation risk is highest)
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.landmark4: Aortic arch indentation — Pulsatile extrinsic bulge on the left lateral wall in the upper thoracic esophagus, roughly 22-27 cm from the incisors (Second physiologic narrowing; avoid deep biopsy of pulsatile extrinsic compression)
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.landmark5: Squamocolumnar junction (Z-line) — Pale pink squamous mucosa meets salmon red columnar mucosa near the hiatal pinch (sniff test helps); normally at or near the top of the gastric folds (Displacement above the gastric folds suggests Barrett esophagus; separation from the hiatus suggests hiatal hernia)
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.danger1: Killian dehiscence / posterior hypopharyngeal wall — where: Posterior midline triangle between the oblique thyropharyngeus and the horizontal cricopharyngeus; protect: Enter the inlet off midline under vision during a swallow; never advance against resistance; injury: Cervical esophageal perforation, neck abscess, mediastinitis
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.danger2: Nasal mucosa (septal spurs, inferior turbinate) — where: Lateral and medial walls of the nasal floor corridor; septal spurs at the maxillary crest; protect: Topical decongestion and anesthesia, small scope, choose the wider side, steer around spurs; injury: Epistaxis, pain, aborted study
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.danger3: Glottis — where: Anterior to the esophageal inlet; protect: Avoid touching the epiglottis and cords; limit topical anesthetic volume; injury: Laryngospasm, cough, aspiration of anesthetic
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.danger4: Thoracic esophagus beside aorta and left atrium — where: Upper thoracic esophagus at the arch; lower thoracic esophagus behind the left atrium; protect: Minimal insufflation, no blind force, no deep biopsy into pulsatile extrinsic masses; injury: Perforation, mediastinitis, rarely major hemorrhage
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.danger5: Esophageal or gastric varices — where: Distal esophagus and cardia (portosystemic anastomosis via left gastric vein); protect: Recognize bluish tortuous columns; do not biopsy; injury: Major hemorrhage
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.variant1: Septal deviation or spur narrowing one nasal floor
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.variant2: Cricopharyngeal bar or Zenker diverticulum (posterior pulsion pouch through Killian dehiscence)
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.variant3: Cervical osteophytes (e.g., DISH) indenting the posterior hypopharynx
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.variant4: Aberrant right subclavian artery passing posterior to the esophagus (posterior extrinsic compression, dysphagia lusoria)
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.variant5: Hiatal hernia with the Z-line displaced above the diaphragmatic pinch
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.supply1: Arterial supply of esophagus — Cervical: inferior thyroid artery; thoracic: esophageal branches of the aorta and bronchial arteries; abdominal: left gastric and inferior phrenic arteries
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.supply2: Venous drainage — Inferior thyroid veins; azygos/hemiazygos; left gastric vein to portal system (site of varices)
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.supply3: Motor innervation — Vagus: pharyngeal plexus and recurrent laryngeal nerves to the cricopharyngeus and upper striated esophagus; vagal esophageal plexus to distal smooth muscle
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.supply4: Nasal sensation — Anterior ethmoidal nerve (V1) anterosuperiorly; nasopalatine and posterior nasal branches of V2 via the pterygopalatine ganglion
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.pimp1: Q: What are the three physiologic narrowings of the esophagus? A: Cricopharyngeus (about 15 cm from incisors), aortic arch/left main bronchus crossing (about 22-27 cm), and the diaphragmatic hiatus/LES (about 38-40 cm)
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.pimp2: Q: What are the boundaries of the Killian dehiscence? A: Oblique fibers of the thyropharyngeus (inferior constrictor) above and the horizontal cricopharyngeus below, in the posterior midline
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.pimp3: Q: Where is the Killian-Jamieson area? A: Anterolateral wall of the proximal cervical esophagus just below the cricopharyngeus, lateral to the longitudinal muscle, near where the RLN enters the larynx
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.pimp4: Q: Where is the Laimer (Laimer-Haeckermann) triangle? A: Posterior wall below the cricopharyngeus, between it and the diverging longitudinal esophageal muscle
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.pimp5: Q: What is the muscle type along the esophagus? A: Upper third mostly striated, middle third mixed, distal third smooth muscle
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.pimp6: Q: Why does an esophageal perforation spread quickly? A: The esophagus has no serosa (only adventitia), so infection tracks directly into the retrovisceral space and mediastinum
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.pimp7: Q: What does the Z-line represent? A: The squamocolumnar junction between esophageal squamous and gastric columnar epithelium
A-transnasal-esophagoscopy.pimp8: Q: What vascular anomaly causes posterior extrinsic compression of the esophagus? A: Aberrant right subclavian artery (arteria lusoria) passing posterior to the esophagus
OR-transnasal-esophagoscopy.step1: Topically anesthetize/decongest the selected nasal cavity and pass the flexible scope along the nasal floor while avoiding turbinate trauma.
OR-transnasal-esophagoscopy.step2: Inspect nasopharynx, oropharynx, hypopharynx, and larynx during advancement, then enter the esophageal inlet under direct visualization rather than blind pressure.
OR-transnasal-esophagoscopy.step3: Advance through cervical and thoracic esophagus while insufflating only enough for mucosal inspection and documenting lesions/strictures by level.
OR-transnasal-esophagoscopy.step4: Traverse the gastroesophageal junction when the indication requires and inspect the proximal stomach before withdrawal.
OR-transnasal-esophagoscopy.step5: Obtain biopsies under direct vision with the scope stabilized and avoid sampling immediately adjacent to major extrinsic vascular compression or suspected perforation.
OR-transnasal-esophagoscopy.step6: For dilation, pass guidewire/dilator only after the lumen and stricture axis are clearly defined and use incremental sizing rather than force.
OR-transnasal-esophagoscopy.step7: Withdraw slowly with a second circumferential inspection for bleeding, mucosal tear, or missed pathology.
OR-transnasal-esophagoscopy.step8: Document findings, biopsy sites, dilation size if performed, and postprocedure warning signs for perforation or bleeding.

## Endoscopic Zenker Treatment  (slug: zenker, region: Laryngology / Voice / Swallowing)

A-zenker.summary: A Zenker diverticulum is a pulsion pseudodiverticulum of mucosa and submucosa herniating through Killian's dehiscence, the posterior weak area between the thyropharyngeus and cricopharyngeus, above a noncompliant cricopharyngeus. Endoscopic treatment divides the party wall (the septum containing the cricopharyngeus) to make the pouch and esophagus one channel; safety depends on staying in the midline of the septum and stopping before the cut breaches the apex into the retrovisceral space and mediastinum.
A-zenker.layer1: Oral cavity and hypopharynx exposure — upper incisors; tongue base; posterior pharyngeal wall; postcricoid area; cervical spine (behind). Protect the teeth and extend the neck as far as the spine allows. Pass the diverticuloscope behind the larynx along the posterior pharyngeal wall.
A-zenker.layer2: Two lumens — esophageal lumen (anterior, behind the cricoid); diverticulum (posterior, usually blind pouch); septum (party wall) between them. Place one lip of the diverticuloscope in the esophagus (anterior) and the other in the pouch (posterior), so the septum lies horizontally in the scope. The esophagus is the anterior opening; the pouch is posterior and often holds retained food.
A-zenker.layer3: Septum (party wall) — esophageal mucosa (anterior surface); cricopharyngeus muscle (horizontal fibers); diverticular mucosa (posterior surface). The septum contains the cricopharyngeus. Its free edge is the upper border of the muscle. Confirm the septal depth to the pouch floor before cutting.
A-zenker.layer4: Septal division — midline of the septum; lateral walls of the pouch and esophagus. Cut exactly in the midline with stapler, CO2 laser or flexible knife, advancing down toward the bottom of the pouch. Lateral deviation enters the parapharyngeal and retrovisceral tissue of the neck.
A-zenker.layer5: Apex of the pouch — diverticular floor; retrovisceral (retroesophageal) space behind; upper mediastinum below. Stop the division a few millimeters above the pouch floor. A stapler leaves a residual spur at its tip because the anvil and staples do not reach the end; traction sutures or laser can reduce it.
A-zenker.layer6: Inspection — divided muscle edges; common lumen; remaining spur. Check for bleeding, gaps in the staple line, mucosal tears and residual muscle bridge.
A-zenker.landmark1: Esophageal lumen — The anterior, collapsible opening directly behind the cricoid; it continues distally and accepts a bougie or nasogastric tube (Confirms which side of the septum is the true esophagus before cutting)
A-zenker.landmark2: Diverticular pouch — The posterior blind opening, often with retained food or secretions, ending in a smooth fundus (The septum lies between it and the esophagus)
A-zenker.landmark3: Cricopharyngeal bar — The firm horizontal muscle at the free edge of the septum, felt as resistance when the scope lips are spread (This is the muscle the procedure divides)
A-zenker.landmark4: Pouch floor — Measure depth from the septal edge with the scope or a ruler; compare with preoperative swallow study (Sets the safe length of division and predicts whether a stapler will fit (very small pouches are hard to staple))
A-zenker.danger1: Retrovisceral space and mediastinum — where: Behind and below the pouch floor; the retropharyngeal/retrovisceral space continues into the posterior mediastinum; protect: Stop above the floor, stay in the midline, test for leak, and keep a low threshold for contrast study; injury: Cervical abscess, mediastinitis
A-zenker.danger2: Lateral pharyngeal and esophageal wall — where: On each side of the septum; protect: Keep the septum centered in the scope and the cut in the midline; injury: Lateral perforation into the neck; RLN at risk in the tracheoesophageal groove
A-zenker.danger3: Teeth and cervical spine — where: Fulcrum of the rigid diverticuloscope; protect: Use a tooth guard; assess neck extension and mouth opening preoperatively; convert to open or flexible technique if exposure is poor; injury: Dental fracture, lip injury, cervical spine injury
A-zenker.danger4: Septal vessels — where: Small branches from the inferior thyroid artery run in the cricopharyngeus and submucosa; protect: Staple or coagulate before cutting; inspect edges; injury: Bleeding
A-zenker.variant1: Diverticulum most often projects to the left of midline
A-zenker.variant2: Killian-Jamieson diverticulum: arises from the anterolateral cervical esophagus below the cricopharyngeus near the RLN entry; not suitable for septal division
A-zenker.variant3: Small pouch (under about 2-3 cm): limited room for a stapler, and lower chance of symptom relief with stapling alone
A-zenker.variant4: Coexisting cervical osteophytes or limited neck extension that prevents rigid exposure
A-zenker.supply1: Arterial supply — Inferior thyroid artery branches to the cricopharyngeus and upper esophagus
A-zenker.supply2: Motor — Pharyngeal plexus (vagus) and RLN branches to the cricopharyngeus
A-zenker.supply3: Sensory — Vagal branches (pharyngeal plexus, RLN)
A-zenker.pimp1: Q: What are the boundaries of Killian's triangle? A: The oblique thyropharyngeus fibers above and the transverse cricopharyngeus fibers below, posteriorly
A-zenker.pimp2: Q: What layers make up a Zenker diverticulum wall? A: Mucosa and submucosa only (a pseudodiverticulum)
A-zenker.pimp3: Q: Where does a Killian-Jamieson diverticulum arise? A: Anterolateral cervical esophagus below the cricopharyngeus, lateral to the longitudinal esophageal muscle
A-zenker.pimp4: Q: What is Laimer's triangle? A: The area below the cricopharyngeus bounded by the longitudinal esophageal muscle
A-zenker.pimp5: Q: What does the septum (party wall) contain? A: The cricopharyngeus muscle between esophageal and pouch mucosa
A-zenker.pimp6: Q: Why does a stapler leave a residual spur? A: The anvil tip extends beyond the last staples, so the distal septum is not divided
A-zenker.pimp7: Q: Which side does a Zenker usually project? A: Left
A-zenker.pimp8: Q: Which nerve supplies the cricopharyngeus? A: Vagal fibers via the pharyngeal plexus and RLN
OR-zenker.step1: Expose the diverticular septum with the chosen rigid diverticuloscope or flexible platform and clearly identify the esophageal lumen versus pouch.
OR-zenker.step2: Center the common wall containing the cricopharyngeus and ensure adequate visualization to the distal end of the septum before dividing it.
OR-zenker.step3: Divide the septum/cricopharyngeal muscle with stapler, laser, or endoscopic knife in a controlled distal progression.
OR-zenker.step4: Maintain the cut in the midline of the septum to avoid lateral perforation into the neck.
OR-zenker.step5: Extend the myotomy adequately to relieve the functional obstruction while stopping before an uncontrolled mediastinal entry.
OR-zenker.step6: Inspect the divided edges for bleeding and for a residual muscular bridge that would cause persistent symptoms.
OR-zenker.step7: Confirm a common channel between pouch and esophagus without obvious perforation.
OR-zenker.step8: Set postoperative diet and perforation-monitoring strategy based on technique and intraoperative findings.

## Direct Laryngoscopy / Bronchoscopy  (slug: DLB, region: Pediatric Otolaryngology)

A-DLB.summary: Pediatric DLB is a shared-airway survey from the supraglottis to the bronchi, done under spontaneous or controlled ventilation. The anatomy that decides safety is the small, nonexpandable cricoid ring (risk of edema with oversized instruments), the posterior glottis where clefts and interarytenoid scar hide, and in tracheotomized children the suprastomal segment and stoma where granulation and collapse occur.
A-DLB.layer1: Oral entry — lips; alveolar ridges or primary teeth; tongue; vallecula. Protect gums and teeth; lift along the laryngoscope axis. A large, posteriorly placed tongue (micrognathia, macroglossia) narrows the view.
A-DLB.layer2: Supraglottis — epiglottis; aryepiglottic folds; arytenoids; false folds and ventricles. Document dynamic collapse with spontaneous breathing before suspension changes the anatomy.
A-DLB.layer3: Glottis — true vocal folds; anterior commissure; vocal processes; posterior commissure / interarytenoid area. Evaluate mobility in light anesthesia; probe the interarytenoid area for cleft and the arytenoids for fixation versus paralysis.
A-DLB.layer4: Subglottis — conus elasticus; cricoid ring; first tracheal ring. Measure lesion distance below the vocal folds and its length; note whether the stenosis is cartilaginous (firm) or soft.
A-DLB.layer5: Trachea and tracheostomy region — suprastomal anterior wall; tracheostomy stoma; tracheal rings; posterior membranous wall. In tracheotomized children, remove or pass beside the tube to see suprastomal collapse and granulation and the tube tip site.
A-DLB.layer6: Carina and bronchi — main carina; right and left main bronchi; lobar orifices. Look for malacia, compression and secretions; avoid pushing the telescope into small bronchi.
A-DLB.layer7: Sizing — cricoid lumen; endotracheal tube outer diameter. Use the largest tube that leaks at a standard pressure (10-25 cm H2O in the Myer-Cotton system; many centers use about 20 cm H2O); express the result as a Myer-Cotton grade.
A-DLB.landmark1: Vocal folds as the zero point — Use the free edge of the true folds as the reference and measure distances along the telescope or tube to the lesion and the carina (Reproducible levels for planning reconstruction)
A-DLB.landmark2: Cricoid ring — Complete ring just below the conus; firm, round narrowing that does not change with respiration (Site of congenital and acquired subglottic stenosis)
A-DLB.landmark3: Posterior glottis — Spread or probe the arytenoids to expose the interarytenoid notch and posterior commissure; check passive arytenoid mobility by palpation (Distinguishes posterior glottic scar or fixation from paralysis and finds clefts)
A-DLB.landmark4: Tracheostomy suprastomal segment — Anterior tracheal wall just above the stoma, seen from above with the tube withdrawn or from the stoma upward (Suprastomal collapse and granulation affect decannulation)
A-DLB.danger1: Teeth, alveolar ridges, lips — where: Laryngoscope fulcrum; protect: Guard and correct lifting technique; injury: Dental and soft tissue injury
A-DLB.danger2: Glottis in light anesthesia — where: Larynx; protect: Topical anesthetic at an accepted weight-based dose, adequate depth, coordinated ventilation; injury: Laryngospasm, desaturation
A-DLB.danger3: Subglottic mucosa — where: Within the cricoid ring; protect: Age-appropriate instruments, stop sizing at leak, no forced tubes; injury: Postoperative edema, stridor, iatrogenic stenosis
A-DLB.danger4: Arytenoids — where: Posterior glottis; protect: Gentle telescope passage; injury: Dislocation, scar
A-DLB.danger5: Small bronchi and membranous wall — where: Distal airway; protect: Under-vision movement only; injury: Perforation, pneumothorax
A-DLB.variant1: Laryngeal cleft or posterior glottic stenosis presenting as aspiration or stridor
A-DLB.variant2: Bilateral vocal fold paralysis (check for Arnold-Chiari or other neurologic cause)
A-DLB.variant3: Complete tracheal rings, tracheal bronchus, vascular compression
A-DLB.variant4: Subglottic cyst or hemangioma
A-DLB.supply1: Laryngeal sensation — Internal SLN above the folds; RLN below the folds
A-DLB.supply2: Laryngeal motor — RLN to all intrinsic muscles except the cricothyroid (external SLN)
A-DLB.supply3: Arterial supply — Superior laryngeal artery (superior thyroid) and inferior laryngeal artery (inferior thyroid); tracheal branches from inferior thyroid and bronchial arteries
A-DLB.pimp1: Q: What is the narrowest point of the pediatric airway, and why does it matter? A: The subglottis at the cricoid; it is a complete ring, so edema cannot be accommodated by expansion
A-DLB.pimp2: Q: What uncuffed ETT size formula is used for children over about 2 years? A: Internal diameter (mm) = age/4 + 4
A-DLB.pimp3: Q: What grades make up the Myer-Cotton scale? A: I 0-50%, II 51-70%, III 71-99%, IV no lumen
A-DLB.pimp4: Q: Which intrinsic muscle abducts the vocal folds? A: Posterior cricoarytenoid
A-DLB.pimp5: Q: How do you separate arytenoid fixation from paralysis at DLB? A: Palpate the arytenoid: a paralyzed arytenoid moves passively, a fixed joint or posterior glottic scar does not
A-DLB.pimp6: Q: What is suprastomal collapse? A: Anterior tracheal wall collapse just above a tracheostomy stoma from cartilage weakening
A-DLB.pimp7: Q: Which nerve supplies sensation to the subglottis? A: The recurrent laryngeal nerve
OR-DLB.step1: Coordinate spontaneous versus controlled ventilation and perform atraumatic direct laryngoscopy with the larynx fully exposed before sizing or instrumentation.
OR-DLB.step2: Document supraglottis, glottis, posterior commissure, subglottis, and trachea systematically, including dynamic findings when relevant.
OR-DLB.step3: Pass the telescope through the cords under direct vision and examine to the carina/bronchi when bronchoscopy is part of the case.
OR-DLB.step4: Size the airway with calibrated endotracheal tubes only when clinically needed, using a standardized leak-pressure method rather than forcing a tight tube.
OR-DLB.step5: Measure stenosis location, length, diameter, and relationship to the vocal folds/cricoid/tracheostomy.
OR-DLB.step6: Perform any biopsy, balloon dilation, scar division, or granulation removal only after the diagnostic anatomy is documented.
OR-DLB.step7: Reinspect the airway after intervention for bleeding, mucosal injury, edema, and lumen gain.
OR-DLB.step8: Define extubation versus postoperative airway support before leaving the OR.

## Adenoidectomy  (slug: adenoidectomy, region: Pediatric Otolaryngology)

A-adenoidectomy.summary: Adenoidectomy clears lymphoid tissue from the nasopharynx to open the choanae and the Eustachian tube orifices. Safety depends on staying in the midline posterior wall, preserving the torus tubarius laterally and leaving an inferior band in children at risk of velopharyngeal insufficiency (submucous cleft, neuromuscular disease).
A-adenoidectomy.layer1: Gag and palate check — hard palate; soft palate; uvula. Look for bifid uvula and zona pellucida and palpate for a notch in the posterior hard palate (submucous cleft) before removing tissue.
A-adenoidectomy.layer2: Palatal retraction — soft palate; red rubber catheters through the nose. Retract the soft palate anteriorly with catheters; avoid tearing the palate.
A-adenoidectomy.layer3: Nasopharyngeal survey — choanae; vomer posterior edge; adenoid pad (posterosuperior wall); torus tubarius; Eustachian tube orifices; fossae of Rosenmuller. Use a mirror or endoscope to see the full pad and both tori before starting.
A-adenoidectomy.layer4: Central removal — adenoid pad; pharyngobasilar fascia / prevertebral fascia (deep); longus capitis muscle (deep). Remove tissue in the midline from the choanae downward; stay superficial to the prevertebral fascia.
A-adenoidectomy.layer5: Lateral and inferior limits — torus tubarius; salpingopharyngeal fold; Passavant ridge region; inferior adenoid band. Spare the torus and its mucosa; leave an inferior band of tissue when VPI risk exists.
A-adenoidectomy.layer6: Hemostasis and inspection — adenoid bed; choanae; posterior nasal cavity. Suction cautery or packs; remove every pack; check choanal patency.
A-adenoidectomy.landmark1: Torus tubarius — Cartilaginous ridge on the lateral nasopharyngeal wall just behind the inferior turbinate's posterior end, framing the Eustachian tube orifice (Lateral limit of resection)
A-adenoidectomy.landmark2: Fossa of Rosenmuller — Recess posterior and superior to the torus (Residual lateral tissue may persist here; deep work risks the ET and parapharyngeal space)
A-adenoidectomy.landmark3: Choanae and vomer — Paired openings separated by the vomer's posterior edge in the midline anteriorly (Endpoint of airway clearance)
A-adenoidectomy.landmark4: Prevertebral plane — Smooth firm surface deep to the adenoid bed overlying the atlas; the anterior arch of C1 is palpable inferiorly (Do not cauterize deeply here)
A-adenoidectomy.danger1: Eustachian tube orifice and torus — where: Lateral nasopharyngeal walls; protect: Keep instruments medial to the torus; limited lateral cautery; injury: Scarring, ET dysfunction, otitis media
A-adenoidectomy.danger2: Velopharyngeal valve — where: Inferior nasopharynx at the level of palatal closure; protect: Assess for submucous cleft; leave an inferior band or do partial (superior) adenoidectomy; injury: Hypernasality, nasal regurgitation (VPI)
A-adenoidectomy.danger3: Prevertebral fascia and C1-C2 — where: Deep to the adenoid bed; protect: Avoid deep curettage and extensive cautery; caution with neck extension in Down syndrome; injury: Grisel syndrome, cervical instability
A-adenoidectomy.danger4: Soft palate — where: Retracted anteriorly; protect: Gentle retraction, avoid thermal injury; injury: Palatal injury, nasopharyngeal stenosis (more with concurrent tonsillectomy)
A-adenoidectomy.danger5: Aberrant internal carotid — where: Rarely medialized near the lateral nasopharyngeal wall; protect: Look for pulsation; stay midline; injury: Hemorrhage
A-adenoidectomy.variant1: Submucous cleft palate (Calnan triad: bifid uvula, zona pellucida, notched posterior hard palate)
A-adenoidectomy.variant2: Thornwaldt cyst in the midline (notochordal remnant)
A-adenoidectomy.variant3: Choanal stenosis or atresia
A-adenoidectomy.variant4: Large tubal tonsils near the torus
A-adenoidectomy.supply1: Arterial supply — Ascending pharyngeal artery, ascending palatine artery, pharyngeal branch of the maxillary artery, artery of the pterygoid canal, tonsillar branch of facial
A-adenoidectomy.supply2: Venous — Pharyngeal venous plexus
A-adenoidectomy.supply3: Sensory — Pharyngeal branch of V2 (via the pterygopalatine ganglion) and glossopharyngeal nerve
A-adenoidectomy.supply4: Lymphatics — Retropharyngeal (node of Rouviere) and level II nodes
A-adenoidectomy.pimp1: Q: What are the boundaries of the nasopharynx? A: Anterior choanae; superior/posterior basisphenoid, basiocciput and C1; lateral Eustachian tube openings and fossae of Rosenmuller; inferior soft palate
A-adenoidectomy.pimp2: Q: What is the Calnan triad? A: Bifid uvula, zona pellucida, notched posterior hard palate (submucous cleft)
A-adenoidectomy.pimp3: Q: Which muscle opens the Eustachian tube? A: Tensor veli palatini (CN V3), assisted by levator veli palatini (vagus via pharyngeal plexus)
A-adenoidectomy.pimp4: Q: What is a Thornwaldt cyst? A: Midline nasopharyngeal cyst from a notochordal remnant
A-adenoidectomy.pimp5: Q: What is Passavant ridge? A: A bulge of the posterior pharyngeal wall during velopharyngeal closure formed by superior constrictor/palatopharyngeus fibers
A-adenoidectomy.pimp6: Q: What is the first-echelon node draining the nasopharynx? A: Lateral retropharyngeal node (node of Rouviere)
A-adenoidectomy.pimp7: Q: What is Grisel syndrome? A: Atlantoaxial subluxation after inflammation of the pharynx, including after adenoidectomy
OR-adenoidectomy.step1: Insert the mouth gag, palpate/inspect the palate for submucous cleft risk, and retract the soft palate atraumatically.
OR-adenoidectomy.step2: Visualize the adenoid pad, choanae, vomer, and both Eustachian-tube orifices with mirror or endoscope before removal.
OR-adenoidectomy.step3: Remove the central obstructing adenoid tissue from superior to inferior using the planned curette/microdebrider/suction cautery technique.
OR-adenoidectomy.step4: Preserve tissue around the torus tubarius and avoid deep lateral dissection that could scar the Eustachian tube.
OR-adenoidectomy.step5: Avoid an excessively aggressive inferior resection when velopharyngeal competence is at risk.
OR-adenoidectomy.step6: Inspect the nasopharynx endoscopically/mirror for residual choanal obstruction and focal bleeding.
OR-adenoidectomy.step7: Control bleeding with targeted cautery/packing and then remove all packing.
OR-adenoidectomy.step8: Recheck the oral cavity/nasopharynx and release suspension before emergence.

## Rigid Bronchoscopy for Airway Foreign Body  (slug: airway-fb, region: Pediatric Otolaryngology)

A-airway-fb.summary: Rigid bronchoscopy in children passes a ventilating scope through a small, high larynx into a short trachea to retrieve aspirated objects, most often from a main bronchus. The anatomy that decides safety is the size of the subglottis relative to the object, and the fact that an object lost at the glottis or subglottis can block the whole airway; if that happens, push it back into a main bronchus so one lung can be ventilated.
A-airway-fb.layer1: Oral entry and laryngeal exposure — lips and teeth; tongue; vallecula; epiglottis; arytenoids. Expose the larynx with a laryngoscope; check for a laryngeal or supraglottic object before passing the bronchoscope.
A-airway-fb.layer2: Glottic passage — true vocal folds; posterior glottis. Rotate the bronchoscope bevel to align with a fold, pass under vision, then return to midline.
A-airway-fb.layer3: Subglottis and trachea — cricoid ring; tracheal rings; posterior membranous wall. The cricoid limits both scope size and the size of object that can be withdrawn through it.
A-airway-fb.layer4: Carina survey — main carina; right main bronchus; left main bronchus. Survey both sides, starting with the uninvolved side when possible, before manipulating the object; note edema, granulation and pus.
A-airway-fb.layer5: Object capture — object; bronchial wall; granulation tissue; distal secretions. Use optical forceps matched to the object; grasp the leading edge and protect the wall from sharp edges.
A-airway-fb.layer6: Withdrawal through the glottis — bronchoscope tip; subglottis; glottis. Withdraw object, forceps and scope as a unit when the object is larger than the scope lumen; the glottis is where objects are stripped off.
A-airway-fb.layer7: Second look — both bronchial trees; lobar orifices; mucosa. Reinsert to check for additional fragments, retained pieces (especially nuts), bleeding and distal secretions.
A-airway-fb.landmark1: Main carina — Sharp ridge at the end of the trachea; in a supine child with the surgeon at the head, the right bronchus is on the surgeon's right (Starting point to assign the object side)
A-airway-fb.landmark2: Right main bronchus — Turn the head to the left to align the scope; the right upper lobe orifice sits on the lateral wall just beyond the carina (Common site of aspirated objects)
A-airway-fb.landmark3: Left main bronchus — Turn the head to the right; longer bronchus passing under the aortic arch before dividing into upper and lower lobe bronchi (Objects here may need a longer scope or telescope)
A-airway-fb.landmark4: Subglottis — Complete cricoid ring a short distance below the vocal folds (Narrowest point; a dropped object here causes complete obstruction)
A-airway-fb.danger1: Subglottis / glottis during withdrawal — where: Cricoid ring and vocal folds; protect: Withdraw as a unit; if the object drops and obstructs, push it distally into a main bronchus and ventilate; injury: Complete airway obstruction, cardiac arrest
A-airway-fb.danger2: Bronchial wall — where: Around the impacted object, often inflamed with granulation; protect: Gentle grasp, no blind forceps closure, control granulation bleeding; injury: Perforation, pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, bleeding
A-airway-fb.danger3: Distal airway — where: Beyond the object; protect: Avoid pushing the object deeper; use telescopes for view; injury: Object migration to a smaller bronchus, lobar collapse
A-airway-fb.danger4: Teeth and arytenoids — where: Entry route; protect: Guard, gentle passage; injury: Dental injury, arytenoid dislocation
A-airway-fb.danger5: Laryngeal and subglottic mucosa — where: Within the cricoid; protect: Appropriate scope size and limited passes; steroids if edema; injury: Postoperative stridor and edema
A-airway-fb.variant1: Bilateral or multiple foreign bodies (fragmenting nuts, seeds)
A-airway-fb.variant2: Laryngeal or tracheal objects, which carry the highest risk of complete obstruction
A-airway-fb.variant3: Tracheal bronchus or other branching variants altering the expected route
A-airway-fb.variant4: Chronic retained object with granulation, stricture or bronchiectasis
A-airway-fb.supply1: Bronchial arteries — From the descending aorta or intercostal arteries; bleeding from inflamed mucosa
A-airway-fb.supply2: Airway sensation — Internal SLN above the folds; RLN and vagal pulmonary branches below (cough reflex)
A-airway-fb.supply3: Adjacent great vessels — Innominate artery anterior to the distal trachea; aortic arch over the left main bronchus; pulmonary arteries beside the lobar bronchi
A-airway-fb.pimp1: Q: What do you do if a foreign body drops and obstructs the subglottis during removal? A: Push it back into a main bronchus to restore ventilation of at least one lung, then retry
A-airway-fb.pimp2: Q: Which side do airway foreign bodies favor in children? A: Right main bronchus overall, though the difference is smaller in young children than in adults
A-airway-fb.pimp3: Q: What radiographic sign suggests a bronchial foreign body? A: Air trapping (hyperinflation) on the affected side on expiratory or decubitus films
A-airway-fb.pimp4: Q: Why are peanuts particularly harmful? A: They fragment and their oils cause marked mucosal inflammation
A-airway-fb.pimp5: Q: What does a rigid bronchoscope size number indicate? A: A nominal size that equals neither the internal nor the outer diameter (e.g., a Storz 3.5 has about 5.0 mm ID and 5.7 mm OD); check the manufacturer's chart against the child's age
A-airway-fb.pimp6: Q: Which way do you turn the head to enter the right main bronchus? A: To the left
A-airway-fb.pimp7: Q: At what level is the carina in an adult, and how long is the trachea? A: About T4-T5; the adult trachea is about 10-13 cm long
OR-airway-fb.step1: Coordinate a shared-airway anesthetic that preserves oxygenation while allowing rigid bronchoscopy; have age-appropriate rescue bronchoscopes/forceps immediately available.
OR-airway-fb.step2: Perform direct laryngoscopy and pass the rigid bronchoscope under direct vision through the glottis.
OR-airway-fb.step3: Survey trachea and both main bronchi before manipulating the object so its location and distal airway are understood.
OR-airway-fb.step4: Choose forceps matched to the object's shape and grasp it securely under direct vision rather than pushing it distally.
OR-airway-fb.step5: Withdraw foreign body and bronchoscope as a controlled unit when necessary to keep the object secured through the glottis.
OR-airway-fb.step6: Reinsert the bronchoscope to inspect for a second object, mucosal injury, granulation, bleeding, and distal secretions.
OR-airway-fb.step7: Suction obstructing secretions and treat airway edema/bronchospasm as required.
OR-airway-fb.step8: Confirm bilateral ventilation and set postoperative observation based on hypoxia, pneumonia, edema, or difficult extraction.

## Branchial Cleft Anomaly Excision  (slug: branchial, region: Pediatric Otolaryngology)

A-branchial.summary: Branchial anomalies follow the course of their arch: each tract runs deep to the derivatives of its own arch and superficial to those of the next arch. Complete removal therefore means following the tract past predictable structures: the facial nerve for first-cleft lesions, the carotid bifurcation and hypoglossal and glossopharyngeal nerves for second-cleft lesions, and the thyrohyoid membrane and SLN for third and fourth pouch tracts to the piriform sinus.
A-branchial.layer1: Incision — skin crease over the lesion; ellipse around the fistula opening at the anterior SCM border; platysma. For a long second-cleft tract use stepladder (stacked) incisions; for first-cleft lesions use a parotidectomy-type incision.
A-branchial.layer2: Superficial dissection — platysma; superficial layer of deep cervical fascia; anterior border of SCM; external jugular vein and great auricular nerve (high lesions). Free the cyst wall without rupture; retract SCM posteriorly.
A-branchial.layer3: First-cleft anomaly — parotid gland; facial nerve trunk and branches; cartilaginous EAC; bony-cartilaginous EAC junction. Find the facial nerve trunk first; Work type II tracts pass through the parotid and may run superficial to, deep to or between facial nerve branches toward the EAC.
A-branchial.layer4: Second-cleft tract — carotid sheath; hypoglossal nerve; glossopharyngeal nerve; external and internal carotid arteries; posterior belly of digastric and stylohyoid; tonsillar fossa. The tract ascends along the carotid sheath, superficial to CN XII and IX, between ECA and ICA, deep to the posterior belly of digastric and stylohyoid, to the tonsillar fossa.
A-branchial.layer5: Third/fourth pouch tracts — thyrohyoid membrane; internal branch of SLN; piriform sinus; thyroid lobe (often left). Third tracts pass posterior to the ICA and enter the piriform sinus through the thyrohyoid membrane above the internal SLN; fourth tracts run below the SLN. Endoscopic cautery of the piriform opening is often combined or used alone.
A-branchial.layer6: Tract endpoint and closure — pharyngeal opening; carotid sheath contents; wound layers. Ligate the tract at the pharyngeal wall; inspect vessels and nerves; close in layers.
A-branchial.landmark1: Facial nerve trunk (first-cleft) — Tragal pointer (about 1 cm deep, anterior-inferior), tympanomastoid suture (about 6-8 mm deep to its outer end), at the depth of the posterior belly of digastric (Identify before following the tract)
A-branchial.landmark2: Carotid bifurcation — Usually near the upper border of the thyroid cartilage (range about C3-C4); ECA has branches and lies anteromedial, ICA has none in the neck (Second-cleft tract passes through the fork)
A-branchial.landmark3: Hypoglossal nerve — Crosses superficial to ICA and ECA just below the posterior belly of digastric, looping around the occipital artery's SCM branch; follow the descendens hypoglossi upward (Lies deep to the second-cleft tract)
A-branchial.landmark4: Glossopharyngeal nerve — Runs with the stylopharyngeus, between ICA and ECA, deep to the styloid process, toward the tonsil bed (At risk near the upper tract)
A-branchial.landmark5: Piriform sinus opening — On direct laryngoscopy, a pit at the apex or anterolateral wall of the piriform sinus; cannulate to guide external dissection (Endpoint of third/fourth pouch tracts)
A-branchial.danger1: Facial nerve — where: Within the parotid in relation to first-cleft tracts, sometimes medial to the tract; protect: Identify the trunk first and dissect antegrade, monitor; injury: Facial paralysis
A-branchial.danger2: Internal and external carotid arteries — where: Second-cleft tract between them; third tract posterior to ICA; protect: Dissect on the tract wall, no traction avulsion; injury: Hemorrhage, stroke
A-branchial.danger3: Hypoglossal nerve — where: Deep to the second-cleft tract, superficial to the carotids; protect: Identify before dividing tissue near the bifurcation; injury: Tongue weakness
A-branchial.danger4: Glossopharyngeal nerve — where: Between ICA and ECA near the stylopharyngeus; protect: Stay on the tract near its upper end; injury: Dysphagia, posterior tongue sensory loss
A-branchial.danger5: SLN and RLN — where: Near third/fourth pouch tracts at the thyrohyoid membrane and tracheoesophageal groove; protect: Endoscopic cannulation of the tract, limited dissection; injury: Aspiration or vocal fold paralysis
A-branchial.danger6: Marginal mandibular and spinal accessory nerves — where: Below the mandible (marginal) and posterior to upper SCM (XI); protect: Subplatysmal plane, identify when dissection is high or posterior; injury: Lip weakness or shoulder dysfunction
A-branchial.variant1: First cleft: Work type I (ectodermal, duplication of membranous EAC, usually lateral to facial nerve) versus type II (ectoderm and mesoderm, at the angle of the mandible, variable relation to the nerve)
A-branchial.variant2: Second cleft (most common, often cited around 90%): cyst, sinus or complete fistula; Bailey types I-IV by depth
A-branchial.variant3: Third and fourth pouch sinuses: left-sided predominance, present as recurrent neck abscess or suppurative thyroiditis
A-branchial.variant4: Bilateral anomalies or syndromic association (branchio-oto-renal syndrome)
A-branchial.supply1: Arterial relations — Carotid bifurcation; tract vessels from the ECA branches
A-branchial.supply2: Nerve relations — Facial (first cleft); XII and IX (second); SLN (third/fourth); RLN (fourth)
A-branchial.supply3: Arch derivatives (third arch) — Glossopharyngeal nerve, common/internal carotid artery, stylopharyngeus
A-branchial.supply4: Arch derivatives (second arch) — Facial nerve, stapedius, stylohyoid, posterior belly of digastric
A-branchial.pimp1: Q: What is the general rule for a branchial tract's course? A: It runs deep to derivatives of its own arch and superficial to derivatives of the next arch
A-branchial.pimp2: Q: Describe the second branchial cleft fistula course. A: From anterior SCM border, along the carotid sheath, superficial to XII and IX, between ECA and ICA, to the tonsillar fossa
A-branchial.pimp3: Q: Where does a third pouch sinus enter the pharynx? A: Piriform sinus, through the thyrohyoid membrane above the internal SLN, after passing posterior to the ICA
A-branchial.pimp4: Q: How does a fourth pouch tract relate to the SLN? A: It runs inferior and deep to the superior laryngeal nerve (4th arch) and superficial to the RLN (6th arch)
A-branchial.pimp5: Q: Distinguish Work type I and type II first-cleft anomalies. A: Type I: ectoderm only, duplicate membranous EAC, usually lateral to VII. Type II: ectoderm and mesoderm, near the angle of the mandible, variable relation to VII
A-branchial.pimp6: Q: What syndrome combines branchial anomalies, hearing loss and renal anomalies? A: Branchio-oto-renal syndrome (often EYA1)
A-branchial.pimp7: Q: Which side do third and fourth pouch sinuses favor? A: Left
A-branchial.pimp8: Q: What is the nerve of the second arch? A: Facial nerve (CN VII)
OR-branchial.step1: Design a skin-crease incision over the lesion and expose the cyst/sinus tract while preserving capsule integrity.
OR-branchial.step2: Develop the lesion circumferentially and identify its relationship to SCM, carotid sheath, and cranial nerves before following the tract deeply.
OR-branchial.step3: For a second-branchial tract, anticipate passage between internal/external carotids toward the tonsillar fossa and protect hypoglossal/glossopharyngeal structures.
OR-branchial.step4: For first-branchial anomalies, define the relationship to parotid and facial nerve before tract removal.
OR-branchial.step5: Control feeding vessels and divide scarred attachments under direct visualization rather than traction avulsion.
OR-branchial.step6: Follow the epithelial tract to its endpoint and remove the cyst/tract completely to minimize recurrence.
OR-branchial.step7: Inspect the carotid sheath/nerve structures and obtain meticulous hemostasis.
OR-branchial.step8: Close the wound in layers and place a drain only when the dead space warrants it.

## Esophageal Button-Battery Emergency  (slug: button-battery, region: Pediatric Otolaryngology)

A-button-battery.summary: A lodged button battery generates hydroxide at its negative pole and causes liquefactive necrosis within hours, so the anatomy that matters is what lies next to the battery at its level: the trachea anteriorly and the aorta at the arch level, plus the recurrent laryngeal nerves. Remove it urgently along the esophageal axis, then grade the injury by depth and by which wall the negative pole faced, because that wall predicts delayed fistula.
A-button-battery.layer1: Airway and positioning — trachea (anterior to the esophagus, compressible in infants); teeth and lips. Intubate and secure the airway; the battery may compress the soft infant trachea.
A-button-battery.layer2: Hypopharynx and cricopharyngeus — postcricoid region; cricopharyngeus; esophageal inlet. Most pediatric batteries lodge at or below the cricopharyngeus near the thoracic inlet; enter under vision.
A-button-battery.layer3: Battery exposure — battery faces (negative pole narrower); surrounding necrotic mucosa; impaction wall. Identify which face is the negative pole and which wall it is against; that wall has the worst injury.
A-button-battery.layer4: Removal — esophageal lumen axis; cricopharyngeus. Grasp the rim with forceps or a basket/magnet and withdraw along the axis without fragmenting or pushing distally.
A-button-battery.layer5: Injury survey — mucosa; submucosa; muscularis; adjacent trachea (anterior); aortic arch level (left lateral/anterolateral). Re-scope and grade injury depth and circumference; note the level relative to the incisors and to the aortic arch and whether the injury faces the trachea.
A-button-battery.layer6: Second objects and airway — remaining esophagus; trachea (bronchoscopy if anterior injury). Survey for additional batteries; consider bronchoscopy when the injury faces the trachea.
A-button-battery.layer7: Debridement limits — adherent eschar. Do not aggressively debride adherent necrosis; it may unroof a contained perforation or vascular injury.
A-button-battery.landmark1: Negative pole — On the lateral film the narrower face is the negative pole; at endoscopy it is the face without the plus marking (The tissue against it has the deepest necrosis)
A-button-battery.landmark2: Aortic arch level — Upper thoracic esophagus at the second narrowing, roughly the T3-T4 level on imaging, just above the carina; pulsatile left-sided impression (Injury here risks aortoesophageal fistula)
A-button-battery.landmark3: Trachea — Directly anterior to the cervical and upper thoracic esophagus; anterior-wall injury faces it (Risk of tracheoesophageal fistula)
A-button-battery.landmark4: Tracheoesophageal grooves — Lateral margins between the esophagus and trachea, where the RLNs ascend (Lateral injury risks vocal fold paralysis)
A-button-battery.danger1: Aorta — where: Left lateral/anterolateral to the esophagus at the arch level and posterior-left in the descending segment; protect: Avoid deep debridement; obtain vascular imaging (CTA or MRA per protocol) when injury is near the aorta; observe before feeding; injury: Aortoesophageal fistula with delayed massive hemorrhage, often days to weeks after removal
A-button-battery.danger2: Trachea / membranous wall — where: Directly anterior; protect: Grade anterior injury; bronchoscopy; delayed imaging before feeding; injury: Tracheoesophageal fistula
A-button-battery.danger3: Esophageal wall — where: At the impaction site; protect: Remove quickly along the axis; no aggressive debridement; injury: Perforation, mediastinitis, stricture
A-button-battery.danger4: Recurrent laryngeal nerves — where: Tracheoesophageal grooves; protect: Document vocal fold mobility after deep lateral injury; injury: Unilateral or bilateral vocal fold paralysis
A-button-battery.danger5: Vertebral column — where: Posterior to the esophagus; protect: Recognize deep posterior injury; injury: Spondylodiscitis
A-button-battery.variant1: Battery lodged at the cricopharyngeus versus mid-esophagus versus distal esophagus (level determines adjacent structure)
A-button-battery.variant2: Stacked batteries or battery with magnets
A-button-battery.variant3: Underlying esophageal narrowing (repaired atresia) causing impaction of a smaller battery
A-button-battery.variant4: Batteries of 20 mm or larger (e.g., lithium coin cells) carry the highest risk in young children
A-button-battery.supply1: Esophageal arterial supply — Inferior thyroid (cervical), aortic and bronchial branches (thoracic), left gastric (abdominal)
A-button-battery.supply2: Adjacent vessels — Aortic arch and descending aorta; innominate and subclavian arteries near the thoracic inlet
A-button-battery.supply3: Nerves — RLNs in the tracheoesophageal grooves; vagal esophageal plexus distally
A-button-battery.supply4: Wall — No serosa; adventitia only
A-button-battery.pimp1: Q: Which pole of a button battery causes the most injury? A: The negative pole (narrower side), where hydroxide forms
A-button-battery.pimp2: Q: What is the mechanism of injury? A: Current through moist tissue generates hydroxide at the negative pole, causing alkaline liquefactive necrosis; pressure necrosis contributes
A-button-battery.pimp3: Q: How do you tell a battery from a coin on AP film? A: Double ring (halo) on AP and a step-off on lateral
A-button-battery.pimp4: Q: What pre-removal measure is recommended for a child over 1 year within 12 hours of ingestion? A: Honey 10 mL by mouth every 10 minutes up to 6 doses if it does not delay removal; in hospital, sucralfate (1 g/10 mL) 10 mL every 10 minutes up to 3 doses (National Capital Poison Center; also endorsed by ESPGHAN 2021)
A-button-battery.pimp5: Q: Which delayed complication is most feared? A: Aortoesophageal fistula with massive hemorrhage
A-button-battery.pimp6: Q: What lies anterior to the cervical esophagus? A: The trachea (membranous wall), with the RLNs in the grooves between them
A-button-battery.pimp7: Q: Why avoid aggressive debridement of eschar? A: It may unroof a contained perforation or vascular injury
OR-button-battery.step1: Treat an esophageal button battery as an immediate removal emergency: secure the airway and proceed to endoscopy without delaying for nonessential testing.
OR-button-battery.step2: Expose the battery under direct esophagoscopy and assess the surrounding mucosa before grasping so the most stable extraction orientation is chosen.
OR-button-battery.step3: Remove the battery along the esophageal axis without fragmentation, prolonged manipulation, or pushing it distally.
OR-button-battery.step4: Reinsert the scope and inspect the injury circumferentially for depth of necrosis, perforation, bleeding, and relationship to the airway/aortic level.
OR-button-battery.step5: Avoid aggressive debridement of adherent necrosis that could unmask a contained perforation or vascular injury.
OR-button-battery.step6: Survey for additional foreign material and document the exact level and severity of mucosal injury.
OR-button-battery.step7: Institute NPO, imaging, antimicrobial, feeding-access, and vascular/airway surveillance according to injury depth and location.
OR-button-battery.step8: Maintain explicit delayed-hemorrhage/fistula precautions because catastrophic aorto-esophageal or tracheoesophageal complications may occur after removal.

## Direct Laryngoscopy & Bronchoscopy / Airway Evaluation  (slug: direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy, region: Pediatric Otolaryngology)

A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.summary: DLB is a level-by-level survey of the airway from the supraglottis to the bronchi, so the anatomy is a checklist read in order. In a child the cricoid is the narrowest complete ring and cannot expand, so oversized instruments injure it; key hidden findings sit in the interarytenoid area (cleft), the subglottis (stenosis, cysts, hemangioma) and the distal trachea (vascular compression, complete rings, fistula).
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.layer1: Oral cavity and laryngoscope placement — lips and alveolar ridges/teeth; tongue; vallecula; epiglottis (omega-shaped, floppy in infants). Place the laryngoscope tip in the vallecula or under the epiglottis and lift; the infant larynx is high and anterior, so less neck extension is needed than in adults.
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.layer2: Supraglottis — epiglottis; aryepiglottic folds; cuneiform and corniculate cartilages; false vocal folds; laryngeal ventricles. Look for laryngomalacia features, saccular cysts and supraglottic masses before the scope distorts them.
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.layer3: Glottis and posterior commissure — true vocal folds; anterior commissure; vocal processes; interarytenoid region; posterior commissure. Assess mobility during light anesthesia; palpate the interarytenoid area with a probe to exclude a laryngeal cleft and palpate the arytenoids for fixation.
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.layer4: Subglottis — conus elasticus; cricoid ring (narrowest complete ring); subglottic mucosa. Inspect circumferentially with a telescope placed just above the folds and then through them; record stenosis, cysts, hemangioma or granulation and its distance below the folds.
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.layer5: Cervical and thoracic trachea — C-shaped tracheal rings; posterior membranous wall; innominate artery pulsation (anterior, distal trachea in children); tracheostomy site if present. Look for complete rings (no membranous wall), anterior pulsatile compression, malacia and an H-type fistula opening on the posterior wall.
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.layer6: Carina and bronchi — main carina; right main bronchus and right upper lobe orifice; bronchus intermedius; left main bronchus. Look for tracheal bronchus above the carina and left main bronchus compression by vascular anomalies or the left atrium.
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.layer7: Sizing and intervention — subglottic lumen; calibrated endotracheal tubes; leak pressure. Size with the largest tube that leaks at a standard pressure (Myer-Cotton used a leak at 10-25 cm H2O; many centers use about 20 cm H2O) and compare with age-expected size to grade stenosis.
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.landmark1: Interarytenoid area — Posterior glottis between the arytenoid apices; spread the arytenoids or probe the notch to see how far the mucosal defect extends toward or below the vocal folds (Laryngeal cleft is missed unless palpated)
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.landmark2: Cricoid ring — Complete cartilage ring seen just below the conus, roughly a few mm to 1 cm below the true folds in infants; a fixed narrowing that does not move with respiration (Narrowest complete ring; site of subglottic stenosis)
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.landmark3: Posterior membranous wall — Smooth, ringless posterior tracheal wall; with the child supine and surgeon at the head it is at the bottom of the view (Orientation reference; absent in complete tracheal rings)
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.landmark4: Innominate artery compression — Pulsatile anterior compression of the distal trachea, from the lower left toward the upper right; lifting the anterior wall with the scope tip may diminish the right radial/temporal pulse (Common extrinsic cause of tracheal compression in infants)
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.landmark5: H-type tracheoesophageal fistula — Small opening in the posterior membranous wall, usually at the cervicothoracic junction or upper thoracic trachea; may need a probe or catheter (Easily missed; changes surgical plan)
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.danger1: Alveolar ridges, teeth, lips — where: Laryngoscope fulcrum; protect: Gum or tooth guard and lift along the axis of the handle, not rotation; injury: Tooth injury, lip laceration
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.danger2: Arytenoids — where: Posterior glottis; protect: Gentle telescope passage in the midline; do not use the arytenoid as a fulcrum; injury: Arytenoid dislocation, posterior glottic scar
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.danger3: Subglottic mucosa — where: Inside the cricoid ring; protect: Use age-appropriate scope and tube sizes; stop sizing at a leak; injury: Edema, acquired subglottic stenosis
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.danger4: Posterior membranous wall and distal bronchi — where: Behind the trachea (esophagus) and in small bronchi; protect: Under-vision advancement, avoid force in small airways; injury: Perforation, pneumomediastinum, pneumothorax
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.danger5: Larynx in light anesthesia — where: Glottis; protect: Topical lidocaine to the larynx at an accepted weight-based dose and adequate depth before instrumentation; injury: Laryngospasm, hypoxia
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.variant1: Laryngeal cleft (Benjamin-Inglis types I-IV)
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.variant2: Complete tracheal rings, often with pulmonary artery sling
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.variant3: Tracheal (pig) bronchus to the right upper lobe
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.variant4: Vascular rings (double aortic arch, right arch with aberrant left subclavian and ligamentum) compressing trachea and esophagus
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.variant5: Subglottic hemangioma (often posterior-lateral, left-sided more often in many series) or subglottic cysts in former preterm infants
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.supply1: Laryngeal sensation — Internal branch of SLN above the vocal folds; RLN below the folds
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.supply2: Laryngeal motor — RLN to all intrinsic muscles except cricothyroid (external SLN)
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.supply3: Laryngeal arteries — Superior laryngeal artery (superior thyroid) and inferior laryngeal artery (inferior thyroid)
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.supply4: Adjacent great vessels — Innominate artery anterior to the distal trachea; aortic arch to the left; pulmonary arteries at the carina
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.pimp1: Q: What subglottic diameter defines stenosis in neonates? A: 4 mm or less in a full-term neonate and about 3-3.5 mm or less in a preterm neonate (sources vary between 3 and 3.5 mm)
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.pimp2: Q: What is the Benjamin-Inglis classification of laryngeal clefts? A: I interarytenoid above the folds; II partial cricoid; III through the cricoid into the cervical trachea; IV into the thoracic trachea
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.pimp3: Q: Why is the infant subglottis vulnerable? A: The cricoid is a complete, nonexpandable ring and the narrowest part of the infant airway, so small mucosal edema greatly reduces lumen
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.pimp4: Q: Where is the infant larynx relative to the cervical spine? A: Higher than in adults: the infant larynx spans roughly C1-C4 (cricoid near C4) versus roughly C3-C6 in adults (cricoid at C6), descending with growth
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.pimp5: Q: What is a pulmonary artery sling? A: Left pulmonary artery arising from the right PA and passing between trachea and esophagus; associated with complete tracheal rings
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.pimp6: Q: What is the usual cause of anterior pulsatile compression of the distal trachea in infants? A: Innominate artery compression
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.pimp7: Q: What is the usual sensory and motor innervation of the larynx? A: Sensory: internal SLN above and RLN below the folds; motor: RLN except the cricothyroid (external SLN)
A-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.pimp8: Q: What does a leak at a low pressure around an age-appropriate tube indicate? A: Adequate subglottic lumen; failure to leak at a standard pressure implies relative stenosis that is graded against an age-based chart
OR-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.step1: Coordinate the ventilation plan with anesthesia and obtain atraumatic direct-laryngoscopic exposure of the supraglottis and true vocal folds before passing a telescope or bronchoscope.
OR-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.step2: Document the supraglottis, vocal-fold mobility/appearance, posterior commissure, and glottic aperture before instrumenting the subglottis.
OR-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.step3: Pass the telescope through the cords under direct vision and inspect the subglottis circumferentially, recording stenosis, scar, edema, or structural abnormality relative to the vocal folds and cricoid.
OR-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.step4: Advance through the trachea to the carina and inspect both mainstem bronchi when bronchoscopy is indicated, maintaining orientation to the posterior membranous wall.
OR-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.step5: Measure any airway lesion by level, length, diameter/grade, and distance from fixed landmarks; perform calibrated leak-pressure sizing only when it changes management.
OR-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.step6: Complete planned biopsy, scar division, dilation, granulation removal, or foreign-body work only after the diagnostic airway has been fully documented.
OR-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.step7: Reinspect the entire treated airway for bleeding, edema, mucosal injury, loose tissue, and the actual lumen gained after intervention.
OR-direct-laryngoscopy-bronchoscopy.step8: Define extubation versus postoperative airway support before leaving the OR and document rescue-airway concerns for the recovery team.

## Laryngotracheal Cleft Repair  (slug: laryngotracheal-cleft-repair, region: Pediatric Otolaryngology)

A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.summary: A laryngotracheal cleft is a posterior midline defect from incomplete fusion of the posterior cricoid lamina and tracheoesophageal septum, leaving the airway open to the esophagus and causing aspiration. Repair separates and closes the airway and esophageal mucosa in two layers; safety depends on the interarytenoid and posterior cricoid anatomy (Benjamin-Inglis type), the recurrent laryngeal nerves running in the tracheoesophageal grooves into the larynx behind the cricothyroid joint, and avoiding posterior glottic stenosis.
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.layer1: Suspension microlaryngoscopy and bronchoscopy — supraglottis and epiglottis; arytenoids and interarytenoid region; posterior commissure; posterior cricoid plate; posterior tracheal wall (membranous); esophageal mucosa seen through the cleft. Spread the arytenoids with a probe or vocal fold spreader to measure the cleft's inferior extent. A normal interarytenoid notch ends well above the vocal folds; type I extends down to but not below the fold level, type II extends into the cricoid partially, type III through the cricoid into the cervical trachea, type IV into the thoracic trachea.
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.layer2: Mucosal incision along the cleft edges — airway (laryngotracheal) mucosa; esophageal (pharyngeal) mucosa; interarytenoid muscle remnants; posterior cricoarytenoid muscles (posterolateral). Incise along the mucosal junction from the superior apex down to the inferior end of the cleft, creating separate airway and esophageal flaps.
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.layer3: Esophageal layer — esophageal mucosa; submucosa. Close the esophageal mucosa first, from inferior to superior, with knots on the esophageal side.
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.layer4: Airway layer — airway mucosa; interarytenoid muscle; cricoid lamina edges (in deeper types). Close the airway mucosa with knots outside the lumen when possible; avoid narrowing the posterior glottis. In deeper clefts close the cricoid lamina edges if possible and reinforce with an interposed graft.
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.layer5: Open approach (for type III-IV) — strap muscles; thyroid isthmus; laryngofissure through the thyroid cartilage, cricoid and trachea; tracheoesophageal grooves and RLNs (lateral). An anterior laryngotracheal fissure gives direct access to the posterior cleft; a lateral pharyngotomy is an alternative but places the RLN at greater risk. Interpose tissue (tibial periosteum, fascia, pericardium or cartilage) between layers.
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.layer6: Final inspection — posterior glottis; subglottis; arytenoid mobility. Confirm the arytenoids move, the posterior commissure is not tethered, and the airway lumen is adequate.
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.landmark1: Interarytenoid notch — Between the arytenoid apices posteriorly; palpate with a right-angle probe to see how far down the gap goes (Defines cleft type)
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.landmark2: Vocal fold level — The line of the true vocal folds and vocal processes (Type I clefts end above it; deeper clefts pass below)
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.landmark3: Posterior cricoid lamina — Below the arytenoids on the posterior airway wall; feel the firm signet plate with a probe (Partial or complete deficiency marks type II or III)
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.landmark4: Tracheoesophageal party wall — Posterior membranous trachea seen through the cleft below the cricoid (Extension here indicates type III or IV)
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.landmark5: Recurrent laryngeal nerve — In the tracheoesophageal groove, entering the larynx deep to the inferior constrictor just behind the cricothyroid joint (At risk in the lateral approach and with deep lateral dissection)
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.danger1: Recurrent laryngeal nerves — where: Tracheoesophageal grooves bilaterally, entering the larynx behind the cricothyroid joint; protect: Use the endoscopic or anterior (laryngofissure) route; dissect in the midline; injury: Vocal fold paralysis, airway compromise if bilateral
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.danger2: Posterior glottis and cricoarytenoid joints — where: Posterior commissure and interarytenoid area; protect: Avoid over-tight closure and excessive scarring; injury: Posterior glottic stenosis, fixed arytenoids
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.danger3: Esophageal lumen — where: Directly behind the cleft; protect: Mucosal-only closure without bunching; injury: Esophageal stricture
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.danger4: Suture lines (fistula) — where: Between airway and esophagus; protect: Two layers with offset suture lines and interposed tissue; injury: Recurrent fistula, aspiration
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.danger5: Airway lumen (subglottis) — where: Cricoid ring is the narrowest complete ring in infants; protect: Avoid excess bulk at the subglottis; injury: Subglottic narrowing
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.variant1: Benjamin-Inglis type I to IV (with type IV subdivided by thoracic extent)
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.variant2: Associations: tracheoesophageal fistula/esophageal atresia, Opitz G/BBB syndrome, Pallister-Hall syndrome, VACTERL
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.variant3: Coexisting laryngomalacia or tracheomalacia
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.variant4: Deep interarytenoid notch (sometimes considered type 0) that is not a true cleft
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.supply1: Larynx — Superior and inferior laryngeal arteries (superior thyroid and inferior thyroid arteries)
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.supply2: Esophagus (cervical) — Inferior thyroid artery branches
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.supply3: Motor — RLN supplies all intrinsic laryngeal muscles except the cricothyroid (external branch of SLN)
A-laryngotracheal-cleft-repair.supply4: Sensory — Internal branch of SLN above the vocal folds; RLN below
