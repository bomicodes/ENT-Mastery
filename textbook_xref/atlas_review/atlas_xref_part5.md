
## Microtia Reconstruction  (slug: microtia-reconstruction, region: Pediatric Otolaryngology)

A-microtia-reconstruction.summary: Microtia leaves a malformed cartilage remnant and lobule from abnormal first and second branchial arch (hillock) development; autologous reconstruction carves a framework from costal cartilage and buries it under thin, well-perfused mastoid skin. The safety-critical relationships are the pleura deep to the costal cartilage at the donor site and the vascularity of the skin pocket (and of the temporoparietal fascia when it is needed), and the cosmetic result depends on matching the contralateral ear's position, axis and height.
A-microtia-reconstruction.layer1: Templating and marking — contralateral helix, antihelix, concha, tragus and lobule; lateral canthus; ala / nasal dorsum (axis reference); hairline; microtic remnant and lobule. Trace the normal ear on film. Position the top of the helix roughly at the level of the lateral brow and the ear axis about parallel to the nasal dorsum; the distance from lateral canthus to helical root should match the normal side. Hemifacial microsomia may lower and move the site forward.
A-microtia-reconstruction.layer2: Costal cartilage harvest — chest skin and subcutaneous tissue; rectus abdominis and external oblique fascia; costal cartilages 6, 7 (base block, synchondrosis) and 8 (helix; the free lower cartilage, not an anatomic floating rib); perichondrium; pleura and intercostal bundle. Usually harvested from the contralateral chest (ipsilateral in some techniques). Leave posterior perichondrium where possible to protect the pleura and allow regeneration. Check for pleural tear with a Valsalva under saline before closure.
A-microtia-reconstruction.layer3: Framework fabrication — base plate (from 6-7 synchondrosis); helical rim (8th costal cartilage); antihelix and crura; tragal strut. Fix components with wire or nonabsorbable suture with knots buried on the deep surface. Make the framework slightly smaller than the template to allow for skin thickness.
A-microtia-reconstruction.layer4: Recipient skin pocket — mastoid and remnant skin (thin dermis); subdermal plexus; remnant cartilage (removed); superficial temporal and posterior auricular vessels. Elevate a thin subcutaneous pocket in the planned area, preserving the subdermal plexus and the skin's pedicle (typically a broad base). Remove remnant cartilage. Avoid buttonholes, which risk framework exposure.
A-microtia-reconstruction.layer5: Framework insertion and suction — framework; skin envelope; suction drains. Insert and orient the framework to the marks; suction drains coapt skin to the contours. Check skin perfusion (capillary refill, color) after suction.
A-microtia-reconstruction.layer6: Later stages (lobule, elevation, tragus) — lobule transposition; postauricular sulcus with skin graft; banked cartilage or TPF flap; temporoparietal fascia on the superficial temporal artery. Elevation needs a cartilage wedge behind the framework and coverage (skin graft or TPF flap) on its posterior surface.
A-microtia-reconstruction.landmark1: Contralateral ear position — Measure from lateral canthus to helical root and the ear's vertical height; the top of the ear is roughly at brow level, the lobule roughly at the nasal base, and the ear axis is inclined posteriorly about 15 to 30 degrees from vertical (Symmetric placement)
A-microtia-reconstruction.landmark2: Synchondrosis of ribs 6 and 7 — Palpate the costal margin; the 6th and 7th cartilages fuse medially into a broad block above the xiphoid region (Base framework block)
A-microtia-reconstruction.landmark3: 8th costal cartilage ("free" or "floating" cartilage in microtia texts; the true floating ribs are 11 and 12) — The lower, more mobile cartilage at the costal margin, below and separate from the 6-7 synchondrosis (Long strip for the helix)
A-microtia-reconstruction.landmark4: Superficial temporal artery — Doppler or palpation anterior to the tragus and remnant, running up into the temporoparietal fascia (Supplies the TPF flap; check before relying on it (may be absent or small in hemifacial microsomia))
A-microtia-reconstruction.landmark5: Hairline — Mark the hairline in relation to the template before incision (Hair-bearing skin over the framework is a common problem; plan hair removal or use the TPF and graft)
A-microtia-reconstruction.danger1: Pleura — where: Deep to the costal cartilages and posterior perichondrium; thin in children; protect: Subperichondrial dissection on the deep surface; test with saline and positive-pressure breath before closure; injury: Pneumothorax
A-microtia-reconstruction.danger2: Skin flap (subdermal plexus) — where: Over the framework; protect: Thin but not too thin; avoid buttonholes; gentle suction pressure; injury: Necrosis, framework exposure and infection
A-microtia-reconstruction.danger3: Facial nerve — where: May be aberrant in microtia and hemifacial microsomia, and the stylomastoid foramen region lies low; usually deeper than the skin pocket; protect: Stay in the subcutaneous plane; avoid deep anterior-inferior dissection; injury: Facial paresis
A-microtia-reconstruction.danger4: Superficial temporal vessels — where: Temporoparietal fascia, anterior to the remnant; protect: Preserve for a possible TPF salvage flap; injury: Loss of salvage option
A-microtia-reconstruction.danger5: Chest wall growth and contour — where: Donor site; protect: Preserve perichondrium and limit harvest; injury: Chest wall depression or deformity
A-microtia-reconstruction.variant1: Hemifacial microsomia (oculo-auriculo-vertebral spectrum) with mandibular hypoplasia, low hairline and facial nerve weakness
A-microtia-reconstruction.variant2: Aural atresia often coexists; plan atresiaplasty timing so the framework skin is not compromised
A-microtia-reconstruction.variant3: Treacher Collins syndrome with bilateral microtia
A-microtia-reconstruction.variant4: Low-set remnant or small chest cartilage in young children that limits framework size
A-microtia-reconstruction.variant5: Absent or small superficial temporal artery on the affected side
A-microtia-reconstruction.supply1: Auricle and pocket — Posterior auricular artery and superficial temporal artery branches
A-microtia-reconstruction.supply2: TPF flap — Superficial temporal artery and vein
A-microtia-reconstruction.supply3: Sensory — Great auricular nerve (C2-C3), auriculotemporal nerve (V3), lesser occipital nerve, Arnold nerve (X)
A-microtia-reconstruction.supply4: Costal cartilage — Avascular; nourished by perichondrium (intercostal and internal thoracic branches)
A-microtia-reconstruction.pimp1: Q: What are the hillocks of His? A: Six mesenchymal hillocks: 1-3 from the first arch (tragus, helical root, superior helix) and 4-6 from the second arch (antihelix, antitragus, lobule)
A-microtia-reconstruction.pimp2: Q: How does the auricle relate to the nose in position? A: Its axis parallels the nasal dorsum roughly, its top is around brow level and its bottom around the nasal base
A-microtia-reconstruction.pimp3: Q: Which ribs are harvested for the framework? A: Usually 6 and 7 (synchondrosis) for the base, 8 for the helical rim
A-microtia-reconstruction.pimp4: Q: What is the main danger at the rib donor site? A: Pleural injury and pneumothorax
A-microtia-reconstruction.pimp5: Q: Which artery supplies the temporoparietal fascia flap? A: Superficial temporal artery
A-microtia-reconstruction.pimp6: Q: What is the Marx classification of microtia? A: Grade I: smaller but normal structures; II: some structures present; III: peanut-shaped remnant (classic microtia); IV: anotia
A-microtia-reconstruction.pimp7: Q: What syndrome often coexists with microtia and facial asymmetry? A: Hemifacial microsomia (oculo-auriculo-vertebral spectrum, Goldenhar when epibulbar dermoids and vertebral anomalies)
OR-microtia-reconstruction.step1: Template the normal contralateral ear and mark the planned auricular position/axis before harvesting cartilage or creating the recipient pocket.
OR-microtia-reconstruction.step2: Harvest costal cartilage through a chest incision while preserving perichondrium/chest-wall integrity and checking for pleural injury before closure.
OR-microtia-reconstruction.step3: Sculpt the framework components to reproduce helix, antihelix, conchal bowl, and base projection, securing pieces without sharp exposed wire/suture points.
OR-microtia-reconstruction.step4: Create a thin, well-vascularized postauricular skin pocket at the premarked position without buttonholing the skin envelope.
OR-microtia-reconstruction.step5: Insert and orient the cartilage framework so vertical height, projection, and lobule relationship mirror the opposite ear.
OR-microtia-reconstruction.step6: Apply controlled suction/bolster so skin conforms to the framework contours without ischemic pressure.
OR-microtia-reconstruction.step7: Close the donor and recipient sites and verify chest stability and framework skin perfusion.
OR-microtia-reconstruction.step8: Document the staged elevation/lobule/tragal refinement plan and monitor closely for hematoma, skin compromise, infection, or cartilage exposure.

## Palatoplasty / Cleft Palate Repair  (slug: palatoplasty, region: Pediatric Otolaryngology)

A-palatoplasty.summary: In cleft palate the levator veli palatini inserts abnormally onto the posterior hard palate edge and runs sagittally instead of forming a transverse sling, so the velum cannot close the velopharynx. Repair closes the nasal and oral layers and repositions the levator into a transverse sling; safety and success depend on preserving the greater palatine neurovascular pedicles from their foramina, releasing the muscle without injuring the tensor and hamulus region, and closing without tension to prevent fistula.
A-palatoplasty.layer1: Exposure and marking — Dingman or similar mouth gag; hard palate cleft margins; alveolus; greater palatine foramina (posterolateral hard palate); maxillary tuberosity and hamulus; uvula halves. Mark relaxing incisions along the alveolar ridge medial to the teeth (for two-flap) and the cleft margin incisions at the oral-nasal mucosal junction. Infiltrate with dilute epinephrine.
A-palatoplasty.layer2: Oral mucoperiosteal flaps — oral mucoperiosteum; greater palatine artery, vein and nerve; palatine bone. Elevate subperiosteally from anterior to posterior, then identify the pedicle as it exits the greater palatine foramen. Free it gently (stripping periosteum around it or opening the foramen posteriorly) for length; do not divide it.
A-palatoplasty.layer3: Nasal layer — nasal mucoperiosteum on the hard palate; vomer mucosa (vomer flap); nasal mucosa of the soft palate. Separate nasal mucosa from the palatal shelves and vomer to close it in the midline. Release laterally from the hard palate edge if tension remains.
A-palatoplasty.layer4: Muscle dissection (intravelar veloplasty) — levator veli palatini (abnormal insertion on the posterior hard palate); palatopharyngeus; tensor veli palatini aponeurosis; pterygoid hamulus; musculus uvulae (hypoplastic). Detach the levator from the posterior hard palate edge and from nasal and oral mucosa, dissect it posteriorly and laterally and rotate it to overlap or approximate in a transverse sling across the midline. The tensor tendon wraps around the hamulus lateral to the levator.
A-palatoplasty.layer5: Soft palate and uvula closure — nasal layer; muscle sling; oral layer; uvula. Close in three layers. Furlow double-opposing Z-plasty is an alternative that lengthens the palate and reorients muscle within the posteriorly based myomucosal flaps.
A-palatoplasty.layer6: Oral closure and relaxing incisions — oral mucoperiosteal flaps; lateral relaxing incisions; exposed bone. Close the oral layer from posterior to anterior; lateral raw areas heal by secondary intention. The junction of hard and soft palate is the commonest fistula site.
A-palatoplasty.landmark1: Greater palatine foramen — Posterolateral hard palate, medial to the second (or third) molar region in adults and close to the tuberosity in infants, about 1 cm medial to the alveolar ridge; pedicle seen emerging after flap elevation (Pedicle of the oral flap; preserve)
A-palatoplasty.landmark2: Pterygoid hamulus — Palpable bony hook posteromedial to the maxillary tuberosity (Landmark for the tensor tendon; some techniques fracture it, many do not)
A-palatoplasty.landmark3: Levator veli palatini insertion — Sagittally oriented muscle bundles inserting on the posterior edge of the hard palate along the cleft (Must be released and reoriented transversely)
A-palatoplasty.landmark4: Lesser palatine foramina and vessels — Just posterior to the greater palatine foramen, supplying the soft palate (Soft palate blood supply)
A-palatoplasty.landmark5: Nasopalatine (incisive) foramen — Midline behind the central incisors (Anterior palate supply; often divided in two-flap repair)
A-palatoplasty.danger1: Greater palatine neurovascular bundle — where: Emerges from the greater palatine foramen posterolaterally and runs anteriorly in the oral mucoperiosteum; protect: Subperiosteal elevation; release, do not stretch or divide; avoid closure tension; injury: Flap necrosis, fistula
A-palatoplasty.danger2: Tensor veli palatini / Eustachian tube function — where: Tensor aponeurosis lateral to the levator, wrapping the hamulus; protect: Avoid excessive lateral dissection and tensor division unless planned; injury: Worse ET dysfunction (already common in cleft palate)
A-palatoplasty.danger3: Airway (tongue and oropharynx) — where: Under the gag and after repair; protect: Release the gag periodically on long cases; consider a tongue stitch; monitor in children with micrognathia (Pierre Robin sequence); injury: Tongue edema, postoperative airway obstruction
A-palatoplasty.danger4: Nasal layer — where: Nasal side of hard-soft palate junction; protect: Tension-free nasal closure; vomer flap; injury: Oronasal fistula
A-palatoplasty.danger5: Tooth buds — where: Alveolus near relaxing incisions; protect: Keep relaxing incisions medial to the alveolar ridge; injury: Dental and maxillary growth problems
A-palatoplasty.variant1: Submucous cleft palate (bifid uvula, zona pellucida, hard palate notch) with similar levator malposition
A-palatoplasty.variant2: Pierre Robin sequence (micrognathia, glossoptosis, U-shaped cleft) with airway risk
A-palatoplasty.variant3: 22q11.2 deletion syndrome with velopharyngeal dysfunction and medialized carotid arteries
A-palatoplasty.variant4: Wide clefts needing vomer flaps, buccal myomucosal flaps or staged repair
A-palatoplasty.variant5: Van der Woude syndrome (lip pits) and other syndromic clefts
A-palatoplasty.supply1: Hard palate — Greater palatine artery (descending palatine branch of maxillary artery); nasopalatine artery anteriorly
A-palatoplasty.supply2: Soft palate — Lesser palatine, ascending palatine (facial), palatine branch of ascending pharyngeal
A-palatoplasty.supply3: Motor — All palatal muscles via pharyngeal plexus (vagus) except tensor veli palatini (V3)
A-palatoplasty.supply4: Sensory — Greater and lesser palatine nerves and nasopalatine nerve (V2)
A-palatoplasty.pimp1: Q: Which muscle is the primary elevator of the velum? A: Levator veli palatini
A-palatoplasty.pimp2: Q: Which palatal muscle is not supplied by the pharyngeal plexus? A: Tensor veli palatini (V3)
A-palatoplasty.pimp3: Q: What embryologic structures form the primary and secondary palate? A: Primary palate from the medial nasal processes (intermaxillary segment, anterior to the incisive foramen); secondary palate from the maxillary processes' palatal shelves
A-palatoplasty.pimp4: Q: Where is the most common site of postoperative palatal fistula? A: Junction of the hard and soft palate
A-palatoplasty.pimp5: Q: What is the abnormal levator insertion in cleft palate? A: Onto the posterior edge of the hard palate, oriented sagittally
A-palatoplasty.pimp6: Q: What are the classic signs of submucous cleft palate? A: Bifid uvula, zona pellucida, notched posterior hard palate
A-palatoplasty.pimp7: Q: Why do children with cleft palate have OME? A: Abnormal tensor veli palatini insertion and function impair Eustachian tube opening
A-palatoplasty.pimp8: Q: What vessel emerges from the greater palatine foramen? A: The greater palatine artery (from the descending palatine branch of the maxillary artery)
OR-palatoplasty.step1: Expose the cleft palate with a mouth gag and mark oral/nasal mucosal flaps, relaxing incisions, and greater-palatal neurovascular pedicles before cutting.
OR-palatoplasty.step2: Elevate oral mucoperiosteal flaps while preserving the greater palatine pedicles and create enough mobility for a tension-free midline closure.
OR-palatoplasty.step3: Separate nasal mucosa from the hard-palate edge and vomer as required, then close the nasal layer continuously/interrupted without leaving a fistula gap.
OR-palatoplasty.step4: Dissect the abnormal levator veli palatini insertions from the posterior hard palate and reorient the muscle sling transversely in an intravelar veloplasty.
OR-palatoplasty.step5: Reconstruct the uvula/soft palate with aligned nasal, muscle, and oral layers while avoiding excess tension or airway-narrowing bulk.
OR-palatoplasty.step6: Close the oral mucosal layer from posterior to anterior and secure hard-palate flaps without strangulating their pedicles.
OR-palatoplasty.step7: Inspect for exposed bone, pedicle kinking, bleeding, and symmetric palatal length/mobility; avoid tight packing against the repair.
OR-palatoplasty.step8: Remove the gag after checking tongue/teeth and institute postoperative airway, hydration, arm-restraint/feeding, fistula, and speech follow-up plans.

## Pediatric Laryngotracheal Reconstruction  (slug: peds-ltr, region: Pediatric Otolaryngology)

A-peds-ltr.summary: Pediatric LTR enlarges a stenotic subglottis by splitting the cricoid anteriorly, posteriorly or both and holding the split open with carved costal cartilage. The structures that decide safety are the recurrent laryngeal nerves just behind the cricothyroid joints, the esophagus immediately behind the posterior cricoid plate, the anterior commissure above the anterior split, and the pleura during rib harvest.
A-peds-ltr.layer1: Incision and flaps — skin (horizontal at cricoid level or around stoma); platysma; anterior jugular veins. Excise or encircle the tracheostomy stoma if present; raise subplatysmal flaps from hyoid to sternal notch.
A-peds-ltr.layer2: Midline exposure — strap muscles (sternohyoid, sternothyroid); thyroid isthmus; cricothyroid muscles; thyroid cartilage; cricoid arch; upper tracheal rings. Separate straps at the midline raphe, divide the isthmus; expose only the anterior framework and stay medial to the cricothyroid muscles.
A-peds-ltr.layer3: Anterior split — anterior cricoid; cricothyroid membrane; lower thyroid cartilage; first 1-2 tracheal rings; subglottic scar. Vertical midline cut through the cricoid and upper rings; extend into the lower thyroid cartilage only as needed and stay below the anterior commissure.
A-peds-ltr.layer4: Posterior split — posterior cricoid lamina; interarytenoid muscle (if posterior glottic stenosis); postcricoid tissue; hypopharyngeal/esophageal wall. From inside the airway, cut the posterior lamina in the exact midline through its full height and thickness until the postcricoid tissue bulges; do not breach the esophageal mucosa.
A-peds-ltr.layer5: Costal cartilage harvest — inframammary skin (avoid the breast bud); chest wall muscle; costal cartilage (commonly 5th-7th); perichondrium; pleura. Harvest cartilage leaving inner perichondrium on the pleura; leak test with saline and a sustained positive-pressure breath.
A-peds-ltr.layer6: Graft carving and inset — anterior boat-shaped graft with flanges; posterior rectangular graft with flanges; perichondrium (toward lumen); cut cricoid edges. Flanges rest outside the cut edges so the graft cannot prolapse; sutures are placed outside the mucosa.
A-peds-ltr.layer7: Stent or tube and closure — stent or nasotracheal tube; suprastomal segment; strap muscles over graft; drain. Single-stage repair uses an endotracheal tube as stent; double-stage uses a stent with a tracheostomy. Cover the graft with straps and drain the wound.
A-peds-ltr.landmark1: Cricoid arch — Ring palpable just below the thyroid cartilage and cricothyroid membrane; in infants it lies very close beneath the thyroid lamina edge (Center of the anterior split)
A-peds-ltr.landmark2: Anterior commissure — Roughly at mid-height of the thyroid cartilage in the midline; confirm by viewing from inside or with a needle through the cartilage (Keep the anterior split below it to avoid a web)
A-peds-ltr.landmark3: Posterior cricoid midline — From the opened airway, the midline between the arytenoids in line with the interarytenoid notch; an NG tube behind it helps feel the depth (Exact midline protects the cricoarytenoid joints and esophagus)
A-peds-ltr.landmark4: RLN entry — Posterior to the cricothyroid joint (palpable on the posterolateral cricoid), deep to the inferior constrictor lower edge (Avoid all lateral cricoid dissection)
A-peds-ltr.landmark5: Costal cartilage — Palpate the bony-cartilaginous junction of the chosen rib below the inframammary crease; cartilage is white, rubbery and blends into the costal margin (Adequate straight segment for the graft)
A-peds-ltr.danger1: Recurrent laryngeal nerves — where: Tracheoesophageal grooves, entering the larynx behind the cricothyroid joints; protect: Midline-only dissection; posterior split exactly midline; injury: Vocal fold paralysis, airway compromise if bilateral
A-peds-ltr.danger2: Esophagus — where: Immediately posterior to the posterior cricoid lamina; protect: Controlled cartilage-only cut; palpate the NG tube; injury: Perforation, fistula, graft infection
A-peds-ltr.danger3: Anterior commissure — where: Midline thyroid cartilage at about mid-height; protect: Limit superior extent of the split; precise closure if laryngofissure is needed; injury: Anterior glottic web, dysphonia
A-peds-ltr.danger4: Pleura — where: Deep to the inner perichondrium; protect: Subperichondrial harvest; leak test; injury: Pneumothorax
A-peds-ltr.danger5: Innominate artery and brachiocephalic vein — where: Can lie at or above the sternal notch in infants and children; protect: Avoid low dissection; palpate before working near the notch; injury: Major hemorrhage
A-peds-ltr.danger6: Graft — where: Within the split framework; protect: Flanged design and secure inset; check endoscopically; injury: Graft prolapse into the lumen, displacement, restenosis
A-peds-ltr.variant1: High-riding innominate artery or brachiocephalic vein in infants
A-peds-ltr.variant2: Thymus extending into the lower neck in infants
A-peds-ltr.variant3: Associated posterior glottic stenosis or laryngeal cleft
A-peds-ltr.variant4: Syndromic airway with additional tracheal stenosis or complete rings
A-peds-ltr.supply1: Laryngeal arteries — Superior laryngeal (superior thyroid), cricothyroid branch, inferior laryngeal (inferior thyroid)
A-peds-ltr.supply2: Tracheal supply — Lateral segmental vessels from the inferior thyroid and bronchial arteries
A-peds-ltr.supply3: Motor nerves — RLN to intrinsic muscles except cricothyroid; external SLN to cricothyroid
A-peds-ltr.supply4: Rib site — Intercostal vein, artery and nerve along the inferior rib border
A-peds-ltr.pimp1: Q: What are the Myer-Cotton grades? A: I 0-50%, II 51-70%, III 71-99%, IV no lumen
A-peds-ltr.pimp2: Q: Why must the posterior cricoid split be midline? A: To avoid the cricoarytenoid joints and keep symmetric expansion; the esophagus lies directly behind
A-peds-ltr.pimp3: Q: What is the difference between single-stage and double-stage LTR? A: Single-stage stents with an endotracheal tube and removes the tracheostomy; double-stage keeps a tracheostomy with a stent
A-peds-ltr.pimp4: Q: Where does the RLN enter the larynx? A: Posterior to the cricothyroid joint, beneath the inferior constrictor
A-peds-ltr.pimp5: Q: What embryologic arch gives rise to the cricoid? A: Sixth arch in most board texts (the laryngeal cartilages other than the epiglottis arise from fused fourth and sixth arch mesenchyme, so some sources say fourth and sixth)
A-peds-ltr.pimp6: Q: What incision consideration applies to rib harvest in girls? A: Place the incision below and away from the breast bud (inframammary)
A-peds-ltr.pimp7: Q: Which way is the perichondrium oriented on the graft? A: Toward the airway lumen
OR-peds-ltr.step1: Expose the larynx/trachea through the existing or planned cervical airway incision, preserving strap and recurrent-nerve anatomy and defining the cricoid/tracheal framework.
OR-peds-ltr.step2: Open the airway in the midline and directly inspect the stenotic segment to confirm the required anterior, posterior, or combined expansion.
OR-peds-ltr.step3: For posterior expansion, split the posterior cricoid plate in the midline without extending into the esophageal mucosa; for anterior expansion, divide the anterior cricoid/tracheal scar as planned.
OR-peds-ltr.step4: Harvest and shape rib cartilage with perichondrial orientation appropriate to the graft surface and size it to expand without excessive tension.
OR-peds-ltr.step5: Inset the posterior and/or anterior cartilage graft securely into the split framework, ensuring it is stable and not protruding dangerously into the lumen.
OR-peds-ltr.step6: Place the stent or appropriately sized ETT as planned to support the reconstruction while avoiding pressure injury.
OR-peds-ltr.step7: Close the airway/framework and soft tissues around the reconstructed lumen, maintaining graft position and a tension-free tracheal/skin relationship.
OR-peds-ltr.step8: Confirm tube/stent position endoscopically and document the postoperative airway, sedation, reflux, and extubation/stent-removal plan.

## Supraglottoplasty for Severe Laryngomalacia  (slug: supraglottoplasty, region: Pediatric Otolaryngology)

A-supraglottoplasty.summary: Supraglottoplasty relieves inspiratory collapse of the supraglottis by dividing short aryepiglottic folds and trimming redundant arytenoid mucosa. Safety comes from limited, superficial cuts that stay medial to the piriform sinus floor (internal SLN, sensation) and away from the interarytenoid mucosa (supraglottic or posterior glottic stenosis).
A-supraglottoplasty.layer1: Suspension and survey — vallecula; epiglottis (tubular or omega shape); aryepiglottic folds; arytenoids; true vocal folds; subglottis and trachea. Complete DLB first to exclude synchronous lesions; inspect dynamic collapse with spontaneous breathing before suspension when possible.
A-supraglottoplasty.layer2: Identify the collapsing components — supra-arytenoid mucosa over cuneiform and corniculate cartilages (anterior prolapse); short AE folds tethering the epiglottis; posteriorly displaced epiglottis. Classify the dominant pattern (Olney type 1 mucosal prolapse, type 2 short AE folds, type 3 epiglottic retroflexion) because each needs a different cut.
A-supraglottoplasty.layer3: Aryepiglottic fold division — aryepiglottic fold (mucosa, aryepiglottic muscle fibers); lateral edge of epiglottis; piriform sinus (lateral). Divide the fold with cold scissors or CO2 laser near its epiglottic end, releasing the epiglottis; keep the cut superficial and medial to the piriform sinus.
A-supraglottoplasty.layer4: Arytenoid mucosal trim — redundant mucosa over cuneiform and corniculate cartilages; lateral (posterolateral) arytenoid mucosa; interarytenoid mucosa (preserve). Excise only mucosa that prolapses into the glottis on inspiration; leave the cartilage and interarytenoid mucosa intact.
A-supraglottoplasty.layer5: Contralateral side — opposite AE fold; opposite arytenoid mucosa. Repeat symmetrically or stage the second side in high-risk patients to limit raw surfaces.
A-supraglottoplasty.layer6: Epiglottopexy if indicated — lingual surface of epiglottis; vallecula; tongue base. Denude opposing vallecular and lingual epiglottic mucosa and suture the epiglottis forward to the tongue base for posterior epiglottic collapse.
A-supraglottoplasty.layer7: Final inspection — true vocal folds; subglottis; cut edges. Check hemostasis and absence of thermal injury to the folds.
A-supraglottoplasty.landmark1: Aryepiglottic fold — Mucosal fold running from the lateral edge of the epiglottis posteriorly to the arytenoid apex; the cuneiform bulge sits in its posterior part and the corniculate at its end (Short folds are the main target of release)
A-supraglottoplasty.landmark2: Piriform sinus — Gutter lateral to the AE fold, bounded laterally by the thyroid cartilage and thyrohyoid membrane (The internal SLN runs submucosally in its lateral wall/floor; deep lateral cuts denervate the supraglottis)
A-supraglottoplasty.landmark3: Interarytenoid mucosa — Midline posterior mucosa between the corniculate cartilages, directly above the posterior commissure (Preserve it to prevent posterior glottic and supraglottic stenosis)
A-supraglottoplasty.landmark4: Cuneiform cartilages — Small rounded elevations within the posterior AE folds, anterior and lateral to the corniculates (Redundant mucosa over them prolapses into the airway; trim mucosa, not cartilage support)
A-supraglottoplasty.danger1: Internal branch of the superior laryngeal nerve — where: Pierces the thyrohyoid membrane and runs submucosally in the lateral wall/floor of the piriform sinus to the supraglottis; protect: Keep AE fold cuts superficial and medial; avoid lateral extension into the piriform sinus; injury: Supraglottic sensory loss, aspiration and feeding difficulty
A-supraglottoplasty.danger2: Interarytenoid / posterior commissure mucosa — where: Posterior midline between the arytenoids; protect: Leave an intact mucosal bridge; avoid circumferential or bilateral contiguous raw surfaces; injury: Supraglottic or posterior glottic stenosis
A-supraglottoplasty.danger3: True vocal folds — where: Just below the arytenoid trim area; protect: Laser shielding, low power and short pulses, cold technique where possible; injury: Thermal injury, web, scarring
A-supraglottoplasty.danger4: Epiglottis — where: Free margin and lateral edges; protect: Avoid overresection of epiglottic edge; injury: Aspiration, epiglottic deformity
A-supraglottoplasty.variant1: Olney type 1 (anterior prolapse of arytenoid/cuneiform mucosa), type 2 (short AE folds), type 3 (posterior epiglottic displacement); mixed patterns are common
A-supraglottoplasty.variant2: Tubular or omega-shaped epiglottis
A-supraglottoplasty.variant3: Synchronous airway lesions (subglottic stenosis, tracheomalacia)
A-supraglottoplasty.variant4: State-dependent (sleep-only) or late-onset laryngomalacia, often with neurologic disease
A-supraglottoplasty.supply1: Supraglottic sensation — Internal branch of SLN (from vagus) through the thyrohyoid membrane with the superior laryngeal artery
A-supraglottoplasty.supply2: Arterial supply — Superior laryngeal artery (from superior thyroid artery)
A-supraglottoplasty.supply3: Motor — Aryepiglottic and interarytenoid muscles: RLN
A-supraglottoplasty.supply4: Reflex arc — Laryngeal adductor reflex: afferent internal SLN, efferent RLN

OR-supraglottoplasty.step4: Trim only clearly redundant supra-arytenoid mucosa while preserving the interarytenoid/posterior commissure mucosa to reduce stenosis risk.
OR-supraglottoplasty.step5: Repeat the planned release on the opposite side while maintaining symmetry and avoiding excessive bilateral raw surfaces.
OR-supraglottoplasty.step6: Reassess epiglottic prolapse and supraglottic opening during spontaneous respiration when feasible; perform epiglottopexy only when specifically indicated.
OR-supraglottoplasty.step7: Control focal bleeding and inspect the true folds/subglottis for thermal or mechanical injury.
OR-supraglottoplasty.step8: End with a clearly documented postoperative airway/feeding observation plan because edema and aspiration risk can worsen transiently after surgery.

## Sistrunk Procedure  (slug: thyroglossal, region: Pediatric Otolaryngology)

A-thyroglossal.summary: The Sistrunk procedure removes a thyroglossal duct cyst with the central hyoid body and a core of tongue-base tissue toward the foramen cecum, following the descent path of the thyroid. Safety and cure depend on taking only the central hyoid (medial to the lesser cornua) so the hypoglossal nerves and lingual arteries lateral to the greater cornua are safe, and on confirming normal thyroid tissue in the neck first.
A-thyroglossal.layer1: Incision and flaps — skin crease over the cyst; platysma; anterior jugular veins. Horizontal incision over the cyst, often at hyoid level; ellipse around any sinus or prior scar. Raise subplatysmal flaps to above the hyoid.
A-thyroglossal.layer2: Cyst and strap muscles — sternohyoid (superficial); thyrohyoid and sternothyroid (deeper); thyrohyoid membrane; cyst wall. The cyst usually lies in the midline over the thyrohyoid membrane, deep to or between the straps; free it without rupture.
A-thyroglossal.layer3: Hyoid body — hyoid body; lesser cornua; mylohyoid and geniohyoid (superior attachments); sternohyoid and thyrohyoid (inferior attachments). Detach muscles from the central hyoid and divide the bone on each side of the tract, medial to the lesser cornua (about 1-1.5 cm central segment).
A-thyroglossal.layer4: Suprahyoid core — geniohyoid; genioglossus; tongue base toward foramen cecum. Take a core of tissue angled upward and backward toward the foramen cecum; a finger in the mouth on the foramen cecum guides direction.
A-thyroglossal.layer5: Pharyngeal limit — vallecula / tongue base mucosa; foramen cecum. Divide the core just short of mucosa; close any mucosal entry primarily.
A-thyroglossal.layer6: Closure — strap muscles; platysma; drain (selective). Reapproximate straps over the hyoid gap; the hyoid ends do not need to be wired.
A-thyroglossal.landmark1: Hyoid body — Palpable horizontal bar above the thyroid notch; the thyroid cartilage is below the thyrohyoid membrane. In infants the hyoid may sit close to or overlap the thyroid cartilage (Avoid mistaking the thyroid cartilage for the hyoid)
A-thyroglossal.landmark2: Lesser cornua — Small upward projections at the junction of the hyoid body and greater cornua (Lateral limit of the bone cut)
A-thyroglossal.landmark3: Foramen cecum — Midline pit at the apex of the sulcus terminalis (junction of anterior two thirds and posterior third of the tongue); press on it from the mouth (Target of the suprahyoid core)
A-thyroglossal.landmark4: Cyst tract — Midline fibrous cord from the cyst upward to the hyoid, passing anterior to, through or posterior to the bone (Must be removed en bloc for cure)
A-thyroglossal.danger1: Hypoglossal nerves — where: Lateral, running above the greater cornua of the hyoid between hyoglossus and mylohyoid; protect: Keep the bone cut medial to the lesser cornua and the core in the midline; injury: Tongue weakness, dysarthria, dysphagia
A-thyroglossal.danger2: Lingual arteries — where: Deep to hyoglossus just above the greater cornua; protect: Midline dissection; injury: Bleeding, tongue base hematoma
A-thyroglossal.danger3: Pharynx at the vallecula — where: Deep and superior to the core; protect: Stop short of mucosa; close any entry in layers; injury: Pharyngocutaneous fistula, infection
A-thyroglossal.danger4: Internal branch of SLN — where: Pierces the thyrohyoid membrane laterally; protect: Avoid lateral dissection on the membrane; injury: Supraglottic sensory loss
A-thyroglossal.danger5: Thyroid cartilage / airway — where: Just below the hyoid, especially in infants; protect: Confirm the hyoid before cutting; injury: Laryngeal framework injury
A-thyroglossal.variant1: Ectopic thyroid in the cyst may be the only functioning thyroid tissue
A-thyroglossal.variant2: Lingual thyroid at the foramen cecum
A-thyroglossal.variant3: Pyramidal lobe extending up toward the hyoid
A-thyroglossal.variant4: Off-midline or suprahyoid/intralingual cysts
A-thyroglossal.variant5: Papillary carcinoma in a small percentage (about 1%) of adult cysts
A-thyroglossal.supply1: Arterial — Branches of the superior thyroid and lingual arteries near the hyoid
A-thyroglossal.supply2: Venous — Anterior jugular veins superficially
A-thyroglossal.supply3: Nerves at risk — Hypoglossal (lateral above the hyoid), internal SLN (lateral thyrohyoid membrane)
A-thyroglossal.supply4: Hyoid muscles — Suprahyoid: mylohyoid, geniohyoid, digastric, stylohyoid; infrahyoid: sternohyoid, omohyoid, thyrohyoid
A-thyroglossal.pimp1: Q: Where does the thyroid originate? A: Foramen cecum, at the junction of the anterior two thirds and posterior third of the tongue
A-thyroglossal.pimp2: Q: What is the embryology of the hyoid? A: Second arch: lesser cornua and upper body; third arch: greater cornua and lower body
A-thyroglossal.pimp3: Q: Why is the central hyoid removed? A: The duct descends in close relation to the developing hyoid and may pass through it; leaving it raises recurrence
A-thyroglossal.pimp4: Q: What is the recurrence rate after Sistrunk versus simple excision? A: Roughly 3-5% after Sistrunk (most series under 10%) versus about 40-70% after cyst excision alone in commonly cited series
A-thyroglossal.pimp5: Q: What is the most common congenital neck mass in children? A: Thyroglossal duct cyst
A-thyroglossal.pimp6: Q: Why does a thyroglossal duct cyst elevate with tongue protrusion? A: The tract attaches to the foramen cecum and hyoid
A-thyroglossal.pimp7: Q: What must be confirmed before excision? A: Presence of a normal thyroid gland in the neck
A-thyroglossal.pimp8: Q: What is the most common malignancy in a thyroglossal duct cyst? A: Papillary thyroid carcinoma
OR-thyroglossal.step1: Make the transverse incision over the cyst, elevate subplatysmal flaps, and identify the cyst/tract without rupturing it.
OR-thyroglossal.step2: Dissect the tract superiorly toward the hyoid while separating it from strap muscles and preserving normal laryngeal framework.
OR-thyroglossal.step3: Expose the central hyoid body and divide it on each side of the tract, removing the central hyoid segment with the specimen.
OR-thyroglossal.step4: Continue the tract dissection superiorly through the tongue-base direction toward the foramen cecum, taking a small core of tissue rather than stopping at the hyoid.
OR-thyroglossal.step5: Avoid entry into pharyngeal mucosa; if entered, close the defect primarily.
OR-thyroglossal.step6: Remove the cyst, central hyoid, and superior tract as one specimen.
OR-thyroglossal.step7: Inspect for bleeding and confirm no residual epithelial tract remains in the operative field.
OR-thyroglossal.step8: Reapproximate strap tissues and close the neck in layers, with drain selectively based on dead space.

## Tonsillectomy  (slug: tonsillectomy, region: Pediatric Otolaryngology)

A-tonsillectomy.summary: Tonsillectomy removes the palatine tonsil in the areolar plane between its capsule and the superior constrictor. Safety depends on staying on the capsule: lateral to the constrictor lie the parapharyngeal space and the carotid arteries, and at the lower pole the glossopharyngeal nerve and the main arterial supply (tonsillar branch of the facial artery) sit just deep to the fossa.
A-tonsillectomy.layer1: Mouth gag and exposure — maxillary teeth; tongue (under gag blade); uvula and soft palate; anterior and posterior tonsillar pillars; posterior pharyngeal wall. Seat the blade in the midline with the endotracheal tube in its groove; suspend without excess tongue compression. Palpate the posterior wall for an aberrant pulsatile carotid when syndromic (e.g., 22q11 deletion).
A-tonsillectomy.layer2: Superior pole mucosal incision — anterior pillar (palatoglossus muscle); supratonsillar fossa; tonsillar capsule. Retract the tonsil medially and incise the mucosa just lateral to the anterior pillar at the superior pole to find the white capsule.
A-tonsillectomy.layer3: Capsular plane — tonsillar capsule (pharyngobasilar fascia condensation); loose areolar plane; superior constrictor muscle (lateral bed); paratonsillar (external palatine) vein. Stay on the capsule with traction medially; the constrictor fibers are horizontal and should stay in the fossa.
A-tonsillectomy.layer4: Posterior pillar and mid-fossa — posterior pillar (palatopharyngeus muscle); tonsillar bed vessels. Separate the tonsil from the posterior pillar without cutting palatopharyngeus, preserving palatal function.
A-tonsillectomy.layer5: Lower pole — plica triangularis; lingual tonsil junction; tonsillar branch of facial artery; dorsal lingual vessels; glossopharyngeal nerve (deep, at the lower pole lateral to the constrictor). Release the lower pole under vision; avoid deep cautery and deep suture ligatures here.
A-tonsillectomy.layer6: Hemostasis and inspection — superior pole; mid-fossa; lower pole; pillars. Use targeted cautery only; release the gag and recheck at normal venous pressure.
A-tonsillectomy.landmark1: Capsular plane — At the superior pole incise just inside the anterior pillar; the capsule is a white, smooth fibrous layer and the plane opens with blunt pressure (The bloodless plane that keeps you off the constrictor)
A-tonsillectomy.landmark2: Superior constrictor — Horizontal, pink muscle fibers forming the lateral wall of the fossa after the tonsil is lifted (Fibers on the specimen mean you are too deep)
A-tonsillectomy.landmark3: Lower pole vascular pedicle — At the lower pole near the tongue base junction, where the tonsillar branch of the facial artery enters the lateral surface (Main bleeding point; controlled precisely)
A-tonsillectomy.landmark4: Paratonsillar (external palatine) vein — Descends from the soft palate on the lateral surface of the capsule near the superior pole (Common source of superior pole bleeding)
A-tonsillectomy.landmark5: Internal carotid artery position — Posterolateral to the fossa, classically about 2-2.5 cm away in adults but much closer in children (MRI means under about 1 cm, minimum about 2 mm); palpate the lateral and posterior pharyngeal walls for pulsation (Medial displacement in syndromic children changes risk)
A-tonsillectomy.danger1: Glossopharyngeal nerve — where: Lateral to the superior constrictor at the lower pole of the fossa, entering the tongue base; protect: Stay on the capsule, minimize deep lower-pole cautery and deep sutures; injury: Loss of taste and sensation to the posterior third of the tongue, pharyngeal pain
A-tonsillectomy.danger2: Internal and external carotid arteries — where: Parapharyngeal space lateral to the constrictor; ICA posterolateral, ECA anterolateral to the fossa; protect: No dissection lateral to the constrictor, no deep suture ligatures, palpate for aberrant pulsation; injury: Massive hemorrhage, pseudoaneurysm
A-tonsillectomy.danger3: Palatopharyngeus and soft palate — where: Posterior pillar and superior pole; protect: Preserve pillars and uvula; avoid cautery burns of the palate; injury: Velopharyngeal insufficiency, nasopharyngeal stenosis
A-tonsillectomy.danger4: Lingual and hypoglossal nerves; teeth — where: Under the gag blade and tongue base; protect: Limit suspension time and pressure; check teeth; injury: Temporary taste disturbance or tongue numbness/weakness, dental injury
A-tonsillectomy.danger5: Atlantoaxial region — where: Prevertebral space behind the posterior wall; protect: Avoid deep posterior wall cautery; caution with neck extension in Down syndrome; injury: Grisel syndrome (atlantoaxial subluxation)
A-tonsillectomy.variant1: Medialized or tortuous internal carotid (22q11.2 deletion, velocardiofacial syndrome)
A-tonsillectomy.variant2: Elongated styloid process palpable in the fossa (Eagle syndrome)
A-tonsillectomy.variant3: Submucous cleft palate (bifid uvula, zona pellucida, notched hard palate) affecting VPI risk
A-tonsillectomy.variant4: Tonsil extending low into the lingual tonsil or high into the soft palate
A-tonsillectomy.supply1: Arterial supply (five arteries) — Tonsillar branch of facial (main, lower pole), ascending palatine (facial), dorsal lingual (lingual), ascending pharyngeal (ECA), lesser palatine (descending palatine of maxillary)
A-tonsillectomy.supply2: Venous — Paratonsillar (external palatine) vein to the pharyngeal plexus and facial vein
A-tonsillectomy.supply3: Sensory — Glossopharyngeal nerve tonsillar branches and lesser palatine nerves (V2)
A-tonsillectomy.supply4: Lymphatics — Jugulodigastric (level II) nodes
A-tonsillectomy.pimp1: Q: Name the five arteries of the palatine tonsil. A: Tonsillar branch of facial, ascending palatine, dorsal lingual, ascending pharyngeal, lesser palatine
A-tonsillectomy.pimp2: Q: Which artery is the main supply? A: Tonsillar branch of the facial artery, entering the lower pole
A-tonsillectomy.pimp3: Q: What muscles form the tonsillar pillars? A: Anterior: palatoglossus; posterior: palatopharyngeus
A-tonsillectomy.pimp4: Q: What forms the lateral wall of the tonsillar fossa? A: Superior constrictor muscle (with the pharyngobasilar fascia)
A-tonsillectomy.pimp5: Q: Through which nerve is post-tonsillectomy otalgia referred? A: Glossopharyngeal nerve, via its tympanic (Jacobson) branch
A-tonsillectomy.pimp6: Q: From which pharyngeal pouch does the palatine tonsil develop? A: Second pharyngeal pouch
A-tonsillectomy.pimp7: Q: Which syndrome has medialized internal carotid arteries? A: 22q11.2 deletion (velocardiofacial) syndrome
A-tonsillectomy.pimp8: Q: What is Grisel syndrome? A: Nontraumatic atlantoaxial subluxation from inflammation after pharyngeal infection or surgery
OR-tonsillectomy.step1: Insert and suspend the mouth gag with tongue/teeth protected, confirming full visualization of both tonsils and the posterior pharynx.
OR-tonsillectomy.step2: Grasp the first tonsil medially and make the superior-pole mucosal incision to enter the plane between capsule and superior constrictor.
OR-tonsillectomy.step3: Follow the tonsillar capsule inferiorly with traction-countertraction, keeping dissection on the capsule and out of pharyngeal muscle.
OR-tonsillectomy.step4: Release the lower pole under direct vision and control feeding vessels without deep lateral thermal injury.
OR-tonsillectomy.step5: Inspect the entire fossa and obtain targeted hemostasis, then repeat the complete dissection on the opposite side.
OR-tonsillectomy.step6: Irrigate/suction and release suspension briefly so occult bleeding appears at a more physiologic pressure.
OR-tonsillectomy.step7: Re-suspend and inspect superior pole, mid-fossa, and lower pole bilaterally; control any persistent bleeding point directly.
OR-tonsillectomy.step8: Remove the gag after confirming no dental/oral injury, retained sponge, or active hemorrhage.

## Pediatric Tonsillectomy ± Adenoidectomy  (slug: tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy, region: Pediatric Otolaryngology)

A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.summary: Removing hypertrophic palatine tonsils and adenoid enlarges the oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal airway. Safety depends on staying in the areolar plane between the tonsil capsule and the superior constrictor, because the glossopharyngeal nerve and the carotid system lie just beyond that muscle, and on limiting adenoid removal to tissue above the palatal contact zone without violating the prevertebral fascia or the torus tubarius.
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.layer1: Exposure with the mouth gag — teeth / alveolar ridge; tongue (depressed by the blade); soft palate and uvula; anterior pillar (palatoglossus); posterior pillar (palatopharyngeus). Look for a submucous cleft (bifid uvula, midline zona pellucida, notched posterior hard palate) before any adenoid work. Check that the blade does not pinch the lips or tongue against the teeth.
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.layer2: Tonsillar mucosa and capsule — tonsillar crypts; plica triangularis (anteroinferior fold); supratonsillar fossa (upper pole); tonsil capsule (pharyngobasilar fascia condensation). Retract the tonsil medially and incise mucosa just lateral to the anterior pillar edge at the upper pole to find the capsule.
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.layer3: Peritonsillar plane — loose areolar tissue (peritonsillar space); superior constrictor fibers (lateral bed); palatopharyngeus fibers (posterior bed). The capsule is lateral to the tonsil; the white glistening plane is between capsule and constrictor. Muscle fibers in the specimen mean you are too deep (lateral).
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.layer4: Upper and lower pole vessels — ascending pharyngeal and lesser (descending) palatine (upper pole); tonsillar branch of facial artery (main supply, enters lower pole laterally); ascending palatine and dorsal lingual branches (lower pole); paratonsillar (external palatine) vein. Most significant intraoperative and secondary bleeding arises at the lower pole and from the paratonsillar vein in the lateral bed. Control the lower pole fully before releasing it from the tongue base.
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.layer5: Tonsillar bed (lateral wall) — superior constrictor; styloglossus and stylopharyngeus deeper; glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX) at the lower bed; parapharyngeal fat and ICA/ECA beyond. Keep cautery superficial in the lower bed. CN IX runs with stylopharyngeus just deep to the constrictor near the lower pole; deep burns cause taste change and referred otalgia beyond the usual.
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.layer6: Nasopharynx (adenoid) — adenoid pad on posterior-superior wall; choanae and posterior vomer (anterior boundary); torus tubarius and Eustachian tube orifices (lateral); fossa of Rosenmüller (posterior to torus); pharyngobasilar fascia / prevertebral fascia (deep). Retract the palate and inspect with a mirror or endoscope. Remove tissue from the posterior-superior wall; leave a cuff at the inferior edge where the palate contacts the adenoid (Passavant ridge region) in a child at risk of VPI.
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.layer7: Adenoid bed hemostasis — ascending pharyngeal artery branches; pharyngeal branches of maxillary artery; longus capitis deep to fascia. Use targeted cautery. Broad, deep cautery on the posterior wall risks atlantoaxial ligament inflammation (Grisel syndrome) and nasopharyngeal stenosis.
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.landmark1: Capsular plane at the upper pole — Grasp the upper pole, pull medially and inferiorly; incise mucosa at the junction of the anterior pillar and tonsil in the supratonsillar fossa and spread until white capsule appears (Entering correctly at the upper pole defines the plane for the rest of the dissection)
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.landmark2: Superior constrictor — Pink-red horizontal muscle fibers lateral to the areolar plane; visible as the floor of the empty fossa (Stay medial to it; it is the barrier to the parapharyngeal space)
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.landmark3: Lower pole vascular pedicle — Where the tonsil narrows toward the tongue base, inferior and lateral; the tonsillar branch of the facial artery enters here (Main arterial supply and site of brisk bleeding)
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.landmark4: Torus tubarius — Cartilaginous ridge on the lateral nasopharyngeal wall just behind the inferior turbinate's posterior end, with the ET orifice in front of it (Lateral limit of adenoid curettage; injury causes ET scarring and stenosis)
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.landmark5: Choanae and vomer — With the mirror, the posterior edge of the vomer splits the two choanae; the adenoid sits above and behind them (Confirms anterior airway clearance and the superior extent of the pad)
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.landmark6: Palatal contact zone (Passavant ridge region) — The level on the posterior pharyngeal wall where the elevated soft palate touches during closure, at about the level of the hard palate plane (Preserve the inferior adenoid in children at risk of VPI (submucous cleft, 22q11.2 deletion, neuromuscular disease))
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.danger1: Internal carotid artery — where: Posterolateral to the tonsillar fossa; classically taught as about 2 to 2.5 cm away, but imaging studies measure much less (in children, mean inferior-pole distances under about 8 to 9 mm with minimums near 2 mm), so do not rely on a fixed margin; may be medialized (tortuous ICA, 22q11.2 deletion); protect: Stay in the capsular plane; palpate the lateral pharyngeal wall for pulsation before surgery in syndromic children; injury: Catastrophic hemorrhage
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.danger2: Glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX) — where: Deep to the superior constrictor at the lower half of the tonsillar bed, alongside stylopharyngeus; protect: Avoid deep lower-bed cautery and deep sutures; injury: Loss of taste and sensation of posterior tongue, persistent otalgia, dysphagia
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.danger3: Anterior and posterior pillars, uvula — where: Borders of the tonsillar fossa; protect: Incise mucosa at the pillar edge, not through the pillar; avoid the uvula with cautery; injury: Velopharyngeal insufficiency, scarring, nasopharyngeal stenosis if posterior pillar and adenoid bed are both raw
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.danger4: Torus tubarius / Eustachian tube — where: Lateral nasopharyngeal wall; protect: Keep curette or microdebrider in the midline posterior wall; injury: ET stenosis, OME, bleeding
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.danger5: Prevertebral fascia and C1-C2 ligaments — where: Deep to the adenoid bed on the posterior wall; protect: Shallow resection and limited cautery, particularly in Down syndrome (atlantoaxial instability); injury: Grisel syndrome (atlantoaxial subluxation), cervical spine injury
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.danger6: Teeth, lips and tongue — where: Under the gag; protect: Seat the gag carefully, check for loose teeth before and after; injury: Dental avulsion, lingual nerve compression, tongue edema
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.variant1: Submucous cleft palate (bifid uvula, zona pellucida, palpable notch) raises VPI risk after adenoidectomy
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.variant2: Medialized or tortuous internal carotid artery in the retropharynx, notably in 22q11.2 deletion syndrome
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.variant3: Lingual tonsil hypertrophy and other sites of lymphoid tissue causing residual obstruction
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.variant4: Atlantoaxial instability in Down syndrome
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.variant5: Elongated styloid process (Eagle) palpable in the tonsillar fossa
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.supply1: Tonsillar arteries — Tonsillar branch of facial artery (dominant), ascending palatine (facial), dorsal lingual (lingual), ascending pharyngeal (ECA), descending/lesser palatine (maxillary)
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.supply2: Venous — Paratonsillar (external palatine) vein in the lateral bed draining to the pharyngeal plexus and facial vein
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.supply3: Adenoid supply — Ascending pharyngeal, ascending palatine, pharyngeal branch of maxillary artery, artery of the pterygoid canal
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.supply4: Sensory — Tonsil: CN IX and lesser palatine nerves; referred otalgia via Jacobson nerve (CN IX)
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.supply5: Palate motor — Levator and palatopharyngeus via pharyngeal plexus (CN X); tensor veli palatini via V3
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.pimp1: Q: What forms the lateral wall (bed) of the tonsillar fossa? A: Superior constrictor muscle, with pharyngobasilar fascia; the capsule lies medial to it
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.pimp2: Q: What are the anterior and posterior tonsillar pillars? A: Anterior: palatoglossus; posterior: palatopharyngeus
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.pimp3: Q: Main arterial supply to the palatine tonsil? A: Tonsillar branch of the facial artery, entering the lower pole
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.pimp4: Q: Which nerve is at risk in the lower tonsillar bed and what symptom results? A: Glossopharyngeal nerve; loss of posterior-tongue taste and referred otalgia
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.pimp5: Q: From which pharyngeal pouch do the palatine tonsils develop? A: Second pharyngeal pouch
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.pimp6: Q: What is Waldeyer's ring? A: Adenoid (pharyngeal tonsil), tubal tonsils, palatine tonsils and lingual tonsil
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.pimp7: Q: Three signs of submucous cleft palate? A: Bifid uvula, zona pellucida (midline translucency), notch in the posterior hard palate (Calnan triad)
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.pimp8: Q: What is Grisel syndrome? A: Non-traumatic atlantoaxial rotatory subluxation from inflammation spreading via pharyngovertebral venous connections after nasopharyngeal infection or surgery
A-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.pimp9: Q: Where is the fossa of Rosenmüller? A: Lateral pharyngeal recess posterior and superior to the torus tubarius
OR-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.step1: Insert and suspend the mouth gag with teeth/tongue protected, inspect the palate for submucous cleft, and expose both tonsils before starting.
OR-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.step2: Grasp the first tonsil medially, enter the plane between capsule and superior constrictor at the superior pole, and follow the capsule to the lower pole with traction-countertraction.
OR-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.step3: Control focal tonsillar-bed bleeding and repeat the full capsular or planned intracapsular dissection on the opposite tonsil.
OR-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.step4: Retract the soft palate and visualize the adenoid pad, choanae, vomer, and both Eustachian-tube orifices with mirror/endoscope.
OR-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.step5: Remove obstructing adenoid tissue while protecting the torus tubarius and avoiding excessive inferior resection in a child at risk for velopharyngeal insufficiency.
OR-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.step6: Inspect the nasopharynx for residual choanal obstruction and obtain targeted adenoid-bed hemostasis rather than broad deep cautery.
OR-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.step7: Irrigate/suction and briefly release suspension so occult tonsillar or adenoid bleeding declares itself at more physiologic pressure.
OR-tonsillectomy-adenoidectomy.step8: Reinspect both tonsillar fossae and nasopharynx, remove all packing/instruments, and confirm no dental/oral injury or active hemorrhage before emergence.

## Myringotomy with Tympanostomy Tube Placement  (slug: tympanostomy-tubes, region: Pediatric Otolaryngology)

A-tympanostomy-tubes.summary: A myringotomy vents a middle ear whose Eustachian tube cannot equalize pressure, and the tube keeps that vent open for months. Safety depends on placing the incision in the anterior or inferior pars tensa, away from the posterosuperior quadrant where the incus long process, stapes and chorda tympani lie just medial to the drum, and on recognizing a high jugular bulb or aberrant vessel behind an inferior drum.
A-tympanostomy-tubes.layer1: Ear canal — cartilaginous lateral third (hair, cerumen glands); bony medial two-thirds (thin skin, no adnexa); anterior canal wall bulge; tympanic annulus. Pull the pinna posterosuperiorly in older children (posteriorly and inferiorly in infants, whose bony canal is not yet developed) to straighten the canal. The medial bony canal skin is thin and bleeds or tears easily, so keep the speculum in the cartilaginous canal.
A-tympanostomy-tubes.layer2: Tympanic membrane survey — pars flaccida (above the lateral process of malleus); pars tensa; lateral (short) process and manubrium of malleus; umbo; cone of light (anteroinferior). Orient by the manubrium: it runs from the lateral process superiorly to the umbo centrally and slopes posteriorly. Divide the pars tensa into four quadrants with a line along the manubrium and a perpendicular through the umbo.
A-tympanostomy-tubes.layer3: Myringotomy — outer epithelial layer; middle fibrous layer (radial and circular fibers); inner mucosal layer. Make a short radial (or circumferential) incision in the anteroinferior or anterosuperior quadrant (some surgeons use posteroinferior). A radial cut parallels the radial fibers and tends to gape less. Do not plunge: the promontory sits only about 2 mm medial to the umbo.
A-tympanostomy-tubes.layer4: Middle ear space beneath the incision — promontory (basal turn of cochlea); Eustachian tube orifice (anterosuperior, protympanum); hypotympanum and jugular bulb floor (inferior); round window niche (posteroinferior). Suction the effusion without passing the tip deep into the mesotympanum. The incision overlies the protympanum or hypotympanum, both away from the ossicles.
A-tympanostomy-tubes.layer5: Tube placement and seating — medial flange (in the middle ear); lateral flange (in the canal); fibrous layer of the TM gripping the tube shaft. Engage the inner flange through the incision and rotate it into place so the drum sits between the flanges and the lumen points to the middle ear, not against the promontory or canal skin.
A-tympanostomy-tubes.landmark1: Lateral process of malleus — The small white knob at the top of the manubrium, just below the pars flaccida, where the anterior and posterior malleolar folds meet (Fixes superior orientation. Everything posterosuperior to it overlies the incus and stapes.)
A-tympanostomy-tubes.landmark2: Umbo and manubrium — Follow the white handle of the malleus down and back from the lateral process to its tip at the center of the drum (Divides the drum into anterior and posterior halves so you can pick a safe quadrant)
A-tympanostomy-tubes.landmark3: Anteroinferior quadrant — Below and in front of the umbo, at the cone of light; it may be hidden behind the anterior canal bulge, so angle the speculum anteriorly (Usual myringotomy site, away from ossicles, and the tube can sit there over the protympanum)
A-tympanostomy-tubes.landmark4: Incus long process and stapes — Seen through a thin or retracted posterosuperior drum just behind the upper manubrium; may be outlined as a white line behind the membrane (Zone to avoid with the knife and the tube)
A-tympanostomy-tubes.landmark5: Tympanic annulus — The thickened gray-white rim where the drum meets the bony canal; it is missing superiorly at the notch of Rivinus (pars flaccida) (Incisions that reach the annulus are more likely to leave a persistent perforation; keep the incision within the pars tensa)
A-tympanostomy-tubes.danger1: Incudostapedial joint and stapes — where: Medial to the posterosuperior quadrant, a few millimeters deep to the drum; protect: Avoid posterosuperior incisions; use a short, shallow cut; injury: Ossicular discontinuity with conductive hearing loss; stapes subluxation with vertigo and possible SNHL
A-tympanostomy-tubes.danger2: Chorda tympani — where: Runs medial to the posterior and upper drum, crossing between the incus long process and malleus neck; protect: Stay out of the posterosuperior quadrant; injury: Altered taste on the ipsilateral anterior two-thirds of the tongue
A-tympanostomy-tubes.danger3: High or dehiscent jugular bulb — where: Floor of the hypotympanum; may bulge behind the inferior or posteroinferior drum as a blue mass; protect: Look for a blue hue behind the inferior drum before cutting; incise anteriorly if suspicious; injury: Brisk venous bleeding from the ear
A-tympanostomy-tubes.danger4: Aberrant internal carotid artery or persistent stapedial artery — where: Aberrant ICA crosses the mesotympanum and promontory, seen as a red mass behind the anteroinferior drum; protect: Do not incise over a pulsatile red retrotympanic mass; stop and image; injury: Life-threatening arterial hemorrhage
A-tympanostomy-tubes.danger5: Bony canal skin — where: Thin skin over the medial bony canal and the anterior canal bulge; protect: Keep the speculum lateral and use gentle cerumen removal; injury: Canal laceration and bleeding that obscures the drum
A-tympanostomy-tubes.variant1: High or dehiscent jugular bulb in the hypotympanum (blue retrotympanic mass)
A-tympanostomy-tubes.variant2: Aberrant internal carotid artery crossing the middle ear (red, pulsatile mass anteroinferiorly); may coexist with a persistent stapedial artery
A-tympanostomy-tubes.variant3: Prominent anterior canal wall hiding the anteroinferior quadrant, so a different quadrant or angled approach is needed
A-tympanostomy-tubes.variant4: Atelectatic or retracted drum draped over the incus and stapes, which brings them closer to the knife
A-tympanostomy-tubes.variant5: Syndromic ears (Down syndrome, craniofacial anomalies) with narrow, stenotic canals
A-tympanostomy-tubes.supply1: Arterial supply of TM — Outer surface from the deep auricular branch of the maxillary artery; inner surface from the anterior tympanic (maxillary) and stylomastoid (posterior auricular) branches
A-tympanostomy-tubes.supply2: Sensory, lateral TM and canal — Auriculotemporal nerve (V3) anterosuperiorly; auricular branch of vagus (Arnold nerve) posteroinferiorly; facial nerve sensory twigs
A-tympanostomy-tubes.supply3: Sensory, medial TM — Tympanic branch of glossopharyngeal (Jacobson nerve) via the tympanic plexus on the promontory
A-tympanostomy-tubes.supply4: Eustachian tube opening — Tensor veli palatini (V3), assisted by levator veli palatini (vagus via pharyngeal plexus)
A-tympanostomy-tubes.pimp1: Q: What are the three layers of the tympanic membrane? A: Lateral squamous epithelium (from ectoderm), middle fibrous layer (mesoderm), medial mucosa (endoderm)
A-tympanostomy-tubes.pimp2: Q: Why is the posterosuperior quadrant avoided? A: The incus long process, incudostapedial joint, stapes and chorda tympani lie just medial to it
A-tympanostomy-tubes.pimp3: Q: Which part of the TM lacks an organized (radial and circular) fibrous middle layer? A: The pars flaccida (Shrapnell membrane), above the anterior and posterior malleolar folds; its middle layer is only loose, irregular connective tissue
A-tympanostomy-tubes.pimp4: Q: What is the notch of Rivinus? A: The superior deficiency of the tympanic ring where there is no annulus; the pars flaccida attaches to the squama there
A-tympanostomy-tubes.pimp5: Q: Why does the child's Eustachian tube predispose to OME? A: It is shorter (about half the adult length), more horizontal (about 10 degrees versus 45 degrees) and floppier, so tensor veli palatini opening is less effective
A-tympanostomy-tubes.pimp6: Q: Which muscle actively opens the Eustachian tube, and what innervates it? A: Tensor veli palatini, by the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve (V3)
A-tympanostomy-tubes.pimp7: Q: What do you suspect with a pulsatile red mass behind the anteroinferior drum? A: Aberrant internal carotid artery (also glomus tympanicum); do not incise, get CT
A-tympanostomy-tubes.pimp8: Q: Arnold nerve is a branch of which cranial nerve, and what reflex does it explain? A: Vagus (CN X); the cough reflex on canal stimulation
OR-tympanostomy-tubes.step1: Inspect the ear canal and tympanic membrane under the microscope, remove obstructing cerumen, and identify the planned anteroinferior or posteroinferior myringotomy site away from ossicles.
OR-tympanostomy-tubes.step2: Make a small radial myringotomy through the tympanic membrane without plunging the knife into the middle ear.
OR-tympanostomy-tubes.step3: Suction middle-ear effusion through the incision and characterize it without repeatedly traumatizing the middle-ear mucosa.
OR-tympanostomy-tubes.step4: Grasp the tube by its flange and insert the medial flange through the myringotomy under microscopic visualization.
OR-tympanostomy-tubes.step5: Rotate/seat the tube so both flanges are correctly positioned and the lumen is patent rather than buried in canal skin or middle-ear mucosa.
OR-tympanostomy-tubes.step6: Clear blood or mucus from the tube lumen and instill topical drops when indicated.
OR-tympanostomy-tubes.step7: Repeat the complete microscopic procedure on the opposite ear when bilateral tubes are planned, reassessing that side independently rather than assuming identical anatomy.
OR-tympanostomy-tubes.step8: Perform a final bilateral check for tube position, patency, canal trauma, and active bleeding before emergence.

## Bilobed Flap for Nasal Defect  (slug: bilobed-flap, region: Facial Plastics / Trauma)

A-bilobed-flap.summary: The bilobed flap is a double transposition flap that moves the thick, tight skin defect of the lower nose to the looser skin of the upper sidewall and dorsum in two smaller steps. Its success depends on the geometry (a total arc of about 90-100 degrees with a Burow triangle at the pivot), elevation in the submuscular plane just above perichondrium, and wide undermining so that the free alar rim is not pulled up.
A-bilobed-flap.layer1: Design — primary defect (lower nose, usually up to about 1.5 cm); pivot point at a distance about equal to the defect radius; first lobe (about defect size); second lobe (smaller and narrower, ending in a point); Burow triangle between defect and pivot. Zitelli design: each lobe rotates about 45-50 degrees, total about 90-100 degrees. Draw two concentric arcs from the pivot: one through the center of the defect and one at its far edge. The first lobe lies in the looser sidewall/dorsal skin; the second lobe lies higher on the nose where its donor site closes easily.
A-bilobed-flap.layer2: Incision and elevation — skin; subcutaneous fat; nasal SMAS / nasalis; perichondrium and periosteum (left down). Elevate just above the perichondrium and periosteum, deep to the nasal musculature. This plane is relatively bloodless and keeps the vessels that run in and above the muscle layer in the flap.
A-bilobed-flap.layer3: Wide undermining — nasal dorsum; contralateral sidewall; tip and alar skin around the defect; cheek edge at the nasofacial junction. Undermine widely in the same submuscular plane, often over most of the nasal dorsum and sidewalls, so tension is spread and trapdoor deformity is less likely.
A-bilobed-flap.layer4: Transposition — first lobe into the primary defect; second lobe into the first lobe donor site; secondary defect closure. Close the second lobe donor site first, which helps seat the flap. Check that the closure vector of each defect does not pull up on the alar rim.
A-bilobed-flap.layer5: Trimming and deep closure — standing cones at the pivot and the second lobe tip; deep dermal sutures. Trim the first lobe to fit the defect only after it is seated; slight undersizing of the lobes helps avoid pincushioning. Use deep sutures to take tension.
A-bilobed-flap.layer6: Skin closure and checks — alar rim; soft triangle; flap color and capillary refill. Evert skin edges and avoid strangulating the narrow flap base. Look at the nose from below and the side to confirm the alar rim is level.
A-bilobed-flap.landmark1: Pivot point — Mark a point about one defect radius away from the edge of the defect, along the line toward the loose upper skin (laterally based for lateral tip and alar-adjacent defects) (Sets the arcs and the location of the Burow triangle)
A-bilobed-flap.landmark2: Submuscular plane — Cut through skin, fat and the thin nasal muscle until glistening perichondrium or periosteum shows; spread in that layer with scissors (Relatively bloodless and keeps the vascular supply within the flap)
A-bilobed-flap.landmark3: Free alar margin — Mark the alar rim on both sides before design and recheck from the basal view during closure (Most common site of distortion; defects too close to the rim may need a different flap)
A-bilobed-flap.landmark4: Nasal skin zones — Pinch the skin: upper two-thirds (dorsum and sidewalls) is thinner and mobile; lower third (tip and ala) is thick, sebaceous and adherent (Explains where to recruit skin and why lower nose defects need a flap)
A-bilobed-flap.landmark5: Subunit boundaries — Alar groove, tip-dorsum junction and sidewall-dorsum junction (Lets you place scars along borders where possible)
A-bilobed-flap.danger1: Alar rim — where: Free margin just caudal to the lateral tip defect; protect: Keep defects a few millimeters or more from the rim; orient tension away from the rim; widely undermine; injury: Alar retraction, notching, asymmetry
A-bilobed-flap.danger2: Flap blood supply — where: Random-pattern flap fed by the subdermal plexus and the vessels within the nasal musculature (dorsal nasal, lateral nasal, angular branches); protect: Elevate below the muscle; avoid a narrow base; avoid tension; injury: Tip necrosis of the lobes
A-bilobed-flap.danger3: Contour — where: Convex lower nose where the curved flap scars contract; protect: Wide undermining; slight undersizing of the lobes; deep sutures; injury: Pincushion (trapdoor) deformity
A-bilobed-flap.danger4: Standing cones — where: At the pivot (Burow triangle) and at the tip of the second lobe; protect: Excise the Burow triangle at the pivot during design; trim conservatively; injury: Dog-ear and irregular contour
A-bilobed-flap.danger5: Medial canthus — where: Above the upper sidewall where a second lobe may be placed; protect: Keep the second lobe low enough that its donor closure does not distort the medial canthus or lower lid; injury: Lid or canthal distortion
A-bilobed-flap.variant1: Thick, sebaceous tip skin hides scars less well and increases trapdoor risk
A-bilobed-flap.variant2: Very lax older skin allows larger defects; young tight skin limits flap size
A-bilobed-flap.variant3: Laterally based and medially based designs are chosen based on defect position and skin laxity
A-bilobed-flap.supply1: Arterial supply — Random pattern from the subdermal plexus; regional supply from dorsal nasal (ophthalmic), lateral nasal and angular (facial) and columellar (superior labial) arteries
A-bilobed-flap.supply2: Sensory — Infratrochlear, external nasal branch of the anterior ethmoidal nerve and infraorbital nerve
A-bilobed-flap.pimp1: Q: What total arc of rotation does the Zitelli bilobed flap use? A: About 90-100 degrees, roughly 45-50 degrees per lobe
A-bilobed-flap.pimp2: Q: In which plane is a nasal bilobed flap elevated? A: Submuscular, just above the perichondrium and periosteum
A-bilobed-flap.pimp3: Q: Where is a Burow triangle excised in a bilobed flap? A: Between the defect and the pivot point
A-bilobed-flap.pimp4: Q: What size defect and location is classic for a bilobed flap? A: Defects up to about 1.5 cm on the lower third of the nose, away from the alar rim
A-bilobed-flap.pimp5: Q: What causes pincushion (trapdoor) deformity, and how is it prevented? A: Contraction of the curved scar around the flap; prevented with wide undermining, slight undersizing and deep sutures
A-bilobed-flap.pimp6: Q: What type of blood supply does a bilobed flap have? A: Random pattern (subdermal plexus)
A-bilobed-flap.pimp7: Q: Why does the lower third of the nose need flaps rather than primary closure? A: Its skin is thick, sebaceous and adherent with little laxity
OR-bilobed-flap.step1: Measure the defect and design the first and second lobes around a pivot point that recruits lax adjacent skin while respecting nasal aesthetic subunits.
OR-bilobed-flap.step2: Incise the flap and elevate it in a plane thick enough to preserve the subdermal vascular plexus throughout both lobes and the common base.
OR-bilobed-flap.step3: Undermine the surrounding recipient and donor skin sufficiently to distribute tension and reduce pincushioning or alar distortion after rotation.
OR-bilobed-flap.step4: Rotate the first lobe into the primary defect and the second lobe into the first-lobe donor defect without excessive torsion or narrowing at the pivot.
OR-bilobed-flap.step5: Trim standing cutaneous deformities conservatively only after the flap is seated and perfusion is confirmed, preserving enough tissue to avoid overcorrection.
OR-bilobed-flap.step6: Place deep dermal sutures to offload skin tension, align the nasal contour, and prevent the flap from retracting away from the defect.
OR-bilobed-flap.step7: Close the epidermis with precise edge eversion while avoiding strangulation of the flap base or excessive tension across the alar margin.
OR-bilobed-flap.step8: Recheck flap color, capillary refill, inset tension, standing deformity, and final nasal contour before applying a noncompressive dressing.

## Cervicofacial Advancement / Rotation Flap  (slug: cervicofacial-flap, region: Facial Plastics / Trauma)

A-cervicofacial-flap.summary: The cervicofacial flap rotates and advances lax cheek and neck skin forward to close large medial or anterior cheek defects. Safety depends on choosing the plane relative to the SMAS and platysma, which decides both flap blood supply and facial nerve risk, and on anchoring the flap to fixed periosteum so its weight does not pull the lower lid into ectropion.
A-cervicofacial-flap.layer1: Design — cheek defect; infraorbital line toward the lateral canthus; temple (incision rises above the lateral canthus); preauricular crease; retroauricular or neck extension. For an anteriorly based flap, carry the incision from the defect laterally along the lower lid-cheek junction, rising above the lateral canthus toward the temple, then down the preauricular crease and into the neck as needed. Rising above the canthus lets the flap move up and in, which reduces downward pull on the lid.
A-cervicofacial-flap.layer2: Subcutaneous elevation in the cheek — skin; subcutaneous fat; SMAS (left down in subcutaneous technique); parotidomasseteric fascia. A subcutaneous plane is quick and safe for the nerve because the facial nerve branches lie deep to the SMAS. Elevation can go deep to the SMAS (deep plane) for better blood supply, especially in smokers or after radiation, but then the nerve branches must be seen and protected medially.
A-cervicofacial-flap.layer3: Preauricular and parotid region — parotid fascia; parotid gland; facial nerve branches emerging from the anterior border of the parotid. Over the parotid, the nerve is protected by the gland. Branches become more superficial as they leave the anterior parotid border and approach the zygomaticus muscles; this is where a deep plane is most dangerous.
A-cervicofacial-flap.layer4: Neck extension — platysma; subplatysmal plane; external jugular vein; great auricular nerve on the SCM; marginal mandibular branch. In the neck, elevate deep to the platysma (subplatysmal), keeping the EJ vein and great auricular nerve down on the SCM. The marginal mandibular branch lies deep to platysma near the mandible; stay on the deep surface of the platysma and do not dissect into the investing fascia near the facial vessels.
A-cervicofacial-flap.layer5: Rotation-advancement and anchoring — periosteum of the zygoma and lateral orbital rim; deep temporal fascia; deep sutures. Place deep suspension sutures from the flap undersurface to the periosteum of the zygoma or lateral orbital rim so the lower lid carries no weight. A lateral canthopexy can be added for lax lids.
A-cervicofacial-flap.layer6: Trimming and closure — standing cones (preauricular or neck); skin edges. Trim only after final position is set. Close in layers; place a drain if the neck dissection is large.
A-cervicofacial-flap.landmark1: Facial nerve branches at the anterior parotid border — At the anterior edge of the parotid, over masseter; the buccal branches lie near the parotid duct, which runs along a line from the tragus to the mid upper lip (Medial to this border the branches are less protected)
A-cervicofacial-flap.landmark2: Frontal branch over the zygomatic arch — Along a line from about 0.5 cm below the tragus to about 1.5 cm above the lateral brow (Pitanguy line), crossing the middle of the arch (Subcutaneous dissection over the arch is safe; deep dissection here is not)
A-cervicofacial-flap.landmark3: Great auricular nerve (McKinney point) — Crosses the middle of the SCM about 6.5 cm below the external auditory canal, running toward the lobule (Keep it down on the SCM during neck elevation)
A-cervicofacial-flap.landmark4: Platysma — Thin muscle sheet just below neck fat; its fibers run up from the chest over the mandible (Neck extension is raised deep to it to carry its blood supply)
A-cervicofacial-flap.landmark5: Zygomatic and lateral orbital periosteum — Palpate the malar eminence and lateral orbital rim; anchor sutures there through the flap undersurface (Fixed points that hold the flap up and protect the lower lid)
A-cervicofacial-flap.danger1: Lower eyelid — where: Upper edge of the flap along the lid-cheek junction; protect: Rise above the lateral canthus in design; suspend the flap to periosteum; canthopexy for lax lids; injury: Ectropion and scleral show
A-cervicofacial-flap.danger2: Facial nerve branches — where: Deep to the SMAS, superficial as they leave the anterior parotid border; marginal mandibular deep to platysma near the mandible; protect: Subcutaneous cheek plane, or identify branches when going deep; subplatysmal neck plane; injury: Facial weakness in the affected branch distribution
A-cervicofacial-flap.danger3: Distal flap — where: Leading edge farthest from the base, especially in a subcutaneous plane; protect: Avoid tension; consider a deep plane in smokers or irradiated skin; keep flap thick; injury: Distal necrosis
A-cervicofacial-flap.danger4: Great auricular nerve and external jugular vein — where: On the surface of the SCM deep to platysma; protect: Keep the dissection plane above them; injury: Ear numbness, bleeding
A-cervicofacial-flap.danger5: Oral commissure and lip — where: Inferomedial flap edge; protect: Deep anchoring and avoiding downward vectors; injury: Commissure distortion
A-cervicofacial-flap.variant1: Anteriorly based (inferomedial pedicle) and posteriorly based designs; the choice depends on the defect position and laxity
A-cervicofacial-flap.variant2: Facial nerve branching patterns vary, with frequent buccal-zygomatic connections
A-cervicofacial-flap.variant3: Lower lid laxity varies widely in older patients; test with a snap-back test
A-cervicofacial-flap.supply1: Arterial supply — Facial artery perforators and submental branches anteriorly; transverse facial and superficial temporal branches laterally; subdermal plexus (random)
A-cervicofacial-flap.supply2: Sensory — Infraorbital (V2), buccal (V3), auriculotemporal (V3) and great auricular (C2-C3) nerves
A-cervicofacial-flap.supply3: Motor in field — Facial nerve branches deep to SMAS and platysma
A-cervicofacial-flap.pimp1: Q: Why should the incision rise above the lateral canthus? A: So the flap moves superiorly and medially, reducing downward pull on the lower lid and ectropion
A-cervicofacial-flap.pimp2: Q: Where is the facial nerve relative to the SMAS in the cheek? A: Deep to the SMAS; branches become more superficial medial to the anterior parotid border
A-cervicofacial-flap.pimp3: Q: Which facial nerve branch is at risk in the neck extension, and where is it? A: Marginal mandibular branch, deep to platysma and superficial to the facial vessels near the mandible
A-cervicofacial-flap.pimp4: Q: Where is McKinney point? A: Where the great auricular nerve crosses the mid SCM, about 6.5 cm below the external auditory canal
A-cervicofacial-flap.pimp5: Q: What is the Pitanguy line? A: Line from about 0.5 cm below the tragus to about 1.5 cm above the lateral brow, approximating the frontal branch course
A-cervicofacial-flap.pimp6: Q: Where should deep suspension sutures anchor? A: To the periosteum of the zygoma or lateral orbital rim
A-cervicofacial-flap.pimp7: Q: What is the advantage of a deep-plane cervicofacial flap? A: Better blood supply (the flap carries the SMAS and platysma), at greater facial nerve risk
OR-cervicofacial-flap.step1: Design a broad rotation-advancement flap along relaxed skin/neck boundaries so the arc recruits enough laxity for a tension-free facial closure.
OR-cervicofacial-flap.step2: Elevate the flap in a safe subcutaneous/SMAS-related plane appropriate to the region, protecting facial-nerve branches.
OR-cervicofacial-flap.step3: Undermine widely rather than narrowing the pedicle to gain reach.
OR-cervicofacial-flap.step4: Rotate/advance the flap into the defect and identify where deep anchoring sutures can shift tension to fixed fascia/periosteum.
OR-cervicofacial-flap.step5: Trim only clearly redundant tissue after final position is established.
OR-cervicofacial-flap.step6: Secure deep sutures to reduce pull on the eyelid, lip, or nasal margin.
OR-cervicofacial-flap.step7: Close the donor and recipient sites in layers with drain placement only if dead space requires it.
OR-cervicofacial-flap.step8: Confirm distal flap perfusion and absence of ectropion/oral commissure distortion.

## Closed Nasal Fracture Reduction  (slug: closed-nasal-reduction, region: Facial Plastics / Trauma)

A-closed-nasal-reduction.summary: Closed reduction pushes displaced nasal bones and septum back to the midline before they set. Safety depends on keeping the elevator below the level of the cribriform plate, draining any septal hematoma, and recognizing when the injury is really a naso-orbito-ethmoid fracture that needs open repair.
A-closed-nasal-reduction.layer1: External and intranasal examination — nasal dorsum and sidewalls; nasal bones (thick and narrow above, thin and wide below); frontal processes of the maxilla; septum and mucosa; medial canthi. Palpate for step-offs, crepitus and mobility. Most fractures involve the thinner lower nasal bones. Look at both sides of the septum for a hematoma and measure the intercanthal distance to screen for NOE injury.
A-closed-nasal-reduction.layer2: Anesthesia and decongestion — topical pledgets under the nasal bones (anterior ethmoidal nerve); posterior pledgets toward the sphenopalatine region; infratrochlear and infraorbital nerve blocks; external nasal branch along the dorsum. Place pledgets high in the nasal vault under the nasal bones and posteriorly along the middle turbinate. Infiltrate the dorsum and the infraorbital and infratrochlear regions for local anesthesia.
A-closed-nasal-reduction.layer3: Measure the elevator depth — intercanthal line / nasofrontal angle; nasal bone segment to be lifted. Lay the Boies (or similar) elevator on the outside of the nose to mark how far to insert it; it should reach the depressed segment but not go above the level of the medial canthi, where the cribriform plate is.
A-closed-nasal-reduction.layer4: Elevation of depressed nasal bones — undersurface of the nasal bone; upper lateral cartilage (internal lining); nasal mucosa. Pass the elevator under the depressed bone and lift anterolaterally while the other hand molds from outside with counterpressure. Walsham forceps grip the bone with one padded blade inside and one outside.
A-closed-nasal-reduction.layer5: Reduction of the laterally displaced side — contralateral nasal bone (outfractured); dorsum and nasal pyramid midline. Push the outwardly displaced bone medially with thumb pressure once the depressed side is up, and recheck the dorsal line from above and in front.
A-closed-nasal-reduction.layer6: Septal reduction — quadrangular cartilage; vomer and maxillary crest groove; caudal septum. Asch forceps straddle the septum to relocate it into the maxillary crest. Avoid large resections in the acute setting; a severely fractured or dislocated septum may need open septoplasty.
A-closed-nasal-reduction.layer7: Re-inspect and splint — septal mucosa (hematoma, tears); nasal airway; external pyramid. Drain any septal hematoma with an incision and bilateral support (splints or quilting). Apply intranasal splints only if the septum needs support, and an external splint to protect the reduction.
A-closed-nasal-reduction.landmark1: Medial canthal (intercanthal) line — Draw a line between the medial canthi; the elevator tip should not go above it when inserted (Above this level the elevator nears the cribriform plate)
A-closed-nasal-reduction.landmark2: Septal hematoma — Bluish or red, soft, fluctuant swelling of one or both sides of the anterior septum that does not shrink with decongestant; press with a cotton-tipped applicator (Must be drained to prevent cartilage necrosis)
A-closed-nasal-reduction.landmark3: Depressed nasal bone segment — Palpate along the sidewall from the nasofacial junction toward the dorsum for a step; compare with the other side (Target for the elevator)
A-closed-nasal-reduction.landmark4: Intercanthal distance — Measure between the medial canthi; it is normally about half the interpupillary distance. Test medial canthal stability with the bowstring test (Widening or a loose canthus suggests NOE fracture, which is not treated by closed reduction)
A-closed-nasal-reduction.landmark5: Maxillary crest groove — Feel the base of the septum along the nasal floor in the midline; a dislocated caudal septum hangs off to one side of the anterior nasal spine (Septum is relocated into this groove with Asch forceps)
A-closed-nasal-reduction.danger1: Cribriform plate — where: Roof of the nasal cavity, roughly at the level of the medial canthi and above; protect: Measure elevator depth externally; do not push upward above the intercanthal line; injury: CSF leak, meningitis risk, anosmia
A-closed-nasal-reduction.danger2: Septal cartilage — where: Depends on the overlying perichondrium for nutrition; protect: Find and drain septal hematoma; prevent reaccumulation with splints or sutures; injury: Cartilage necrosis, abscess, saddle nose
A-closed-nasal-reduction.danger3: Anterior ethmoidal artery branches — where: Upper septum and lateral nasal wall below the nasal bones; protect: Gentle elevation; decongest well; injury: Epistaxis
A-closed-nasal-reduction.danger4: Medial canthal tendon and lacrimal system — where: Attach to the frontal process of the maxilla and lacrimal bone lateral to the nasal bones; protect: Recognize NOE injury before closed reduction; injury: Missed telecanthus, epiphora
A-closed-nasal-reduction.danger5: Nasal mucosa — where: Lines the undersurface of nasal bones and upper laterals; protect: Avoid tearing with forceps; use padded blades; injury: Bleeding, synechiae, infection
A-closed-nasal-reduction.variant1: Children have more cartilage and less bone; injuries can disturb growth centers of the septum
A-closed-nasal-reduction.variant2: Prior nasal fractures or surgery may leave old deviation that will not reduce
A-closed-nasal-reduction.variant3: Septum may be fractured horizontally or vertically and buckled, which closed reduction may not correct
A-closed-nasal-reduction.supply1: Arterial supply — Anterior and posterior ethmoidal arteries (ophthalmic), sphenopalatine (maxillary), superior labial and angular arteries (facial)
A-closed-nasal-reduction.supply2: Sensory — Anterior ethmoidal nerve (internal and external nasal branches), infratrochlear, infraorbital and nasopalatine nerves

OR-closed-nasal-reduction.step3: Elevate depressed nasal bones from inside the nose while applying counterpressure externally.
OR-closed-nasal-reduction.step4: Reduce laterally displaced fragments toward the midline in a controlled sequence and reassess dorsal width/projection.

OR-closed-nasal-reduction.step6: Palpate the bony pyramid and inspect intranasally for mucosal injury, persistent obstruction, or septal hematoma.
OR-closed-nasal-reduction.step7: Apply internal support only when needed and mold an external splint to maintain the reduction.
OR-closed-nasal-reduction.step8: Document final alignment and postoperative precautions against repeat trauma.

## Facial Nerve Repair / Cable Graft / Nerve Transfer  (slug: facial-nerve-reanimation, region: Facial Plastics / Trauma)

A-facial-nerve-reanimation.summary: Facial nerve repair restores motor input to the facial muscles by direct coaptation, an interposition graft, or transfer of a nearby motor nerve (masseteric or hypoglossal) when the proximal facial nerve is unusable. It only works while the facial muscles are still viable and the recipient branches are intact; safety and outcome depend on finding the right branches, a tension-free repair and limiting donor deficit.
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.layer1: Parotid exposure and trunk — preauricular-modified Blair incision; parotid fascia; cartilaginous tragal pointer; posterior belly of digastric; tympanomastoid suture; facial nerve main trunk. Raise a skin flap superficial to the parotid fascia. Find the trunk deep to the tragal pointer, at about the depth of the posterior belly of digastric, about 6-8 mm deep to the outer end of the tympanomastoid suture. Retrograde dissection from a distal branch is an option when the trunk is scarred or resected.
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.layer2: Distal branches — pes anserinus; temporofacial and cervicofacial divisions; frontal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular and cervical branches; parotid duct (with buccal branches). Branches leave the anterior parotid border and run on the masseter deep to the SMAS. Zygomatic and buccal branches have many connections; the frontal and marginal mandibular branches have few, so injuries to them recover less well.
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.layer3: Nerve to masseter (donor) — subzygomatic region; parotid and masseter fascia; masseter muscle fibers; masseteric nerve on the deep part of masseter. Find it below the zygomatic arch in front of the TMJ, roughly 3 cm anterior to the tragus and 1 cm below the arch, about 1.5 cm deep to the SMAS within the muscle, running obliquely forward and down. Confirm with a stimulator (jaw clenching).
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.layer4: Hypoglossal nerve (donor) — posterior belly of digastric; ECA and ICA; occipital artery and its SCM branch; ansa cervicalis (descendens hypoglossi); hypoglossal nerve. The nerve runs forward just below the posterior belly of digastric, crossing superficial to the ICA and ECA, looping under the SCM branch of the occipital artery. Partial (split) or side-to-end transfers with a jump graft limit tongue deficit.
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.layer5: Graft harvest — great auricular nerve on the SCM; sural nerve behind the lateral malleolus; medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve. The great auricular nerve is in the field and gives several centimeters; the sural nerve gives long grafts and branches for multiple distal coaptations.
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.layer6: Coaptation — epineurium; perineurium and fascicles; fibrin sealant (optional, around but not between nerve ends). Cut back to healthy fascicles under the microscope. Use the fewest fine sutures (often 9-0 or 10-0) needed to align the ends without tension or twist.
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.layer7: Bed and closure — parotid and cheek soft tissue; neck position. Lay grafts in a well-vascularized bed, avoid kinks, and check that turning the head does not pull on the repair.
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.landmark1: Facial nerve main trunk — Deep to the tragal pointer (about 1 cm deep and slightly anterior-inferior to its tip), about 6-8 mm deep to the outer end of the tympanomastoid suture, and at the depth of the posterior belly of digastric (Proximal stump or source of axons for grafts)
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.landmark2: Distal facial branches — At the anterior parotid border on the masseter; the buccal branch runs near the parotid duct along a line from the tragus to the mid upper lip; the zygomatic branch to zygomaticus major is often found near the midpoint of a line from the helical root to the oral commissure (Zuker point) (Recipient targets for smile and eye closure)
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.landmark3: Masseteric nerve — About 3 cm anterior to the tragus and 1 cm below the zygomatic arch, about 1.5 cm deep to the SMAS in the masseter (subzygomatic triangle); confirm with stimulation (Strong, reliable donor for smile reanimation)
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.landmark4: Hypoglossal nerve — Below the posterior belly of digastric, crossing superficial to the carotids and looping under the occipital artery's SCM branch, with the ansa (descendens) coming off its lower edge (Donor for hypoglossal-facial transfer)
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.landmark5: Great auricular nerve — On the lateral surface of the SCM, emerging at its posterior border near Erb point and running up toward the lobule, posterior and roughly parallel to the EJ vein (Graft source within the field)
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.landmark6: Sural nerve — Behind the lateral malleolus, between it and the Achilles tendon, next to the lesser saphenous vein (Long graft source)
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.danger1: Remaining functioning facial branches — where: Intermixed with recipient branches in the cheek; protect: Stimulate before dividing; choose recipient branches that serve the targeted muscles; injury: Loss of existing function; asymmetric or mass movement
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.danger2: Hypoglossal nerve function — where: Donor in the upper neck; protect: Partial (split) or side-to-end transfer with a jump graft; injury: Tongue atrophy, dysarthria, dysphagia
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.danger3: Parotid duct — where: Runs with the buccal branches across the masseter, then pierces buccinator opposite the upper second molar; protect: Identify it when working on buccal branches; injury: Sialocele or fistula
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.danger4: Eye — where: Orbicularis oculi deficit persists until reinnervation; protect: Eye care, lid loading (gold or platinum weight) and lower lid support as needed; injury: Exposure keratopathy
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.danger5: Graft donor sites — where: Great auricular, sural or antebrachial cutaneous nerve territories; protect: Plan length; warn of numbness; injury: Ear, lateral foot or forearm numbness; neuroma
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.variant1: Facial nerve branching patterns vary, with many buccal-zygomatic interconnections
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.variant2: The trunk may divide early, even within the temporal bone or just outside the stylomastoid foramen
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.variant3: The masseteric nerve branching and depth vary; stimulation is more reliable than any single measurement
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.variant4: Long-standing paralysis (often taken as beyond roughly 1-2 years) leaves facial muscles fibrosed and unable to accept reinnervation; free muscle transfer is then needed
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.supply1: Facial nerve course — Pons (facial motor nucleus) → IAC → labyrinthine segment → geniculate ganglion → tympanic and mastoid segments → stylomastoid foramen → parotid branches
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.supply2: Donor nerves — Masseteric nerve (V3 motor), hypoglossal nerve (XII), cross-facial grafts from contralateral buccal branches
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.supply3: Graft nerves — Great auricular (C2-C3), sural (lateral foot), medial antebrachial cutaneous
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.supply4: Regional arteries — Stylomastoid branch of the posterior auricular artery near the trunk; transverse facial artery in the field
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.pimp1: Q: Through which foramen does the facial nerve exit the skull? A: Stylomastoid foramen
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.pimp2: Q: Up to how long after transection can the distal facial nerve still be stimulated? A: About 72 hours, until Wallerian degeneration
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.pimp3: Q: Why are frontal and marginal mandibular branch injuries more troublesome than buccal injuries? A: They have few interconnections with other branches, so there is little redundancy
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.pimp4: Q: Where is the masseteric nerve found for transfer? A: About 3 cm anterior to the tragus and 1 cm below the zygomatic arch, about 1.5 cm deep to the SMAS
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.pimp5: Q: Which artery loops over the hypoglossal nerve in the upper neck? A: The SCM branch of the occipital artery
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.pimp6: Q: Where is the sural nerve found? A: Behind the lateral malleolus with the lesser saphenous vein, between the malleolus and the Achilles tendon
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.pimp7: Q: What is the main disadvantage of a classic hypoglossal-facial transfer? A: Ipsilateral tongue atrophy with possible speech and swallowing problems
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.pimp8: Q: What does EMG fibrillation mean in a paralyzed face? A: Denervated but viable muscle that can still be reinnervated
A-facial-nerve-reanimation.pimp9: Q: Which tool helps restore eye closure while waiting for reinnervation? A: Upper lid loading with a gold or platinum weight
OR-facial-nerve-reanimation.step1: Map the deficit, chronicity, available proximal facial nerve, distal branches, and donor nerves before incision; expose recipient facial-nerve branches in healthy tissue whenever possible.
OR-facial-nerve-reanimation.step2: Identify the proximal facial stump if usable and freshen it to healthy fascicles; if unavailable, expose the selected donor such as masseteric or hypoglossal nerve with enough length for a tension-free coaptation.
OR-facial-nerve-reanimation.step3: Harvest the interposition nerve graft when required and preserve orientation/length sufficient to bridge without stretch or redundant kinking.
OR-facial-nerve-reanimation.step4: Prepare proximal/donor and distal recipient nerve ends under magnification, minimizing crush and stripping only enough epineurium for accurate alignment.
OR-facial-nerve-reanimation.step5: Perform epineurial/perineurial microsuture coaptation with the fewest sutures needed for stable fascicular contact; use fibrin support selectively without interposing glue between nerve ends.
OR-facial-nerve-reanimation.step6: For nerve transfer, choose distal facial branches that reproduce the intended smile/eye-closure vector and avoid unnecessary denervation of functioning territories.
OR-facial-nerve-reanimation.step7: Lay grafts/coaptations in a vascularized, noncompressed bed and ensure neck/parotid position does not tension the repair.
OR-facial-nerve-reanimation.step8: Obtain hemostasis and close while documenting the exact donor-recipient construct and the expected months-long timeline for reinnervation/therapy.

## Paramedian Forehead Flap  (slug: forehead-flap, region: Facial Plastics / Trauma)

A-forehead-flap.summary: The paramedian forehead flap is an interpolated, staged flap that moves forehead skin on a narrow pedicle to cover large or deep nasal defects with matching skin. Safety depends on centering the pedicle over the supratrochlear artery, keeping the deep plane near the brow, and rebuilding lining and cartilage support as separate layers.
A-forehead-flap.layer1: Defect analysis and template — nasal aesthetic subunits (dorsum, two sidewalls, tip, two soft triangles, two alae, columella); missing lining; missing cartilage or bone support. Enlarge the defect to a whole subunit when it involves most of it. Make an exact foil or suture-packet template of the defect from the contralateral normal side and measure the distance from pivot to the distal defect.
A-forehead-flap.layer2: Flap design over the vessel — supratrochlear artery (Doppler); medial brow / glabellar frown line; pivot point near the medial brow or below it; frontal hairline. Center a pedicle about 1.2-1.5 cm wide over the Doppler signal or roughly 1.7-2.2 cm from the midline at the medial brow. The pivot can be low, near the medial canthus, because the supratrochlear artery joins the dorsal nasal and angular arteries. The flap runs vertically; use a scalp extension or oblique design if the forehead is short.
A-forehead-flap.layer3: Distal flap elevation — skin; subcutaneous fat; frontalis muscle; galea. Distally the artery lies in the subcutaneous tissue, so the distal flap can be raised with thin frontalis or in the subcutaneous plane for thin defects; many surgeons raise full thickness with frontalis and thin it at a later intermediate stage.
A-forehead-flap.layer4: Proximal elevation near the brow — subgaleal loose areolar plane; corrugator supercilii; procerus; periosteum (subperiosteal for the last 1-2 cm). Near the brow the artery is deep: after leaving the orbit it runs deep to orbicularis (and superficial to corrugator), then pierces orbicularis and frontalis to become subcutaneous; sources place this from about the level of the brow to roughly 2 cm above the rim. Go subgaleal, then subperiosteal near the rim; corrugator fibers deep to the vessel can be divided carefully to lengthen the pedicle.
A-forehead-flap.layer5: Lining and framework — septal mucoperichondrial hinge flaps; turnover flaps; skin graft lining; septal, conchal or rib cartilage grafts; alar batten (alar rim has no native cartilage). Repair lining first, then cartilage support, then cover. Place alar or rim grafts even where no native cartilage existed so the new ala resists collapse and contraction.
A-forehead-flap.layer6: Inset and pedicle — recipient subunit borders; pedicle bridge across the medial canthus. Inset without tension along subunit borders. Do not kink or compress the pedicle; cover its raw undersurface with a skin graft or dressing.
A-forehead-flap.layer7: Donor site — forehead skin; frontalis and galea; residual superior defect. Undermine in the subgaleal plane and close primarily as far as possible, leaving any upper defect to heal by secondary intention; scoring the galea gives a little extra advance.
A-forehead-flap.layer8: Later stages — pedicle division (about 3 weeks); intermediate thinning stage (in three-stage technique); medial brow restoration. Divide the pedicle after about 3 weeks; inset the proximal flap and return the base to the medial brow as a small inverted V, keeping the brows symmetric.
A-forehead-flap.landmark1: Supratrochlear artery — Doppler at the medial brow; otherwise roughly 1.7-2.2 cm from the midline, often under the corrugator crease and at the medial end of the brow (Axial supply of the flap)
A-forehead-flap.landmark2: Supraorbital notch or foramen — Palpable on the superior orbital rim in about the midpupillary line, lateral to the supratrochlear vessels (Keeps the pedicle medial to the supraorbital nerve)
A-forehead-flap.landmark3: Nasal subunit borders — Alar groove, sidewall-dorsum junction, tip-dorsum (supratip) line, soft triangle edges and columella base on the normal side (Scars on subunit borders are less visible)
A-forehead-flap.landmark4: Frontal hairline — Mark it before design; measure pivot-to-defect length to see if the template will reach hair (Hair-bearing skin on the nasal tip is a common problem; adjust design or use depilation)
A-forehead-flap.landmark5: Subgaleal plane — Below the galea, loose areolar tissue separates easily with blunt dissection (Fast, bloodless plane for proximal elevation and donor closure)
A-forehead-flap.danger1: Supratrochlear pedicle — where: Exits the orbit at the superomedial rim, runs deep to orbicularis and frontalis near the brow, then becomes subcutaneous in the upper forehead; protect: Doppler; adequate pedicle width; deep (subperiosteal) elevation near the brow; avoid twisting and compression; injury: Flap ischemia and necrosis
A-forehead-flap.danger2: Distal flap — where: Thin distal segment over the tip, columella or ala; protect: Thin conservatively; delay thinning to a separate stage in smokers or long flaps; keep the subdermal plexus; injury: Distal necrosis and contracture
A-forehead-flap.danger3: Lining and support — where: Full-thickness defects of the ala, sidewall or tip; protect: Rebuild lining and cartilage before the cover; place rim grafts; injury: Alar collapse, notching, airway obstruction, contracted shapeless nose
A-forehead-flap.danger4: Supraorbital and supratrochlear nerves — where: Exit at the superior orbital rim with their vessels; protect: Stay medial to the supraorbital notch; careful dissection near the rim; injury: Forehead and scalp numbness
A-forehead-flap.danger5: Medial brow — where: At the pedicle base; protect: Return the base to its exact position at division; injury: Brow malposition or asymmetry
A-forehead-flap.variant1: The supratrochlear artery position varies slightly side to side; confirm with Doppler
A-forehead-flap.variant2: Low frontal hairline or short forehead may need an oblique or hairline-extended design
A-forehead-flap.variant3: Prior forehead surgery, scars or radiation may compromise the pedicle
A-forehead-flap.variant4: Smoking and diabetes favor a three-stage technique with delayed thinning
A-forehead-flap.supply1: Arterial supply — Supratrochlear artery (ophthalmic artery, from the ICA), with anastomoses to the dorsal nasal and angular arteries at the medial canthus
A-forehead-flap.supply2: Sensory of donor — Supratrochlear and supraorbital nerves (V1)
A-forehead-flap.supply3: Motor in field — Frontal branch of CN VII innervates frontalis laterally (not usually in the field); corrugator and procerus near the pedicle base
A-forehead-flap.pimp1: Q: What artery supplies the paramedian forehead flap, and from which system? A: Supratrochlear artery, a branch of the ophthalmic artery (internal carotid system)
A-forehead-flap.pimp2: Q: Where is the supratrochlear artery usually found? A: Roughly 1.7-2.2 cm from the midline at the medial brow, near the corrugator crease
A-forehead-flap.pimp3: Q: What are the nine nasal aesthetic subunits? A: Dorsum, two sidewalls, tip, two soft triangles, two alae and columella
A-forehead-flap.pimp4: Q: What is the subunit principle? A: When a defect involves more than about half of a convex subunit, replace the whole subunit (Burget and Menick)
A-forehead-flap.pimp5: Q: When is the pedicle usually divided? A: About 3 weeks after transfer
A-forehead-flap.pimp6: Q: What are the five layers of the forehead and scalp? A: Skin, subcutaneous tissue, galea aponeurotica (with frontalis), loose areolar tissue and periosteum
A-forehead-flap.pimp7: Q: Why can the pivot point be placed below the brow? A: The supratrochlear artery anastomoses with the dorsal nasal and angular arteries near the medial canthus
A-forehead-flap.pimp8: Q: Why place a cartilage graft along the alar rim when the native ala has no cartilage? A: To resist collapse and upward contraction of the reconstructed ala
OR-forehead-flap.step1: Template the nasal/facial defect and design the paramedian forehead flap around the supratrochlear vascular axis with sufficient length and width.
OR-forehead-flap.step2: Incise the distal flap and elevate in the appropriate subcutaneous/subgaleal plane, transitioning deeper near the pedicle to protect the supratrochlear vessels.
OR-forehead-flap.step3: Complete the flap elevation without narrowing, twisting, or skeletonizing the pedicle.
OR-forehead-flap.step4: Thin the distal flap selectively while preserving the subdermal plexus needed for perfusion.
OR-forehead-flap.step5: Rotate the flap into the defect without tension and reconstruct lining/support separately when required.
OR-forehead-flap.step6: Inset the flap precisely along aesthetic subunits and secure the pedicle bridge without compression.
OR-forehead-flap.step7: Close the forehead donor site primarily where possible and manage the residual superior defect appropriately.
OR-forehead-flap.step8: Check flap color/capillary refill and document the staged pedicle-division/refinement plan.

## Frontal Sinus Fracture Repair / Cranialization Framework  (slug: frontal-sinus-trauma, region: Facial Plastics / Trauma)

A-frontal-sinus-trauma.summary: Frontal sinus fractures threaten three things: forehead contour (anterior table), the dura and frontal lobe (posterior table) and drainage through the frontal sinus outflow tract. Management is decided by outflow-tract injury and posterior-table displacement or CSF leak; safety depends on the supraorbital and supratrochlear neurovascular bundles at the rim, the dura and sagittal sinus behind the posterior table, and complete removal of mucosa (including from foramina of Breschet) when obliterating or cranializing.
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.layer1: Coronal (or laceration / endoscopic) approach — skin; subcutaneous tissue; galea aponeurotica; loose areolar layer (subgaleal plane); pericranium. Elevate in the subgaleal plane and keep the pericranium on bone for a later pericranial flap. Laterally, stay deep to the superficial layer of the deep temporal fascia (or on the deep temporal fascia) near the zygoma to protect the frontal branch of the facial nerve.
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.layer2: Pericranial flap and supraorbital rim — pericranium; supraorbital neurovascular bundle (notch or foramen); supratrochlear bundle (medial); frontal bone. Raise the pericranium with its blood supply from the supraorbital and supratrochlear vessels. Free the supraorbital bundle from its notch (or osteotomize a foramen) to drop the flap over the rims.
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.layer3: Anterior table — anterior table fragments; sinus cavity; intersinus septum. Remove and label fragments; the sinus is often asymmetrical with a midline or off-midline septum. Template the sinus from imaging when making osteotomies.
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.layer4: Sinus interior and mucosa — sinus mucosa and recesses (supraorbital, lateral); foramina of Breschet (venous pits); floor of sinus (orbital roof). For obliteration or cranialization, remove all mucosa and burr the inner cortex lightly to clear mucosa in the foramina of Breschet; residual mucosa causes mucoceles.
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.layer5: Frontal sinus outflow tract — frontal sinus ostium in the posteromedial floor; frontal recess; agger nasi cell, frontal cells, suprabullar cells; anterior ethmoid artery behind the recess. Assess patency with endoscopy or dye. If preserving, restore drainage; if obliterating, invert mucosa and plug the outflow with fascia, muscle or bone.
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.layer6: Posterior table and dura — posterior table; frontal lobe dura; superior sagittal sinus (midline); crista galli and foramen cecum (inferior midline). Cranialization removes the posterior table and strips mucosa. Bone removal near the midline and crista galli must protect the sagittal sinus and its anterior bridging veins and the olfactory region.
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.layer7: Reconstruction — dural repair; pericranial flap over the floor / outflow; anterior table plating. Lay the pericranial flap across the anterior cranial fossa floor and outflow tract to separate the nose from the intracranial space. Plate the anterior table with low-profile plates.
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.landmark1: Supraorbital neurovascular bundle — At the supraorbital notch or foramen on the rim, roughly in line with the medial limbus / mid-pupillary line (about 2.5 to 3 cm from the midline); the supratrochlear bundle is about 1 cm medial (Preserve forehead sensation and the pericranial flap's blood supply)
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.landmark2: Frontal sinus outline — From a CT-based template or image guidance; the six-foot Caldwell template is historical (Accurate osteotomies without entering the cranium)
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.landmark3: Frontal sinus ostium — Posteromedial floor of each sinus, leading into the frontal recess; probe from above while watching endoscopically from below (Decide preservation versus obliteration)
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.landmark4: Posterior table and dura — Deep wall of the sinus; look for comminution, displacement more than a table's width, CSF or brain (Indication for cranialization or dural repair)
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.landmark5: Frontal branch of facial nerve — Along Pitanguy's line (0.5 cm below the tragus to 1.5 cm above the lateral brow), within or just deep to the temporoparietal fascia over the zygomatic arch (Lateral flap dissection must stay deep to it)
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.danger1: Dura and frontal lobe — where: Directly behind the posterior table; protect: Careful bone removal with a thin footplate Kerrison or diamond burr; repair tears watertight; injury: CSF leak, meningitis, brain injury
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.danger2: Superior sagittal sinus — where: Midline, behind the posterior table, extending up from the crista galli region; protect: Avoid midline dural tears; leave a midline bony strip if needed; injury: Major venous bleeding, air embolism
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.danger3: Frontal branch of facial nerve — where: Temporoparietal fascia over the zygomatic arch and temple; protect: Dissect deep to the superficial temporal fat pad / on the deep temporal fascia over the arch; injury: Brow ptosis, forehead paralysis
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.danger4: Supraorbital and supratrochlear nerves — where: Notch/foramen at the rim and medial rim; protect: Release from the notch; osteotomize the foramen; injury: Forehead and scalp numbness
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.danger5: Frontal sinus outflow tract — where: Posteromedial floor to frontal recess; protect: Avoid damage when preserving; obliterate completely when it cannot be preserved; injury: Mucocele, mucopyocele, frontal sinusitis
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.danger6: Orbit / orbital roof and trochlea — where: Sinus floor and medial roof; the trochlea sits at the superomedial orbit; protect: Subperiosteal elevation at the superomedial rim; injury: Diplopia (superior oblique dysfunction), orbital hematoma
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.variant1: Frontal sinus aplasia or hypoplasia (a minority, often unilateral)
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.variant2: Very large sinuses with lateral supraorbital extension
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.variant3: Asymmetric or off-midline intersinus septum
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.variant4: Supraorbital foramen instead of a notch
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.variant5: Frontal cell or supraorbital ethmoid cell pneumatizing into the frontal region
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.supply1: Pericranial flap — Supraorbital and supratrochlear arteries (ophthalmic artery, ICA)
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.supply2: Sinus mucosa — Supraorbital, supratrochlear and anterior ethmoid arteries; venous drainage through foramina of Breschet to the dura
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.supply3: Sensory — Supraorbital and supratrochlear nerves (V1)
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.supply4: Scalp flap — Superficial temporal arteries laterally; supraorbital and supratrochlear centrally
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.pimp1: Q: What are the layers of the scalp? A: SCALP: Skin, Connective tissue, Aponeurosis (galea), Loose areolar tissue, Pericranium
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.pimp2: Q: What are the foramina of Breschet? A: Small venous foramina in the frontal sinus mucosa-bone interface that communicate with the dura; residual mucosa there causes mucoceles
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.pimp3: Q: When is the frontal sinus outflow tract considered injured? A: Fractures involving the sinus floor, the medial orbital rim/NOE region or the ostium on CT, or failure of dye/endoscopic patency
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.pimp4: Q: Where does the frontal sinus drain? A: Through the frontal ostium and recess into the middle meatus, medial or lateral to the uncinate depending on its superior attachment
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.pimp5: Q: Describe Pitanguy's line. A: From 0.5 cm below the tragus to 1.5 cm above the lateral brow, approximating the course of the frontal branch
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.pimp6: Q: At what age does the frontal sinus first appear on imaging, and when does it complete development? A: It begins to be seen around 5 to 7 years and reaches adult size in late adolescence
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.pimp7: Q: What is cranialization? A: Removal of the posterior table and sinus mucosa, plugging of the outflow tract, so the frontal lobe occupies the former sinus space
A-frontal-sinus-trauma.pimp8: Q: Which artery supplies the pericranial flap? A: Supraorbital and supratrochlear arteries
OR-frontal-sinus-trauma.step1: Expose the frontal sinus through the planned coronal/existing-laceration approach and define anterior table, posterior table, supraorbital rims, and nasofrontal outflow anatomy.
OR-frontal-sinus-trauma.step2: Remove and preserve usable anterior-table fragments in an oriented fashion, then inspect the sinus for mucosal injury, posterior-table comminution, CSF leak, and outflow-tract disruption.
OR-frontal-sinus-trauma.step3: If sinus preservation is appropriate, restore a patent outflow pathway and repair/reduce the anterior table without trapping mucosa behind displaced fragments.
OR-frontal-sinus-trauma.step4: For obliteration, remove sinus mucosa meticulously from all walls/recesses and permanently separate the sinus from the nasal outflow tract before filling/obliterating the cavity.
OR-frontal-sinus-trauma.step5: For cranialization, remove the posterior table, strip all sinus mucosa, and allow the frontal lobes/dura to occupy the former sinus after secure dural repair.
OR-frontal-sinus-trauma.step6: Repair CSF/dural defects with a watertight vascularized or grafted barrier and protect the frontal recess from intracranial communication.
OR-frontal-sinus-trauma.step7: Reconstruct the anterior table/supraorbital contour with stable fixation when needed for protection and cosmesis.
OR-frontal-sinus-trauma.step8: Confirm hemostasis and closure of dead space, then document long-term surveillance for mucocele, contour deformity, CSF leak, and intracranial infection.

## Mandible ORIF  (slug: mandible-orif, region: Facial Plastics / Trauma)

A-mandible-orif.summary: Mandible ORIF restores occlusion first and then fixes the fracture along lines of tension so muscle pull cannot distract it. Safety depends on the inferior alveolar canal and tooth roots inside the bone, the mental nerve exiting below the premolars for intraoral approaches, and the marginal mandibular and other facial nerve branches for external approaches.
A-mandible-orif.layer1: Occlusion and MMF — dentition; maxillary and mandibular arches; first molars (Angle relationship); wear facets. Set preinjury occlusion with arch bars or MMF screws placed in interradicular bone near the mucogingival junction, clear of root apices.
A-mandible-orif.layer2: Intraoral vestibular approach (symphysis, body, angle) — gingivobuccal mucosa (leave a cuff for closure); mentalis muscle (anterior); mental nerve branches (premolar region); buccinator and masseter (posterior); periosteum. Incise in the vestibule leaving cuff on the gingival side; reapproximate mentalis anteriorly to prevent chin ptosis; identify the mental nerve before extending past the canine.
A-mandible-orif.layer3: External submandibular approach (angle, ramus, body) — skin about 2 cm below the mandible; platysma; superficial layer of deep cervical fascia; facial vein and artery; marginal mandibular nerve; pterygomasseteric sling; periosteum. Incise the fascia low and lift it with the facial vein (Hayes Martin maneuver) to protect the marginal nerve; divide the sling at the inferior border to reach bone.
A-mandible-orif.layer4: Retromandibular / preauricular approach (condyle, subcondyle) — parotid gland; facial nerve branches (cervicofacial division); retromandibular vein; superficial temporal vessels and auriculotemporal nerve (preauricular); frontal branch (preauricular); TMJ capsule. Dissect through or around the parotid bluntly in line with nerve branches; nerve monitoring helps.
A-mandible-orif.layer5: Reduction — fracture segments; condyles in the glenoid fossae; lingual cortex. Reduce with condyles seated and occlusion held; check the lingual cortex for splay at symphysis fractures.
A-mandible-orif.layer6: Fixation — tension band (superior/alveolar border); compression zone (inferior border); tooth roots; inferior alveolar canal; mental foramen. Miniplates follow Champy lines: below root apices and above the canal in the body, along the external oblique ridge at the angle, two plates at the symphysis/parasymphysis. Load-bearing reconstruction plates at the inferior border for comminution or atrophy.
A-mandible-orif.layer7: Check and closure — occlusion; hardware; mucosa and mentalis; external layers. Release MMF and confirm reproducible occlusion; close mentalis and mucosa in layers.
A-mandible-orif.landmark1: Mental foramen — Below the second premolar or between the premolars, roughly midway between alveolar crest and inferior border in dentate adults (Mental nerve exits here; screws and incisions must avoid it)
A-mandible-orif.landmark2: Inferior alveolar canal — Runs from the mandibular foramen (behind the lingula on the medial ramus) forward below the molar roots to the mental foramen; on CT/panorex it is the radiolucent line with cortical borders; may loop anteriorly before exiting (Body miniplates sit in the zone between root apices and the canal; bicortical screws belong at the inferior border below the canal)
A-mandible-orif.landmark3: Marginal mandibular nerve — Deep to platysma and within/under the superficial layer of deep cervical fascia, crossing superficial to the facial artery and vein near the antegonial notch; may dip up to about 1-2 cm below the inferior border posterior to the facial artery (Protect during submandibular and retromandibular approaches)
A-mandible-orif.landmark4: External oblique ridge — Palpable bony ridge on the lateral mandible running up from the molar region to the anterior ramus (Site for the angle tension-band plate)
A-mandible-orif.landmark5: Tooth root apices — Estimate root length from CT/panorex; canine roots are longest; place subapical plates below them (Avoid screw injury to roots)
A-mandible-orif.danger1: Inferior alveolar nerve — where: Within the mandibular canal from the ramus to the mental foramen; protect: Monocortical screws between apices and canal, bicortical only at the inferior border; drill guides and length checks; injury: Lower lip and chin numbness
A-mandible-orif.danger2: Mental nerve — where: Exits the mental foramen below the premolars; branches fan into the lower lip just under the mucosa; protect: Identify before extending the incision; avoid stretching with retractors; injury: Lower lip and chin numbness
A-mandible-orif.danger3: Tooth roots — where: Alveolar bone above the canal; protect: Monocortical screws below apices; MMF screws between roots; injury: Devitalized teeth, root fracture
A-mandible-orif.danger4: Marginal mandibular nerve — where: Below and along the inferior border, superficial to the facial vessels; protect: Incise 2 cm below the mandible, subfascial plane, Hayes Martin maneuver; injury: Lower lip depressor weakness
A-mandible-orif.danger5: Facial nerve trunk and branches — where: Within the parotid during retromandibular and preauricular approaches; protect: Blunt dissection parallel to branches; nerve monitor; injury: Facial weakness
A-mandible-orif.danger6: Facial artery and retromandibular vein — where: Antegonial notch and behind the ramus; protect: Identify and ligate as needed; injury: Hemorrhage
A-mandible-orif.variant1: Anterior loop of the inferior alveolar nerve before the mental foramen
A-mandible-orif.variant2: Accessory mental foramina
A-mandible-orif.variant3: Bifid mandibular canal
A-mandible-orif.variant4: Edentulous atrophic mandible with the canal and mental foramen near the alveolar crest
A-mandible-orif.supply1: Arterial — Inferior alveolar artery (maxillary artery) within the canal plus periosteal supply from muscle attachments
A-mandible-orif.supply2: Sensory — Inferior alveolar nerve (V3) to lower teeth; mental nerve to lower lip and chin; lingual nerve medial to the third molar
A-mandible-orif.supply3: Motor to elevators — V3: masseter, temporalis, medial pterygoid; lateral pterygoid protrudes and pulls the condyle anteromedially
A-mandible-orif.supply4: Facial nerve branches — Marginal mandibular and cervical branches near the inferior border; cervicofacial division in the parotid
A-mandible-orif.pimp1: Q: What are Champy lines? A: Ideal lines for monocortical miniplates along the tension zone: subapical in the body, along the external oblique ridge at the angle, two plates in the symphysis
A-mandible-orif.pimp2: Q: Which mandibular border is in tension and which in compression? A: Superior (alveolar) border tension, inferior border compression
A-mandible-orif.pimp3: Q: Which muscle displaces a condylar fracture anteromedially? A: Lateral pterygoid
A-mandible-orif.pimp4: Q: What is Angle class I occlusion? A: Mesiobuccal cusp of the maxillary first molar sits in the buccal groove of the mandibular first molar
A-mandible-orif.pimp5: Q: Where is the mental foramen? A: Below the second premolar or between the premolars
A-mandible-orif.pimp6: Q: What does the Hayes Martin maneuver do? A: Ligates and elevates the facial vein with the overlying fascia to protect the marginal mandibular nerve
A-mandible-orif.pimp7: Q: What is a favorable fracture? A: One whose orientation resists displacement by the masseter, temporalis and medial pterygoid pull
A-mandible-orif.pimp8: Q: What nerve runs medial to the third molar? A: Lingual nerve
OR-mandible-orif.step1: Establish preinjury occlusion using dentition/arch relationships and secure maxillomandibular fixation or an equivalent occlusal reference before plating.
OR-mandible-orif.step2: Expose the fracture through intraoral or external access while protecting mental/inferior alveolar/facial nerve structures appropriate to the site.
OR-mandible-orif.step3: Debride only clearly nonviable tissue and reduce the fracture anatomically with the condyles seated and occlusion maintained.
OR-mandible-orif.step4: Place fixation along tension/compression zones appropriate to fracture location, keeping screws away from tooth roots and the inferior alveolar canal.
OR-mandible-orif.step5: For comminution/segmental instability, use load-bearing fixation with adequate screw purchase on both sides of the injury.
OR-mandible-orif.step6: Release or test MMF and verify stable reproducible occlusion through opening/closing.
OR-mandible-orif.step7: Irrigate and inspect for hardware conflict, nerve entrapment, or unstable fragments.
OR-mandible-orif.step8: Close intraoral/external incisions and set postoperative diet, oral hygiene, and fixation plan.

## Melolabial / Nasolabial Flap  (slug: melolabial-flap, region: Facial Plastics / Trauma)

A-melolabial-flap.summary: The melolabial flap uses loose medial cheek skin along the melolabial fold to cover alar, lateral nasal or lip defects, either as a single-stage transposition or as a staged interpolated flap. It is a random-pattern flap fed by perforators from the facial and angular arteries; safety depends on raising it in the subcutaneous plane superficial to the mimetic muscles, where the facial nerve branches do not run.
A-melolabial-flap.layer1: Design along the fold — melolabial fold; alar-facial groove; medial cheek skin; beard-bearing area in men. Place the medial incision in the fold so the donor scar hides there. A superiorly based flap is common for nasal defects. Avoid crossing the alar-facial groove with a single-stage flap when possible, because it blunts it.
A-melolabial-flap.layer2: Incision and subcutaneous elevation — skin; subcutaneous fat with subdermal plexus; superficial surface of the levator labii superioris and zygomaticus muscles (left down). Elevate in the subcutaneous fat. The mimetic muscles and their facial nerve branches lie deeper; staying superficial to the muscles avoids them.
A-melolabial-flap.layer3: Pedicle (interpolated flap) — subcutaneous pedicle at the superior base; perforators from the angular artery and facial artery. For an interpolated flap, keep a subcutaneous pedicle at the upper end; perforators near the alar base supply it. Do not skeletonize the pedicle.
A-melolabial-flap.layer4: Thinning — distal flap fat. Thin the distal flap to match alar thickness while keeping the subdermal plexus; thin the proximal flap less.
A-melolabial-flap.layer5: Lining and support — vestibular lining; alar rim cartilage graft; lateral crus remnants. For full-thickness alar defects, rebuild lining (a folded flap, septal flap or graft) and support (cartilage graft) before covering.
A-melolabial-flap.layer6: Inset — alar subunit borders; nostril margin. Inset with deep sutures to recreate the alar shape and groove. Whole-subunit replacement often looks better than a patch.
A-melolabial-flap.layer7: Donor closure — cheek skin; melolabial fold; upper lip and commissure. Undermine the cheek in the subcutaneous plane and close along the fold; tension should run along the fold, not pull on the lip or ala.
A-melolabial-flap.layer8: Pedicle division — pedicle; alar-facial groove. Divide an interpolated flap after about 3 weeks and reconstruct a sharp alar-facial groove.
A-melolabial-flap.landmark1: Melolabial fold — Ask the patient to smile before marking, or mark the natural crease from the alar base to the commissure (Hides the donor scar)
A-melolabial-flap.landmark2: Facial artery and angular artery — The facial artery runs from the mandibular notch at the anterior masseter toward about 1-1.5 cm lateral to the oral commissure, then upward as the angular artery near the alar base; Doppler can confirm (Perforators supply the flap base; the artery bleeds if the dissection goes too deep)
A-melolabial-flap.landmark3: Subcutaneous plane — Leave a thin layer of fat on the muscle surface; you should see muscle fibers below but not cut into them (Safe from facial nerve branches)
A-melolabial-flap.landmark4: Alar-facial groove — The crease where the ala meets the cheek and upper lip (Must be preserved or recreated for a natural result)
A-melolabial-flap.landmark5: Infraorbital foramen — About 6-10 mm below the infraorbital rim (individual range wider) in the midpupillary line, deep to levator labii superioris (Deep to the plane; relevant if dissection near the upper base is too deep)
A-melolabial-flap.danger1: Facial nerve buccal and zygomatic branches — where: Deep to the SMAS and mimetic muscles in the medial cheek; they enter most muscles on their deep surfaces; protect: Stay in the subcutaneous plane; injury: Upper lip weakness or asymmetry
A-melolabial-flap.danger2: Facial and angular arteries — where: Deep to the zygomaticus muscles and lateral to the commissure, rising along the nasofacial groove; protect: Avoid deep dissection; preserve perforators at the base; injury: Bleeding; loss of perforators and flap ischemia
A-melolabial-flap.danger3: Alar rim and alar base — where: Recipient margin and the donor closure near the alar base; protect: Use cartilage support; close the donor so tension runs along the fold; injury: Alar retraction, distortion, nasal valve collapse
A-melolabial-flap.danger4: Alar-facial groove — where: Junction of nose and cheek crossed by single-stage flaps; protect: Use an interpolated design or reconstruct the groove at division; injury: Blunted groove
A-melolabial-flap.danger5: Upper lip and commissure — where: Inferior end of the donor site; protect: Avoid excess skin removal at the lower end; plan the inferior standing cone; injury: Lip elevation or commissure distortion
A-melolabial-flap.variant1: Beard-bearing cheek skin in men can transfer hair to the nose
A-melolabial-flap.variant2: Facial artery course is tortuous and its distal end may stop before becoming an angular artery
A-melolabial-flap.variant3: Prominent or deep melolabial folds give more tissue in older patients
A-melolabial-flap.supply1: Arterial supply — Random pattern with perforators from the facial and angular arteries (ECA); infraorbital artery contributes superiorly
A-melolabial-flap.supply2: Venous — Facial vein, which runs posterior (lateral) to the artery and in a straighter line
A-melolabial-flap.supply3: Sensory — Infraorbital nerve (V2)
A-melolabial-flap.supply4: Motor in field — Buccal and zygomatic branches of CN VII to levator labii superioris, zygomaticus major and minor, and orbicularis oris (deep to the flap plane)
A-melolabial-flap.pimp1: Q: What vessels supply a superiorly based melolabial flap? A: Perforators from the angular and facial arteries, with a random-pattern subdermal supply
A-melolabial-flap.pimp2: Q: In which plane is the flap raised? A: Subcutaneous, superficial to the mimetic muscles
A-melolabial-flap.pimp3: Q: Which three facial muscles receive their nerve supply on their superficial surface? A: Buccinator, levator anguli oris and mentalis
A-melolabial-flap.pimp4: Q: How does the facial vein relate to the facial artery in the face? A: The vein lies posterior (lateral) to the artery and takes a straighter course
A-melolabial-flap.pimp5: Q: When is an interpolated melolabial flap divided? A: After about 3 weeks
A-melolabial-flap.pimp6: Q: What is the main drawback of a single-stage melolabial transposition to the ala? A: It blunts the alar-facial groove
A-melolabial-flap.pimp7: Q: Where do the zygomaticus major and other elevators insert near the corner of the mouth? A: The modiolus
OR-melolabial-flap.step1: Design the flap within the melolabial crease with dimensions matched to the defect and a base that preserves the intended subdermal/facial-artery blood supply.
OR-melolabial-flap.step2: Incise and elevate the flap at the planned thickness, protecting the vascular base and avoiding unnecessary facial-nerve injury.
OR-melolabial-flap.step3: Create the transfer path so the flap reaches the defect without pedicle compression or a conspicuous dog-ear.
OR-melolabial-flap.step4: Thin the distal flap only as needed for contour while maintaining perfusion.
OR-melolabial-flap.step5: Inset the flap into the recipient defect with deep sutures that restore three-dimensional support.
OR-melolabial-flap.step6: Close the donor site along the melolabial fold with tension distributed away from the alar margin/lip.
OR-melolabial-flap.step7: Check oral/nasal lining if the flap traverses a full-thickness defect.
OR-melolabial-flap.step8: Confirm flap perfusion and document staged division if an interpolated design is used.
